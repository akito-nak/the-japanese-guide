# How the JLPT Works

How good is your Japanese? It is a surprisingly hard question to answer. You can order ramen with confidence, follow half of an anime episode and read a train timetable, but an employer, a university or an immigration officer wants something more definite than "pretty good, I think". For millions of learners, the answer is a test result with a letter and a number: N5, N4, N3, N2 or N1.

The **Japanese-Language Proficiency Test** (日本語能力試験, *Nihongo Nōryoku Shiken*), known everywhere as the **JLPT**, is the most widely taken Japanese certification in the world. It is run by the Japan Foundation outside Japan and by Japan Educational Exchanges and Services (JEES) inside Japan, and in 2025 it drew about 1.9 million applicants, the largest number in its history. It won't make you fluent, but it gives you a roadmap, a deadline and a certificate that Japan recognizes, and that combination is hard to beat.

All test details in this section were checked against the official site, [jlpt.jp](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/), in September 2026. Fees, dates and rules change, so confirm them there before you register.

## The Five Levels

The JLPT has five levels, from N5 (the easiest) to N1 (the hardest). The official descriptions are short and deliberately general:

| Level | Official summary of ability |
|---|---|
| N5 | Can understand some basic Japanese |
| N4 | Can understand basic Japanese |
| N3 | Can understand Japanese used in everyday situations to a certain degree |
| N2 | Can understand Japanese used in everyday situations, and in a variety of circumstances to a certain degree |
| N1 | Can understand Japanese used in a variety of circumstances |

Learners understandably want harder numbers: how many words, how many kanji, how many hours. The JLPT does not provide them. Since the test was revised in 2010, the organizers have chosen not to publish official lists of vocabulary, kanji or grammar, and point learners to the level summaries and sample questions instead. The figures below are therefore **community estimates**, mostly traced back to the lists published for the pre-2010 test, with N3 (a level added in 2010) placed between its neighbors.

| Level | Vocabulary (est.) | Kanji (est.) |
|---|---|---|
| N5 | ~800 words | ~100 |
| N4 | ~1,500 words | ~300 |
| N3 | ~3,750 words | ~650 |
| N2 | ~6,000 words | ~1,000 |
| N1 | ~10,000 words | ~2,000 |

Study hours are harder still. [Section 1.4](../01-how-to-learn-japanese/04-the-jlpt-map.md) gives the two sourced sets of figures that exist, the classroom hours the pre-2010 test assumed and one Tokyo school's records of how long its full-time students took, and explains why both are orders of magnitude rather than promises. Readers of Chinese often move much faster through the kanji-heavy levels, and immersion (living in Japan, daily Japanese media) changes the picture entirely.

> **Note:** Different textbooks, apps and websites publish different kanji and vocabulary lists for each level, and they disagree at the edges. That is normal: no list is official, so use one consistently rather than worrying about which one is "right".

## What the Test Looks Like

Every level tests three areas: **language knowledge** (言語知識, *gengo chishiki*: vocabulary, kanji and grammar), reading (読解, *dokkai*) and listening (聴解, *chōkai*). The test is on paper, and the answers are multiple choice: four options for most questions, three for some listening questions. There is no speaking and no writing at any level, which is both the JLPT's main limitation and the reason it can be given to a million people on a single day.

The test sections and their times, as of 2026, are:

| Level | Test sections and times |
|---|---|
| N1 | Language Knowledge (Vocabulary/Grammar) and Reading, 110 min · Listening, 55 min |
| N2 | Language Knowledge (Vocabulary/Grammar) and Reading, 105 min · Listening, 50 min |
| N3 | Language Knowledge (Vocabulary), 30 min · Language Knowledge (Grammar) and Reading, 70 min · Listening, 40 min |
| N4 | Language Knowledge (Vocabulary), 25 min · Language Knowledge (Grammar) and Reading, 55 min · Listening, 35 min |
| N5 | Language Knowledge (Vocabulary), 20 min · Language Knowledge (Grammar) and Reading, 40 min · Listening, 30 min |

Listening times can vary slightly with the length of the recordings.

## How Scoring Works

Your result is a **scaled score**: raw answers are converted onto a fixed scale so that a score means the same thing from one test to the next, whatever the difficulty of that particular paper. Every level has a total of 180 points.

To pass, you must clear two bars. The first is the overall pass mark. The second is a **sectional pass mark** for each scoring section: fall below it in any one section and you fail, however high your total. A brilliant reading score cannot rescue a weak listening score.

| Level | Scoring sections (points) | Overall pass mark | Sectional pass marks |
|---|---|---|---|
| N1 | Language Knowledge 0–60 · Reading 0–60 · Listening 0–60 | 100 | 19 each |
| N2 | Language Knowledge 0–60 · Reading 0–60 · Listening 0–60 | 90 | 19 each |
| N3 | Language Knowledge 0–60 · Reading 0–60 · Listening 0–60 | 95 | 19 each |
| N4 | Language Knowledge and Reading 0–120 · Listening 0–60 | 90 | 38 and 19 |
| N5 | Language Knowledge and Reading 0–120 · Listening 0–60 | 80 | 38 and 19 |

Notice that N3 needs a higher total than N2 (95 against 90) even though N2 is the harder level. At N4 and N5, vocabulary, grammar and reading share one combined score out of 120.

## When, Where and How Much

- **Dates.** The JLPT is held twice a year, on a Sunday in early July and another in early December. In 2026 the dates are July 5 and December 6. Some cities outside Japan hold only one of the two.
- **Places.** For the December 2025 test, it was held in all 47 prefectures of Japan and in 247 cities across 81 countries and regions.
- **Registration.** In Japan, you apply online through the MyJLPT system roughly three months before the test (for December 2025, applications ran from August 18 to September 8). Outside Japan, a local host institution handles registration, with its own dates and procedure.
- **Fee.** In Japan the fee is ¥7,500 including tax (the figure for the 2025 tests). Fees elsewhere are set locally and vary widely.
- **Results.** In Japan, score reports for the July test are sent in early September and for the December test in early February; outside Japan, in early October and early March.

## What the JLPT Proves, and What It Doesn't

A JLPT certificate has concrete uses in Japan. The official site lists, among others:

- **Immigration points.** Under Japan's points-based system for highly skilled professionals, N1 earns 15 points and N2 earns 10.
- **Work visas.** N4 or above satisfies the Japanese-language requirement for the Specified Skilled Worker residence status.
- **Professional licensing.** Doctors trained abroad need N1 to sit Japan's national medical examination, and the nurse and caregiver programs under Japan's Economic Partnership Agreements set JLPT levels for candidates.
- **Education and hiring.** Universities use N2 and above as a benchmark for admission, and many employers use JLPT levels in hiring, promotion and pay.

What it does not measure is just as important: speaking, writing and the ability to hold a natural conversation. Plenty of N2 and N1 holders freeze in casual chat, because they have never had to produce Japanese under pressure. Plan your study to include speaking and writing alongside test preparation.

## Study Principles That Work at Every Level

The first four principles rest on research into how adults learn languages, explained in [section 1.2](../01-how-to-learn-japanese/02-study-and-immersion.md); the last two rest on test-taking experience. The daily routine by level at the end of section 1.2 is the backbone of any JLPT plan; these principles adjust it for the exam.

**Learn grammar early, and explicitly.** At N5 to N3, grammar gives you the frame that vocabulary hangs on, and grammar patterns are finite while vocabulary never ends. A large review of classroom studies by John Norris and Lourdes Ortega (2000) found that explicit instruction, being taught a rule and practicing it, produces substantial and lasting gains, so a textbook or grammar guide is time well spent.

**Use spaced repetition for vocabulary and kanji.** A spaced repetition system (SRS, [section 3.4](../03-writing-system/04-studying-kanji.md)) shows you each word just before you would forget it, and makes every review a test. Spacing (Cepeda et al., 2006) and retrieval practice (Karpicke and Roediger, 2008) are two of the best-supported findings in memory research. Anki and similar tools make the vocabulary load of N3 and above manageable, and N2 needs you to recognize thousands of words quickly, not slowly.

**Read every day, mostly at a comfortable level.** Comfortable reading needs about 98% of the words known (Hu and Nation, 2000), and reading a lot of text near that threshold builds the speed the reading section demands. Pair it with slower, intensive reading of one harder text. NHK News Web Easy, covered in [section 12.3](../12-japanese-for-media/03-news.md), suits learners from about N4.

**Practice listening on purpose.** The listening section trips up learners who study only from books, and input you understand is the engine of acquisition (Krashen, 1982). Listen to Japanese at your target level every day: podcasts and slow news for beginners, then NHK radio, drama and anime from N3 up. Shadowing a short clip a few times a week sharpens word recognition, especially for lower-level listeners (Hamada, 2016).

**Practice with real test materials.** This one is test-taking experience: the format barely changes, so official practice questions are the best preparation once you know the content. The JLPT site has free sample questions, and the Official Practice Workbooks contain full-length tests. Working through questions is also retrieval practice, so check every wrong answer and add what you missed to your flashcards.

**Sit full timed mock exams.** Also experience rather than research: time pressure is real, especially in the reading section of N2 and N1. Take at least three or four full practice tests under exam conditions, spread over the last two months rather than crammed into the last week.

## Choosing Your Level

Take a practice test before you commit. These rough pointers help you choose which one:

| If you… | Try |
|---|---|
| Are starting from zero | N5 |
| Have finished Genki I | N5, or N4 with extra study |
| Have finished Genki II | N4, or N3 with extra study |
| Have studied steadily for a year or more | A practice N3 test, then decide |
| Read manga comfortably | N3–N2 |
| Watch anime without subtitles | N2–N1 |
| Work or study in Japanese | N2–N1 |

Aim at the level that stretches you. Sitting N5 when you are clearly at N3 costs the same fee and the same Sunday, and the certificate only shows the level you passed.

## The Real Goal

The JLPT is a tool, not a destination. Scraping through N1 while struggling to hold a conversation is less useful than chatting naturally with N3 grammar and a rich vocabulary. Use the test to find your weak areas, to set milestones with a date attached and to earn a certificate that matters to employers and immigration. Don't use it as the measure of your "real" Japanese, or as a substitute for talking to people. The learners who do best study for the JLPT *and* live in real Japanese media and conversation.

## Summary

- The JLPT has five levels, N5 (easiest) to N1 (hardest), and tests language knowledge, reading and listening by multiple choice, with no speaking or writing.
- Every level is scored out of 180, and you must reach both the overall pass mark and a minimum score in each section to pass.
- Official vocabulary and kanji lists have not been published since 2010, so word and kanji counts per level are community estimates.
- As of 2026 the test is held twice a year, in early July and early December; the fee in Japan was ¥7,500 for the 2025 tests, and jlpt.jp has current details.
- N1 and N2 earn points in Japan's immigration system for highly skilled professionals, and N4 meets the language requirement for Specified Skilled Worker status.
- A JLPT certificate proves reading and listening ability, not conversation, so practice speaking and writing alongside test preparation.
