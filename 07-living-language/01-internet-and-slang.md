# Internet Language and Slang — wwww and Other Mysteries

You post a joke in a Japanese group chat. The replies come back: 「www」, then 「草」, then 「草生えた」, "grass grew". Nobody is talking about gardening. They are laughing at your joke, and you have just met one of the most inventive corners of the Japanese language.

Japanese internet language is rich, playful and fast-moving. Much of it was born on 2ちゃんねる (*ni-channeru*, 2channel), the huge anonymous bulletin board founded in 1999 and renamed 5ちゃんねる (5channel) in 2017. From there it spread through blogs, Twitter (now X), LINE and video sites until much of it became everyday speech. Knowing it helps with social media, comment sections, streams, anime and manga, and with being online in Japanese.

> **Note:** Slang ages fast. The words in this section are described as they stand as of 2026, and some are flagged as dated. For the newest youth slang and buzzwords, see [section 7.2](02-contemporary-japanese.md).

## The Basics: w and 草

The most famous piece of Japanese internet slang is a single letter: w stands for 笑 (*warai*, laughter): people once typed (笑) after a joke, then shortened it to the first letter of its romanized reading. Stack it for intensity:

| Expression | Meaning |
|---|---|
| w | lol |
| ww | haha |
| www | lmao |
| wwwwwwww | I can't breathe |

A long row of w's looks like a patch of grass, so laughter also became 草 (*kusa*, grass). 草生えた (*kusa haeta*, grass grew) means "that's hilarious", and as of 2026 a bare 草 is one of the most common reactions online.

## Everyday Chat Slang

These words are widely understood across age groups, though most belong in casual chat rather than conversation with your boss.

| Slang | Reading | Origin | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| リア充 | riajū | リアル (real) + 充実 (fulfilled) | someone whose offline life is full (friends, romance), often said with envy |
| 陰キャ / 陽キャ | inkya / yōkya | 陰気 / 陽気 + キャラ | introvert / extrovert type |
| ぼっち | botchi | ひとりぼっち (all alone) | being alone; ぼっち飯 is eating alone |
| ガチ | gachi | from sumo's ガチンコ (a real bout) | serious, for real |
| ガチ勢 | gachizei | ガチ + 勢 (group) | the hardcore players or fans of something |
| 中二病 / 厨二病 | chūnibyō | 中二, the second year of middle school | "eighth-grade syndrome", embarrassing teenage grandiosity |
| 神 | kami | god | god-tier, the best |
| ヤバい | yabai | dangerous | anything from awful to amazing, depending on tone |
| エモい | emoi | English "emo" | nostalgic, bittersweet, moving |
| 尊い | tōtoi | precious, sacred | so wonderful it hurts (fan culture) |
| メンヘラ | menhera | メンタルヘルス (mental health) + -er | emotionally volatile; often self-deprecating, and can be hurtful |
| 闇深 | yamibuka | 闇が深い (the darkness is deep) | having a disturbing or tragic backstory |
| ワンチャン | wanchan | "one chance" | maybe, there's a chance |
| ガン無視 | ganmushi | ガン (intensifier) + 無視 (ignore) | ignoring someone completely |
| 秒で | byō de | "in seconds" | instantly |

Some once-hot words now sound dated, as of 2026. なう (*nau*, from English "now", as in 渋谷なう, "in Shibuya now") belongs to the early days of Twitter, around 2010. 卍 (*manji*), a teen intensifier around 2017, and ぴえん (*pien*, a whimper inspired by the pleading-face emoji), which peaked around 2019 and 2020, are both recognized but rarely used in earnest. [Section 7.2](02-contemporary-japanese.md) has a longer list of words to recognize but not use.

## The Bulletin-Board Legacy

These terms came from 2channel and its descendants, and many have spread into mainstream speech.

| Term | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 煽る | aoru | to provoke, bait |
| 荒らし | arashi | a troll, someone who wrecks discussions |
| 釣り | tsuri | a deliberately misleading post (literally "fishing") |
| まとめ | matome | a roundup page compiling posts or news |
| スレ | sure | a thread |
| レス | resu | a reply (from "response") |
| 乙 | otsu | "thanks for your work" (from お疲れ様) |
| GJ | jījē | good job |
| KY | kēwai | 空気読めない, "can't read the room" |
| スルー力 | surūryoku | the skill of ignoring provocation |
| 既出 | kishutsu | already posted |

既出 has a famous joke attached: so many board users misread it as がいしゅつ (*gaishutsu*) that the misreading itself became a meme.

## LINE and Messaging Culture

LINE is Japan's dominant messaging app, used by most of the population for everything from family chats to customer service. Its stickers shaped a whole style of communication, and it gave Japanese a few words that every learner should know.

- 既読スルー (*kidoku surū*, read and ignored): seeing a message, which marks it 既読 (read), and not replying. It is one of the most discussed small anxieties of modern relationships.
- LINE交換 (*rain kōkan*, LINE exchange): swapping LINE accounts, the Japanese equivalent of "can I get your number?"
- ブロック (*burokku*, block): being blocked on LINE, which is a serious social statement.

## Gyaru Language

**Gyaru** (ギャル, from English "gal") was a youth fashion subculture of the 1990s and 2000s, with tanned skin, bleached hair and a heavily contracted, playful way of speaking. ギャル語 (*gyaru-go*, gyaru speech) fed a lot of vocabulary into general slang.

| Slang | Standard | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ウザい (*uzai*) | うっとうしい | annoying |
| キモい (*kimoi*) | 気持ち悪い | gross, creepy |
| ムカつく (*mukatsuku*) | 腹が立つ | irritating, makes me mad |
| マジ / マジで (*maji de*) | 本当に | seriously, really |
| チョー (*chō*) | とても | super |
| だるい (*darui*) | 面倒くさい | can't be bothered |
| テンション上がる | 興奮する | to get excited, hyped |

Many of these, such as マジ and ウザい, are older than the gyaru boom, but gyaru speech made them famous. めっちゃ (very) is another nationwide intensifier, which spread from Kansai dialect ([section 6.2](../06-spoken-japanese/02-dialects.md)) through comedians and television.

## Kaomoji: Emoticons Built Around the Eyes

Long before emoji, Japan had **kaomoji** (顔文字, face characters): emoticons you read upright, with no head-tilting required. Western emoticons lie sideways and focus on the mouth, as in :) and :(. Kaomoji face you and focus on the eyes, as in (^_^) and (T_T). A 2007 study led by the psychologist Masaki Yuki found that Japanese participants weighted the eyes more, and American participants the mouth, when judging emotion in faces, and it used emoticons to make the point. Kaomoji are also far more expressive: arms, sweat drops and whole flailing bodies are fair game.

| Mood | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Happy | `(^_^)` `(^o^)` `\(^o^)/` | smiling, celebrating |
| Sad | `(T_T)` `(;_;)` | tears |
| Angry | `(｀Д´)` | glaring |
| Awkward | `(^_^;)` `(-_-;)` | the ; is a drop of sweat |
| Surprised | `(O_O)` `Σ(°△°)` | shock |
| Dejected | `(´・ω・｀)` | "shobon", from 2channel |
| Defeated | `orz` | a person on hands and knees, seen from the side |
| Flipping out | `(╯°□°)╯︵ ┻━┻` | a table flip, like 卓袱台返し in old TV dramas |
| Shrug | `¯\_(ツ)_/¯` | the face is katakana ツ |

orz dates from Japanese web forums around 2002, and (´・ω・｀) from 2channel. On a Japanese phone keyboard, typing かお (*kao*, face) offers a menu of ready-made kaomoji.

> **Note:** As of 2026, kaomoji read as slightly nostalgic. People in their 30s and older still use them freely, while many younger users prefer emoji and stickers. Recognize them everywhere; but know that a classic (´・ω・｀) can make you sound a little older.

## Number Wordplay

**Goroawase** (語呂合わせ) is wordplay that reads numbers as words. It works because each digit has several possible readings, from Japanese, Sino-Japanese and even English.

| Digit | Possible readings |
|---|---|
| 0 | zero, rei, re, o, wa |
| 1 | ichi, i, hi |
| 2 | ni, fu, futa, tsu (from "two") |
| 3 | san, sa, mi |
| 4 | yon, yo, shi |
| 5 | go, ko, itsu |
| 6 | roku, ro, mu |
| 7 | nana, na, shichi |
| 8 | hachi, ha, ya, ba, pa |
| 9 | kyū, ku |

Some famous examples:

- 4649 is よろしく (*yo-ro-shi-ku*), "nice to meet you" or "best regards".
- 0840 is おはよう (*o-ha-yo-u*), "good morning".
- 0833 is おやすみ (*o-ya-su-mi*), "good night".
- 39 is サンキュー (*san-kyū*), "thank you".
- 1192 is いいくに (*ii kuni*), "a good country", the classic schoolroom mnemonic 1192つくろう鎌倉幕府 for the founding of the Kamakura shogunate. Many textbooks now date the shogunate to 1185 instead, so a new mnemonic, いい箱 (*ii hako*, 1185), has joined it.

You will see goroawase in phone numbers, prices, product names, pager-era messages and internet jokes.

## New Jobs for Old Kanji

The internet keeps repurposing ordinary kanji as intensifiers and labels.

| Kanji | Usual meaning | Online meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 草 | grass | lol |
| 神 | god | god-tier, amazing |
| 闇 | darkness | dark, disturbing depths |
| 鬼 | demon | super-, insanely: 鬼速い (insanely fast) |

## Staying Current

Internet Japanese moves weekly, and no list keeps up for long. The most reliable way to stay current is exposure: follow Japanese accounts on X, read the comments under Japanese videos, and join a Japanese-speaking Discord server or LINE group. Real usage beats any vocabulary table, including the ones in this book.

## Summary

- w and 草 both mean laughter online: w from 笑 (*warai*), and 草 because a row of w's looks like grass.
- Much Japanese internet slang came from the anonymous board 2channel (now 5channel) and spread through X, LINE and video sites into everyday speech.
- Slang ages quickly; as of 2026, words such as なう, 卍 and ぴえん are recognized but sound dated.
- Fandom vocabulary such as 推し, 推し活, ツンデレ and 聖地巡礼 is central to modern Japanese pop culture.
- Kaomoji such as (^_^) are read upright and focus on the eyes, unlike sideways Western emoticons.
- Goroawase turns numbers into words, as in 4649 for よろしく and 39 for サンキュー.
