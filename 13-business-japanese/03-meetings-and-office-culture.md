# Meetings, Phone Calls and Office Culture — Reading the Room at Work

A foreign manager presents a bold new proposal at a meeting in Osaka. The room listens politely, nods, asks two mild questions and says 「検討させていただきます」. The manager leaves delighted: they are going to consider it. Weeks pass. Nothing happens. What went wrong had nothing to do with grammar. The proposal arrived cold, without the quiet conversations that should have come first, and "we will consider it" was the gentlest available way of saying no.

Meetings and phone calls are where business Japanese is spoken live, at speed, with no time to look anything up. They run on set phrases, and behind the phrases sit habits of office culture that shape what those phrases mean.

## Meeting Vocabulary

A few words cover most of what happens around a meeting.

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 会議 | *kaigi* | Meeting (formal) |
| 打ち合わせ | *uchiawase* | Working meeting, briefing |
| 議題 | *gidai* | Agenda item, topic |
| 議事録 | *gijiroku* | Meeting minutes |
| 本題 | *hondai* | The main subject |

会議 is the formal meeting with an agenda and minutes; 打ち合わせ is the everyday working session where details get settled, and it is the word you will hear most.

## Meeting Phrases

### Starting a Meeting

Meetings open with thanks for the other side's time, then move to business.

> 本日はお時間をいただき、ありがとうございます。\
> *Honjitsu wa o-jikan o itadaki, arigatō gozaimasu.*\
> Thank you for taking the time to meet with us today.

> 早速ですが、本題に入らせていただきます。\
> *Sassoku desu ga, hondai ni hairasete itadakimasu.*\
> If I may, let's get straight to the main topic.

> 本日の議題は〜です。\
> *Honjitsu no gidai wa ~ desu.*\
> Today's agenda is ~.

### Asking for Clarification

Asking someone to repeat or explain is fine, as long as the request is wrapped politely and the problem is framed as yours, not theirs.

> もう少し詳しくお聞かせいただけますか。\
> *Mō sukoshi kuwashiku o-kikase itadakemasu ka.*\
> Could you tell us a little more about that?

> おっしゃる意味がよく分かりませんでした。\
> *Ossharu imi ga yoku wakarimasen deshita.*\
> I didn't quite understand what you meant.

### Agreeing and Acknowledging

> おっしゃる通りです。\
> *Ossharu tōri desu.*\
> It's exactly as you say.

> 承知いたしました。\
> *Shōchi itashimashita.*\
> Understood; I'll take care of it.

> かしこまりました。\
> *Kashikomarimashita.*\
> Certainly. (very formal, common with customers)

> **Warning:** 了解しました (*ryōkai shimashita*, understood) is widely used between colleagues, but many people consider it too casual toward a superior or a client, and business-manner guides usually advise against it there. Use 承知いたしました with anyone above you or outside the company; [section 5.5](../05-politeness/05-modern-keigo.md) covers debates like this one.

### Declining and Pushing Back

Direct refusals are rare in Japanese business. Disagreement arrives padded, and "no" is often implied rather than said.

> ご提案はごもっともですが、弊社の状況といたしましては……\
> *Go-teian wa go-mottomo desu ga, heisha no jōkyō to itashimashite wa…*\
> Your proposal is very reasonable, but given our company's situation…

> 検討させていただきます。\
> *Kentō sasete itadakimasu.*\
> We will consider it.

> それはちょっと難しいですね。\
> *Sore wa chotto muzukashii desu ne.*\
> That would be a little difficult.

検討させていただきます can be a sincere promise to think things over, but in many contexts it is a polite way to close a topic without agreeing. "That's difficult" (難しい) in a business setting often means no. Listen for what comes next: a concrete follow-up date suggests real consideration, while silence suggests the answer has already been given.

### Closing a Meeting

> 本日は大変参考になりました。\
> *Honjitsu wa taihen sankō ni narimashita.*\
> Today was very helpful and informative.

> それでは、今後ともよろしくお願いいたします。\
> *Sore de wa, kongo tomo yoroshiku onegai itashimasu.*\
> We look forward to continuing to work with you.

## On the Phone

Business phone calls compress all of this into a few seconds of rapid formulas, without the help of facial expressions. Many learners find them the most nerve-racking part of office life, and the cure is to memorize the script. Note that もしもし, the everyday "hello" on the phone, is usually avoided when answering a business call.

> はい、〇〇株式会社でございます。\
> *Hai, 〇〇 kabushiki-gaisha de gozaimasu.*\
> Hello, 〇〇 Corporation.

> 〇〇部の田中様はいらっしゃいますか。\
> *〇〇-bu no Tanaka-sama wa irasshaimasu ka.*\
> Is Ms. Tanaka of the 〇〇 department available?

> 少々お待ちください。\
> *Shōshō o-machi kudasai.*\
> One moment, please.

> 申し訳ございません。鈴木はただいま席を外しております。\
> *Mōshiwake gozaimasen. Suzuki wa tadaima seki o hazushite orimasu.*\
> I'm sorry, Suzuki is away from their desk at the moment.

> 戻りましたら、折り返しお電話いたします。\
> *Modorimashitara, orikaeshi o-denwa itashimasu.*\
> When they're back, they will call you back.

> 恐れ入りますが、もう一度お名前を伺ってもよろしいでしょうか。\
> *Osoreirimasu ga, mō ichido o-namae o ukagatte mo yoroshii deshō ka.*\
> I'm sorry, may I ask your name once more?

The fourth example shows the inside-outside rule at work: to a caller from outside, your colleague is plain 鈴木, with no さん, even if Suzuki is the company president. 折り返し (*orikaeshi*, calling back) is the word to listen for when someone promises to return your call. Calls end with 失礼いたします (*shitsurei itashimasu*), and the caller traditionally hangs up first.

## Hōrensō: Report, Contact, Consult

**報連相** (*hōrensō*) is the backbone of communication inside a Japanese team. The word is a pun: it sounds like ほうれん草 (spinach), which makes it easy to remember. It stands for three habits:

- **報告 (*hōkoku*, report):** update your superior on the progress and outcome of tasks they gave you, without waiting to be asked.
- **連絡 (*renraku*, contact):** pass on information that affects others in the team, promptly and proactively.
- **相談 (*sōdan*, consult):** bring problems and decisions to your superior *before* acting on your own.

Compared with many Western workplaces, 報連相 asks you to communicate more and earlier. Surprises are bad; a small problem reported early is far better than a solved problem reported late, because the second one means your manager was left out.

> **Tip:** When you are unsure whether something is worth mentioning, mention it. 念のため (*nen no tame*, just to be safe) is the phrase that makes an extra update sound conscientious rather than nervous.

## Nemawashi: The Meeting Before the Meeting

**根回し** (*nemawashi*) literally means "going around the roots": when a tree is moved, the gardener first digs around its roots and prepares them so the transplant survives. In business it means building consensus informally before a decision is made in public.

Before a proposal reaches a formal meeting, its author typically:

1. Talks one-on-one with the key stakeholders.
2. Shares documents and ideas informally in advance.
3. Listens for objections and adjusts the proposal.
4. Arrives at the formal meeting with agreement already in place.

The formal meeting then confirms a decision that has largely been made, which is why Japanese meetings can feel ceremonial to newcomers. Understanding 根回し prevents a great deal of frustration: a proposal introduced cold at a meeting, as in the story that opens this section, will often stall however good it is.

## Company Culture Words

A handful of words describe how Japanese companies have traditionally worked and how that is changing. You will meet all of them in conversation and in the news.

**年功序列** (*nenkō joretsu*, the seniority system) is the system in which pay and position rise largely with years of service. It is fading, especially in younger and international firms, but it still shapes many large companies.

**終身雇用** (*shūshin koyō*, lifetime employment) means one company, one career, from graduation to retirement. It was never universal (it applied mainly to regular employees of large firms) and job changes are increasingly common.

**飲みニケーション** (*nominikēshon*) blends 飲む (*nomu*, to drink) with コミュニケーション (communication) and means bonding with colleagues over after-work drinks. Office drinking parties (飲み会, *nomikai*) were long treated as close to obligatory; attitudes have relaxed, especially among younger workers, and declining politely is far more accepted than it once was.

有給が取れない (*yūkyū ga torenai*), "I can't take my paid leave", is a familiar complaint. Workers are legally entitled to paid leave, but social pressure has long kept many from using it, and since 2019 employers have been required to make sure eligible employees take at least five days a year.

**ブラック企業** (*burakku kigyō*), a "black company", is an exploitative employer with excessive overtime and poor treatment. Its opposite is ホワイト企業 (*howaito kigyō*), a "white company" that treats its staff well.

## Summary

- Meetings open with thanks for the other side's time and move to business with 早速ですが.
- Use 承知いたしました rather than 了解しました when acknowledging a superior or a client.
- 検討させていただきます and 難しいですね often signal a polite no; a concrete next step is the sign of genuine interest.
- On the phone, refer to your own colleagues without さん to outside callers and promise a callback with 折り返しお電話いたします.
- 報連相 (report, contact, consult) means keeping your superior informed early and often, and 根回し means building agreement before the formal meeting.
- Traditional patterns such as seniority pay, lifetime employment and obligatory drinking are weakening but still shape many Japanese workplaces as of 2026.
