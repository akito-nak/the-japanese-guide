# Workplace Language and Introductions — Your First Day at the Office

Picture your first morning at a Tokyo office. Your new manager walks you to the front of the room, 20 faces turn toward you, and someone says 「じゃあ、自己紹介をお願いします」. You know polite Japanese. You know the textbook です and ます. But the words that come next, the way you name your own company, the way you hand over a business card and the way you speak about your boss to a client, follow a script that everyone in the room has known since their first job.

**Business Japanese** (ビジネス日本語, *bijinesu nihongo*) is that script: the vocabulary, set phrases and conventions of professional life in Japan. It is not "polite Japanese turned up to 11". It has its own words, its own rituals, its own written forms and its own unspoken rules. Even learners who chat happily in casual Japanese study it separately, and so do Japanese university graduates, who buy shelves of books on it before their first job.

## Why Business Japanese Is Its Own World

Business Japanese differs from everyday polite Japanese in four ways:

1. **Vocabulary.** A distinct set of words, set expressions and honorific forms: you will rarely say 会社 (*kaisha*, company) about a client's company.
2. **Written conventions.** Emails and documents follow fixed structures with standard openings and closings (section 13.2 covers them).
3. **Implicit rules.** What is said and what is meant can differ, especially when someone is saying no.
4. **Hierarchy encoding.** Every sentence signals your position and theirs, and whether the person you are talking about is inside or outside your company.

Being generally polite is not enough. Specific formulas are expected, and departing from them reads as inexperienced rather than rude. The good news is that formulas can be learned, and once you have them, they carry you through situations that would be terrifying to improvise.

## The Core Vocabulary

The words for people and relationships come first, because they decide how everything else is phrased. Notice that "your company" and "our company" have entirely different words: you raise the other side and lower your own.

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 御社 / 貴社 | *onsha* / *kisha* | Your company (spoken / written) |
| 弊社 / 当社 | *heisha* / *tōsha* | Our company (humble / neutral) |
| 担当者 | *tantōsha* | Person in charge |
| 上司 | *jōshi* | Superior, boss |
| 部下 | *buka* | Subordinate |
| 同僚 | *dōryō* | Colleague |
| 取引先 | *torihikisaki* | Business partner, client |
| 得意先 | *tokuisaki* | Regular customer, client |
| お客様 | *okyakusama* | Customer, guest (highest respect) |
| 先方 | *senpō* | The other party |

The terms 御社 and 貴社 for "your company" and 弊社 for "our company" are worth learning as a set. Use **御社** (*onsha*) when speaking, for example in an interview, and **貴社** (*kisha*) in writing, partly because 貴社 sounds identical to 記者 (reporter) and 汽車 (steam train) and would be ambiguous aloud. **弊社** (*heisha*, literally "our humble company") is the humble form you use for your own employer when talking to outsiders.

The verbs of office life turn up in every email and meeting. Most are a noun plus する, so one table gives you dozens of sentences.

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 確認する | *kakunin suru* | To confirm, check |
| 共有する | *kyōyū suru* | To share (information) |
| 対応する | *taiō suru* | To respond to, handle |
| 検討する | *kentō suru* | To consider, examine |
| 調整する | *chōsei suru* | To coordinate, adjust (schedules, plans) |
| 承認する | *shōnin suru* | To approve |
| 手配する | *tehai suru* | To arrange (tickets, rooms, deliveries) |
| ご連絡する | *go-renraku suru* | To contact (humble) |
| ご確認いただく | *go-kakunin itadaku* | To have someone check (humble, receiving the favor) |

> **Note:** Inside your company, colleagues are addressed with さん or by title (部長, *buchō*, department head). When you talk about those same colleagues to someone outside the company, you drop さん and the title and use the plain family name, because they are now on your side of the line. [Section 5.4](../05-politeness/04-honorific-japanese.md) explains this inside-outside logic in full.

## Introducing Yourself

A workplace self-introduction (自己紹介, *jiko shōkai*) is short and formulaic: greeting, name, where you come from (company, department or previous role), one line about yourself, and the closing phrase that does the real work.

> 本日からお世話になります、スミスと申します。\
> *Honjitsu kara o-sewa ni narimasu, Sumisu to mōshimasu.*\
> My name is Smith, and I'll be joining you from today.

> 前職では五年間、営業を担当しておりました。\
> *Zenshoku de wa gonenkan, eigyō o tantō shite orimashita.*\
> In my previous job I worked in sales for five years.

> 至らない点も多いかと思いますが、どうぞよろしくお願いいたします。\
> *Itaranai ten mo ōi ka to omoimasu ga, dōzo yoroshiku onegai itashimasu.*\
> I'm sure there is much I still have to learn, but I look forward to working with you.

The last line sounds self-deprecating in English, but in Japanese it is the expected modesty, and it lands warmly. 申します (*mōshimasu*) is the humble "am called", and よろしくお願いいたします is the humble version of the phrase you learned as a beginner.

## Exchanging Business Cards

The exchange of business cards, **名刺交換** (*meishi kōkan*), is a small ritual with its own choreography. The card stands in for the person, so how you treat it is how you treat them.

1. **Prepare.** Keep your cards in a card holder (名刺入れ, *meishi-ire*), never loose in a pocket or wallet.
2. **Present.** Stand, face the other person, and offer your card with both hands, text turned toward them, with a slight bow and your name and company.
3. **Receive.** Take their card with both hands, look at it properly and acknowledge it.
4. **Place it respectfully.** During a meeting, set it on the table in front of you, on top of your card holder; with several people, arrange the cards to match where everyone is sitting.
5. **Never** write on it, fold it, put it in a back pocket or leave it behind on the table.

The phrase for receiving a card is short and worth having ready:

> 頂戴いたします。\
> *Chōdai itashimasu.*\
> Thank you, I humbly receive it.

> **Tip:** When you hand over your card, hold it slightly lower than the other person's if you are the junior party or the visitor. It is a small gesture, but people notice it, and it shows you know the ritual from the inside.

## Job Interview Japanese

Interviews combine the self-introduction with keigo under pressure. A model opening statement looks like this:

> 私は〇〇大学〇〇学部を卒業し、〇〇株式会社に三年間勤めておりました。\
> *Watashi wa 〇〇 daigaku 〇〇 gakubu o sotsugyō shi, 〇〇 kabushiki-gaisha ni sannenkan tsutomete orimashita.*\
> I graduated from the faculty of 〇〇 at 〇〇 University and worked at 〇〇 Corporation for three years.

> 主に〇〇業務を担当し、〜の経験を積んでまいりました。\
> *Omo ni 〇〇 gyōmu o tantō shi, ~ no keiken o tsunde mairimashita.*\
> I was mainly responsible for 〇〇 and have built up experience in ~.

The same questions come up in almost every interview, phrased politely as requests:

| Question | Japanese |
|---|---|
| Tell me about yourself | 自己紹介をお願いします |
| Why do you want this job? | 志望動機をお聞かせください |
| What are your strengths? | あなたの長所は何ですか |
| What are your weaknesses? | 短所についてお聞かせください |
| Where do you see yourself in five years? | 5年後のビジョンを教えてください |

志望動機 (*shibō dōki*, motivation for applying) is the question interviewers care about most, so prepare it carefully, and remember to call their company 御社 throughout. For answers about past experience, the familiar situation-task-action-result structure works well in Japanese:

- 状況 (*jōkyō*) — the situation
- 課題 (*kadai*) — the challenge or task
- 行動 (*kōdō*) — the action you took
- 結果 (*kekka*) — the result

## Employment and Working Conditions

Job ads, contracts and conversations about pay use a compact set of words. Learn them before you sign anything.

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 給料 / 給与 | *kyūryō* / *kyūyo* | Salary, pay |
| 基本給 | *kihonkyū* | Base salary |
| 賞与 / ボーナス | *shōyo* / *bōnasu* | Bonus |
| 残業 | *zangyō* | Overtime |
| 有給休暇 | *yūkyū kyūka* | Paid leave |
| 社会保険 | *shakai hoken* | Social insurance |
| 試用期間 | *shiyō kikan* | Probationary period |
| 正社員 | *seishain* | Regular (permanent, full-time) employee |
| 契約社員 | *keiyaku shain* | Contract employee |
| 派遣社員 | *haken shain* | Agency (dispatched) worker |
| アルバイト | *arubaito* | Part-time worker |

The difference between 正社員 and the other categories matters: it usually decides job security, bonuses and benefits, and it is one of the first things Japanese friends will ask about a new job.

## Summary

- Business Japanese is a distinct register with its own vocabulary, written forms and rituals, and Japanese graduates study it too.
- Call another company 御社 when speaking and 貴社 in writing; call your own company 弊社 when talking to outsiders.
- A workplace self-introduction uses 申します for your name and ends with よろしくお願いいたします.
- Business cards are offered and received with both hands, read with attention, kept on the table during the meeting and never written on.
- Interviewers ask about your 志望動機 (motivation for applying) above all, and expect you to call their company 御社.
