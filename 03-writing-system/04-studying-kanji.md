# Studying Kanji — Methods, WaniKani and Anki

Two thousand kanji is not a mountain you climb in one go. It is ten or fifteen new characters a day, reviewed at the right moments, kept up for a year or two. Learners who reach comfortable reading rarely have better memories than anyone else. They have a method they trust and a daily habit they protect, and the software does the hard part: remembering what you are about to forget.

## Choosing a Method

Learners argue passionately about the right way to learn kanji. The honest answer is that several approaches work, and they suit different people:

**Remembering the Kanji, by James W. Heisig.** About 2,200 kanji, each given an English keyword and an imaginative story built from its components. Readings are left for later. It gives fast recognition and memorable stories covering the whole jōyō list, but you finish knowing meanings without being able to read the kanji aloud. It suits learners who want to recognize kanji first and are happy to learn readings separately.

**[WaniKani](https://www.wanikani.com).** A web-based spaced repetition system that teaches components, then kanji, then vocabulary in a fixed order. It teaches meanings and readings together and reinforces each kanji with real words. It is a paid subscription after the first three levels, it takes a year or two to finish, and you follow its order rather than your own. It suits consistent learners who like structure.

**The Kodansha Kanji Learner's Course, by Andrew Scott Conning.** About 2,300 kanji in one book, grouped so that related shapes and meanings reinforce each other. It is comprehensive and self-contained, but dense, and it rewards discipline. It suits self-studiers who want a physical book.

**Context first.** Learn kanji through the vocabulary you meet: read real Japanese, look kanji up as you go and build flashcards from your own reading. You learn only what is relevant and retain it well, but coverage has gaps and it takes effort to organize. It suits intermediate learners who already read a lot.

Most successful learners combine approaches: a structured system to build a recognition base, plus plenty of real reading. The kanji you meet in your own interests, whether manga, news or work, stick fastest.

## What the Research Says

Start with the good news: kanji use is extremely uneven. A few hundred characters turn up constantly, and the rest thin out fast. Japan's National Language Research Institute counted every kanji in a year of three national newspapers (1966 editions, reported in 1971) and in 90 magazines (1956):

| Most frequent kanji | Share of kanji in newspapers | Share of kanji in magazines |
|---|---|---|
| 500 | 80.0% | 74.5% |
| 1,000 | 94.1% | 90.0% |

The curve has not flattened since. When the Agency for Cultural Affairs counted about 50 million kanji in books, magazines and textbooks for its 2007 survey, the 1,945 characters of the old jōyō list covered over 96% of them. The first thousand kanji deserve most of your effort, and the full jōyō list takes you to the point where an unknown kanji is a rare event.

Coverage is not comprehension, though. Knowing 94% of the kanji on a page still leaves about one in seventeen unknown, and the unknown ones tend to be the informative ones: the name, the technical term, the key verb. Reading research puts comfortable reading at about 98% of the *words* known (Hu and Nation, 2000; see [section 1.2](../01-how-to-learn-japanese/02-study-and-immersion.md)), and in Japanese a word is not the same as its kanji. You can know 大 (big) and 人 (person) and still need to learn that 大人 is read おとな (*otona*) and means "adult".

That is the case for learning words, not isolated kanji. A kanji alone often has several readings; a word pins down one of them. 生 is *sei* in 学生 (がくせい, *gakusei*, student), *nama* in 生ビール (なまビール, *nama bīru*, draft beer) and *u* in 生まれる (うまれる, *umareru*, to be born). Learn the word and the reading comes with it. This rests on how reading works rather than on a kanji experiment: the unit you read is the word, so the unit you study should be too.

The evidence on components is more direct. Mary Flaherty and Mary Sisk Noguchi (1998, *JALT Journal*) taught 30 new kanji to 29 English-speaking adults who knew only a few dozen, half by the traditional whole-kanji method of repeated copying and half by component analysis: breaking each kanji into named parts and linking them with a story. The component group remembered meanings, readings and written forms significantly better on tests given in the same session and one to three weeks later; among the learners living in Japan, it recalled the meanings of about 25 of the 30 kanji against about 14 for the copying group. A month later the advantage held only for the learners living in Japan, who kept meeting kanji around them; for the group studying in Ireland it had faded to a trend. It is one small study, but it points the same way as the larger research on mnemonics (Atkinson and Raugh, 1975; see [section 3.1](01-hiragana.md)): give each new shape a structure you can remember it by, then keep meeting it.

Finally, the general memory research applies in full. Reviews spaced over time beat cramming (Cepeda et al., 2006), and recalling an answer beats rereading it (Karpicke and Roediger, 2008). Every method in this section is a way of combining components or stories with those two principles, and [section 1.2](../01-how-to-learn-japanese/02-study-and-immersion.md) explains them. What the evidence does not support is copying each kanji out twenty times as a whole picture: it feels productive, and it is the slowest route there is.

## Studying with WaniKani

[WaniKani](https://www.wanikani.com) teaches over 2,000 kanji and about 6,000 vocabulary words across 60 levels, as of 2026. The first three levels are free.

### Components, Kanji, Vocabulary

It builds knowledge in three layers, and each layer unlocks the next:

| Layer | What it is | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Radicals | A named visual component | 氵, which WaniKani calls "tsunami" |
| Kanji | Built from components, taught with meaning and readings | 海 (sea): tsunami plus 毎 (every) |
| Vocabulary | Real words using the kanji | 海外 (かいがい, *kaigai*, overseas), 日本海 (にほんかい, *Nihonkai*, the Sea of Japan) |

You cannot unlock a kanji until you have learned its components, or a word until you know its kanji. The system is deliberately sequential.

### The SRS Stages

WaniKani is a **spaced repetition system** (SRS): software that shows you each item again just before you are likely to forget it, at intervals that grow every time you answer correctly. Each item climbs through these stages:

| Stage | Wait before the next review |
|---|---|
| Apprentice 1 | 4 hours |
| Apprentice 2 | 8 hours |
| Apprentice 3 | 1 day |
| Apprentice 4 | 2 days |
| Guru 1 | 1 week |
| Guru 2 | 2 weeks |
| Master | 1 month |
| Enlightened | 4 months |
| Burned | No more reviews |

Answer wrongly and the item drops back down. That is the point: the system spends your time where your memory is weakest. (A few early levels use shorter intervals to get you started.)

### A Typical Day

- **Morning, 10 to 20 minutes.** Do your reviews first. They come due on WaniKani's schedule, not yours, and skipping them builds a backlog.
- **Midday, 5 to 15 minutes.** Learn a batch of new items. Keep it to 10 or 15 a day: each new item comes back four times in its first few days, and learners who take on 50 at once drown in reviews two days later.
- **Evening, 5 to 10 minutes.** A second review session. Because new items return after four hours, learning in the morning and reviewing in the evening keeps the cycle moving.

### Mistakes to Avoid

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Instead |
|---|---|---|
| Taking on too many new items | Floods your reviews a day or two later | Cap new items at 10 to 15 a day |
| Skipping vocabulary | Vocabulary is what makes the kanji stick | Do all the vocabulary at each level |
| Ignoring the radicals | They are the hooks the mnemonics hang on | Learn the component names, even the silly ones |
| Marking wrong answers right | Breaks the scheduling | Answer honestly; the system needs accurate data |
| Letting a busy week end the habit | Reviews pile up and motivation drops | Do a short review session every day, however small |
| Doing only WaniKani | It teaches recognition, not usage | Read real Japanese alongside it from the start |

A few more habits help. Look each new kanji up on [Jisho](https://jisho.org) to see more words that use it. Read the mnemonic story properly instead of memorizing the answer, because the story is the method. Give extra attention to **leeches**, the items you keep getting wrong, by writing them out by hand or studying them in a separate drill. Do not try to speed-run: a level takes about a week at full pace, and the intervals exist for a reason. The [WaniKani Community](https://community.wanikani.com) forums are friendly and full of study tools.

## Studying with Anki

[Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net) is free, open-source flashcard software built on spaced repetition. Unlike WaniKani it comes with no content: you make the cards or download a shared deck.

### Getting Started

1. **Install it** from [apps.ankiweb.net](https://apps.ankiweb.net). It is free on Windows, Mac, Linux and Android; the iPhone and iPad app (AnkiMobile) is a one-time purchase.
2. **Find a deck or make your own.** The [AnkiWeb shared decks](https://ankiweb.net/shared/decks) library has many Japanese decks: frequency-based vocabulary decks (the long-running Core 2k/6k and the newer Kaishi 1.5k are popular), decks organized by JLPT level, and kanji decks that follow Remembering the Kanji. Read the reviews before you commit to one.
3. **Review every day.** Consistency is the whole trick.

### Settings Worth Changing

Anki's defaults suit general use rather than heavy vocabulary study. These changes, made in Deck Options, are common advice among Japanese learners:

| Setting | Default | Suggested | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| New cards/day | 20 | 10 to 15 | More creates a review load that is hard to sustain |
| Maximum reviews/day | 200 | Leave it high | A low cap lets due cards pile up out of sight |
| New interval (after a lapse) | 0.00 | 0.20 | A forgotten card keeps a little of its progress |
| Leech threshold | 8 | 5 | Flags trouble cards sooner |

Since version 23.10, Anki also offers FSRS, a newer scheduling algorithm that adapts its intervals to your own review history. If you turn it on, the main setting is desired retention (90 percent by default), and several of the older interval settings no longer apply. The [Anki manual](https://docs.ankiweb.net/deck-options.html) explains every option.

### Two Kinds of Card

A vocabulary card shows a word and asks for its reading and meaning:

```text
Front: 電車
Back:  でんしゃ (densha) — train
```

It is fast to review and covers a lot of ground, but you can learn to recognize the card without truly knowing the word.

A sentence card shows the word inside a sentence:

```text
Front: 毎日電車で通勤しています。
Back:  I commute by train every day.
       電車 (densha) — train
```

It is slower, but the context makes the word stick and transfers better to real reading. Start with vocabulary cards as a beginner, then move to sentence cards built from what you actually read.

### Sentence Mining

**Sentence mining** means making cards from the Japanese you are already reading or watching: manga, news, subtitles. The routine is short:

1. Read or watch something in Japanese.
2. Notice an unknown word that matters.
3. Look it up with a pop-up dictionary such as [Yomitan](https://github.com/yomidevs/yomitan), a browser extension that shows a definition when you hover over a word.
4. Send the word and its sentence to Anki with one click (Yomitan can do this for you).
5. Review it with the rest of your cards.

Because you met the word in a context you cared about, these cards tend to stick much better than cards from a pre-made deck.

### Reviewing Honestly

Anki can only schedule well if your answers are honest. Press *Again* if you got it wrong, hesitated for long, or only vaguely recognized the card; *Hard* if you got it with real effort; *Good* for a normal correct answer; and *Easy* only when the answer was instant. Most of your answers should be Good or Again. Above all, review every day: 10 minutes daily beats a two-hour catch-up on Sunday, because missed reviews stack up quickly. Attach your reviews to something you already do, like morning coffee or the commute, and protect that time.

## Habits for the Long Haul

1. **Learn words, not isolated kanji.** 森 (もり, *mori*, forest) is easy to remember once you know 木 (tree) and can picture three of them together.
2. **Write by hand now and then.** You do not need to write fluently, but drawing the strokes makes you notice details you would otherwise blur together, such as 未 (not yet) and 末 (end), which differ only in which horizontal is longer.
3. **Use spaced repetition.** Anki or WaniKani decides *when* to review, so you do not waste time on what you already know. Spacing and self-testing are the best-supported techniques in memory research ([section 1.2](../01-how-to-learn-japanese/02-study-and-immersion.md)).
4. **Read what you enjoy.** Manga kanji stick because you care what happens next.
5. **Pace yourself.** Ten to 15 new kanji a day, kept up for months, beats 50 a day for a week.
6. **Make progress visible.** Crossing kanji off a paper chart is surprisingly motivating.

## Summary

- Kanji use is uneven: in newspaper counts the 1,000 most frequent kanji make up about 94% of all kanji, and the old jōyō list covered over 96% of the kanji in books, so the first thousand deserve most of your effort.
- Research supports learning kanji through their components (Flaherty and Noguchi, 1998), learning them inside words, which fix the reading, and spaced self-testing rather than rote copying.
- Several kanji methods work: keyword stories (Remembering the Kanji), a structured app (WaniKani), a single comprehensive book (the Kodansha course) or learning through your own reading; most successful learners combine a structured system with real reading.
- WaniKani teaches components, then kanji, then vocabulary across 60 levels, in a fixed order; Anki is free flashcard software that you fill with shared decks or your own cards.
- A spaced repetition system shows each item again just before you are likely to forget it; keep new items to about 10 to 15 a day, review every day, answer honestly and give extra attention to leeches, the items you keep getting wrong.
- Sentence mining, making cards from the Japanese you already read or watch, gives each word a context you care about and makes it stick.
