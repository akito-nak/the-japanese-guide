# Hiragana — Your First 46 Friends

Picture a shop door in Japan with a small sign hanging from it: いらっしゃいませ. Right now it is a row of pretty curls. A couple of weeks from now you will read it aloud without thinking, *irasshaimase* ("welcome in"), and you will hear it shouted cheerfully every time you walk into a shop. That shift, from decoration to meaning, is what learning hiragana gives you.

**Hiragana** (ひらがな) is the basic phonetic script of Japanese: every sound the language makes can be written with it. Each character stands for a syllable, not a letter, so there are no spelling surprises once you know the set. There are 46 basic characters, a handful of marks that modify them, and a few small characters that combine with them. With daily practice, many learners can read all of it within two or three weeks. There are no shortcuts, but there are good mnemonics, and this section has plenty.

## Three Scripts, One Sentence

Japanese is written with three scripts, often all in the same sentence. That sounds alarming until you see how neatly they divide the work:

| Script | Main use | How many |
|---|---|---|
| Hiragana | Native words, grammar endings, particles | 46 basic characters plus variations |
| **Katakana** | Loanwords, foreign names, sound effects, emphasis | 46 basic characters plus variations |
| **Kanji** | Meaning-carrying characters borrowed from Chinese | 2,136 in the official list for everyday use |

You will lean on hiragana for everything at first. Japanese books for children and learners print small hiragana called **furigana** above or beside kanji to show how they are read, so knowing hiragana lets you read far more than hiragana words. Katakana gets its own treatment in [section 3.2](02-katakana.md), and kanji in [section 3.3](03-kanji.md).

## The Five Vowels

Every hiragana character except ん ends in one of five vowels, and those vowels never change their sound. Learn them first and every row of the chart falls into place.

| Character | Romanization | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| あ | a | "ah" in *father* |
| い | i | "ee" in *feet*, but shorter |
| う | u | "oo" in *food*, short and with relaxed, unrounded lips |
| え | e | "e" in *bed* |
| お | o | "o" in *open*, without the English glide at the end |

Japanese vowels are short and crisp. English speakers tend to slide *o* into "oh-oo" and *e* into "ay-ee"; keep each one pure and you will already sound better than most beginners. [Section 2.2](../02-sounds-of-japanese/02-vowels-and-consonants.md) covers each vowel and consonant in detail, with exercises for the ear as well as the mouth.

## The Gojūon Chart

The full set of basic characters is laid out in a grid called the **gojūon** (五十音, "fifty sounds"). Columns are vowels; rows are consonants. Read along a row and you hear the consonant paired with each vowel in turn: か き く け こ, *ka ki ku ke ko*.

|  | a | i | u | e | o |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| (vowel) | あ | い | う | え | お |
| k | か | き | く | け | こ |
| s | さ | し | す | せ | そ |
| t | た | ち | つ | て | と |
| n | な | に | ぬ | ね | の |
| h | は | ひ | ふ | へ | ほ |
| m | ま | み | む | め | も |
| y | や | | ゆ | | よ |
| r | ら | り | る | れ | ろ |
| w | わ | | | | を |
| n | ん | | | | |

The name promises fifty, but the gaps in the *y* and *w* rows (sounds that dropped out of the language centuries ago) leave 46 characters in modern use.

> **Note:** Four characters break the pattern of their row: し is *shi* (not *si*), ち is *chi* (not *ti*), つ is *tsu* (not *tu*) and ふ is *fu*, a soft sound made by blowing through relaxed lips. Learn these exceptions early and they stop being exceptions.

The *r* row deserves a word too. The Japanese *r* is a quick tap of the tongue behind the upper teeth, somewhere between an English *r*, *l* and the *d* in American "ladder". Aim for the tap and you will be understood.

## Three Characters with Day Jobs

Three hiragana have a second pronunciation when they work as **particles**, the small grammar words that mark the role of the word before them:

| Character | Normal reading | Reading as a particle | Particle's job |
|---|---|---|---|
| は | ha | **wa** | marks the topic |
| へ | he | **e** | marks a direction |
| を | (w)o | **o** | marks the object |

The spellings are fossils from older Japanese; the pronunciation moved on and the spelling stayed put. You will meet these three particles in almost every sentence:

> わたしは がくせいです。\
> *Watashi wa gakusei desu.*\
> I am a student.
>
> がっこうへ いきます。\
> *Gakkō e ikimasu.*\
> I go to school.
>
> みずを のみます。\
> *Mizu o nomimasu.*\
> I drink water.

Hiragana-only text, as in children's books, puts spaces between phrases to help the eye. Ordinary Japanese uses no spaces at all, and the mix of kanji and kana does that job instead. The particles themselves are the subject of [section 4.1](../04-building-sentences/01-grammar-basics.md).

## Dakuten and Handakuten

Two small marks turn the basic 46 into a much bigger set. **Dakuten** (゛, two short strokes at the top right) voice the consonant: *k* becomes *g*, *s* becomes *z*, *t* becomes *d* and *h* becomes *b*. **Handakuten** (゜, a small circle) appear only on the *h* row and turn it into *p*.

| Base | With dakuten | Base | With dakuten |
|---|---|---|---|
| か ka | が ga | た ta | だ da |
| き ki | ぎ gi | ち chi | ぢ ji |
| く ku | ぐ gu | つ tsu | づ zu |
| け ke | げ ge | て te | で de |
| こ ko | ご go | と to | ど do |
| さ sa | ざ za | は ha | ば ba |
| し shi | じ ji | ひ hi | び bi |
| す su | ず zu | ふ fu | ぶ bu |
| せ se | ぜ ze | へ he | べ be |
| そ so | ぞ zo | ほ ho | ぼ bo |

| Base | With handakuten |
|---|---|
| は ha | ぱ pa |
| ひ hi | ぴ pi |
| ふ fu | ぷ pu |
| へ he | ぺ pe |
| ほ ho | ぽ po |

ぢ and づ sound exactly like じ and ず, and you will rarely write them. They appear mainly when a word that starts with ち or つ becomes voiced inside a compound: はな (*hana*, nose) plus ち (*chi*, blood) gives はなぢ (*hanaji*, a nosebleed), and つづく (*tsuzuku*, to continue) repeats a つ. Everywhere else, *ji* is じ and *zu* is ず.

## Yōon: Combined Sounds

The *i*-column characters (き, し, ち, に, ひ, み, り and their voiced forms) can combine with a small や, ゆ or よ to make a single syllable. These combinations are called **yōon** (拗音). The size matters: きや is two syllables, *ki-ya*, while きゃ is one, *kya*.

| | small ゃ | small ゅ | small ょ |
|---|---|---|---|
| き | きゃ kya | きゅ kyu | きょ kyo |
| し | しゃ sha | しゅ shu | しょ sho |
| ち | ちゃ cha | ちゅ chu | ちょ cho |
| に | にゃ nya | にゅ nyu | にょ nyo |
| ひ | ひゃ hya | ひゅ hyu | ひょ hyo |
| み | みゃ mya | みゅ myu | みょ myo |
| り | りゃ rya | りゅ ryu | りょ ryo |
| ぎ | ぎゃ gya | ぎゅ gyu | ぎょ gyo |
| じ | じゃ ja | じゅ ju | じょ jo |
| び | びゃ bya | びゅ byu | びょ byo |
| ぴ | ぴゃ pya | ぴゅ pyu | ぴょ pyo |

Getting the size right is not pedantry. びょういん (*byōin*) is a hospital; びよういん (*biyōin*) is a beauty salon. One of those is a much better place to go with a broken arm.

## Small っ: Double Consonants

A small っ is not pronounced as *tsu*. It is the **sokuon** (促音), a tiny pause that doubles the consonant after it. Romanization shows it by writing that consonant twice.

| Word | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| きって | kitte | postage stamp |
| ざっし | zasshi | magazine |
| ちょっと | chotto | a little |
| がっこう | gakkō | school |

Feel the beat: *ki-t-te* has three beats, and the middle one is silence. Compare きて (*kite*, "come") with きって (*kitte*, "stamp"): the only difference is that held breath. Japanese timing counts these beats, called *mora*, evenly, and a missing pause changes the word. [Section 2.3](../02-sounds-of-japanese/03-long-vowels-and-double-consonants.md) trains your ear to hear that pause, which is harder than writing it.

## Long Vowels

A **long vowel** is held for two beats instead of one, and in hiragana you write it by adding a vowel character. Length changes meaning just as much as the small っ does.

| Spelling | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| おかあさん | okāsan | mother |
| おにいさん | onīsan | older brother |
| すうがく | sūgaku | mathematics |
| おねえさん | onēsan | older sister |
| おおきい | ōkii | big |

Two spellings catch everyone. A long *e* is usually written えい, not ええ: せんせい (*sensei*, teacher) is pronounced close to *sensē*. A long *o* is usually written おう: ありがとう (*arigatō*, thank you) and がっこう (*gakkō*). A small number of words use おお instead, such as おおきい (*ōkii*, big) and とおい (*tōi*, far), and those have to be learned one by one.

The classic pair to practice is おばさん (*obasan*, aunt or middle-aged woman) and おばあさん (*obāsan*, grandmother or elderly woman). Hold the vowel for the wrong length and you have just aged someone by 30 years. Spelling length is the easy half; hearing it at full speed takes practice, and [section 2.3](../02-sounds-of-japanese/03-long-vowels-and-double-consonants.md) shows how to train it.

## Mnemonics That Stick

A picture hook for each shape speeds up memorization a lot. The table gives some that learners find memorable; better still, invent your own, the sillier the better.

Mnemonics have a long research record. In a classic experiment, Richard Atkinson and Michael Raugh (1975) taught American students 120 Russian words; students who linked each word to an English keyword and a vivid image recalled 72% of them on a final test, against 46% for students who studied without keywords, and still led six weeks later (43% against 28%). That study was about vocabulary, not scripts, but picture hooks for kana apply the same principle: tie a new, meaningless shape to something you already know. A mnemonic gets a character into memory; recalling it again and again, as the two-week plan below explains, keeps it there.

| Character | Mnemonic |
|---|---|
| あ | Someone throwing their arms up and shouting "Ah!" |
| き | An old-fashioned key |
| し | A fishhook: "she caught a fish" |
| す | A swing, hanging from its loop |
| つ | A tsunami wave rolling to the left |
| ね | A cat (ねこ, *neko*) curled up with its tail looped at the end |
| ぬ | A bowl of noodles with chopsticks: *nu*-dles |
| め | An eye; め (目) is the Japanese word for "eye" |
| む | A cow's face saying "moo" |
| ろ | A 3 rolling along the road |

Some pairs look alike and need deliberate attention. These are the usual suspects:

| Pair | How to tell them apart |
|---|---|
| ぬ / め | ぬ (*nu*) has a small loop at the end; め (*me*) does not |
| ね / れ / わ | All share a vertical stroke: ね (*ne*) ends in a loop, れ (*re*) in an outward kick, わ (*wa*) in a rounded curve |
| る / ろ | る (*ru*) ends in a loop; ろ (*ro*) is open |
| さ / ち | Mirror images: さ (*sa*) opens to the left, ち (*chi*) to the right |
| は / ほ | ほ (*ho*) has an extra horizontal line at the top of the right side |
| い / り | い (*i*) is two short strokes side by side; り (*ri*) has a longer right stroke |

> **Tip:** Visual mnemonic sets such as [Dr. Moku](https://drmoku.com) and the free illustrated charts at [Tofugu's Learn Hiragana](https://www.tofugu.com/japanese/learn-hiragana/) turn each character into a picture. Most learners find pictures stick far faster than rote copying.

## Stroke Order

Even if you mostly type, learn to write each character with the right **stroke order**: the conventional sequence and direction of the strokes. Stroke order is not a tradition for its own sake. It produces the proportions and flow that make handwriting legible, it helps you tell similar characters apart, and dictionaries and handwriting input on phones expect it. Learn it correctly from the start and it becomes automatic.

### The Seven Rules

The same handful of principles governs hiragana, katakana and kanji. Hiragana uses mainly the first three; the rest matter most for kanji.

| Rule | What it means |
|---|---|
| 1. Top to bottom | Upper strokes come before lower ones |
| 2. Left to right | Left strokes come before right ones |
| 3. Horizontal before vertical | Where lines cross, the horizontal usually comes first |
| 4. Right-falling after left-falling | A stroke sweeping down to the left comes before one sweeping down to the right |
| 5. Center before sides | In symmetrical shapes, the middle stroke comes first |
| 6. Frame, contents, then close | Draw an enclosing frame, fill it, then seal the bottom |
| 7. One stroke means one movement | A stroke is everything drawn without lifting the pen, however many times it turns |

Rule 7 trips up most beginners. In hiragana a single stroke can curve, loop and change direction several times, and lifting the pen halfway through is the most common mistake.

The best practice routine is simple: watch an animation of the character once, write it 10 times while saying the sound aloud, then write it again from memory the next day.

### The Vowels

| Character | Strokes | Order |
|---|---|---|
| あ | 3 | (1) Horizontal; (2) a long, slightly curved vertical crossing it; (3) the large loop, starting with a short diagonal and swinging round to the right |
| い | 2 | (1) Left stroke, curving down and flicking up at the end; (2) a shorter right stroke |
| う | 2 | (1) A short dash at the top; (2) a curve that goes right, then down and back to the left |
| え | 2 | (1) A short dash at the top; (2) a zigzag: right, down to the left, then a wavy tail to the right |
| お | 3 | (1) Horizontal; (2) a vertical that turns into a loop and a wide curve to the right; (3) a small dot at the top right |

### The *K* to *H* Rows

| Character | Strokes | Order |
|---|---|---|
| か | 3 | (1) Horizontal that bends down into a hook; (2) a diagonal crossing it, down to the left; (3) a separate dot on the right |
| き | 4 | (1, 2) Two horizontals; (3) a long diagonal down to the right crossing both; (4) the separate curve at the bottom |
| く | 1 | One stroke: down to the left, then down to the right, without lifting the pen |
| け | 3 | (1) Left vertical with a small hook; (2) horizontal; (3) a long vertical sweeping down to the left |
| こ | 2 | (1) Top horizontal with a small hook; (2) the lower curve |
| さ | 3 | (1) Horizontal; (2) a diagonal crossing it; (3) the separate curve at the bottom |
| し | 1 | Straight down, then a curve up to the right, in one movement |
| す | 2 | (1) Horizontal; (2) a vertical crossing it that makes a loop and continues down |
| せ | 3 | (1) Long horizontal; (2) right vertical with a small hook; (3) left vertical that turns right along the bottom |
| そ | 1 | A zigzag that ends in a curve; some fonts split the top, but handwritten it is one stroke |
| た | 4 | (1) Horizontal; (2) a diagonal down to the left; (3, 4) the two short strokes on the right, upper then lower |
| ち | 2 | (1) Horizontal; (2) a diagonal that turns into a curve bulging to the right |
| つ | 1 | Right, then a curve down and back to the left |
| て | 1 | Right, then a curve down and back, like a backwards C |
| と | 2 | (1) A short diagonal tick; (2) a curve down to the left that sweeps back right |
| な | 4 | (1) Horizontal; (2) a diagonal down to the left; (3) a dot at the right; (4) a vertical ending in a loop |
| に | 3 | (1) Left vertical with a small hook; (2) upper short horizontal; (3) lower horizontal |
| ぬ | 2 | (1) A short diagonal; (2) a long stroke that crosses it, swings round and ends in a small loop |
| ね | 2 | (1) Vertical; (2) a zigzag that crosses it and ends in a loop |
| の | 1 | Start in the middle, go down to the left, then circle up and over to the right without closing |
| は | 3 | (1) Left vertical; (2) horizontal; (3) a vertical that ends in a loop |
| ひ | 1 | One movement: a small rise, a deep U-shaped curve, then a flick upward |
| ふ | 4 | (1) A small top stroke; (2) the central curve; (3) the left dot; (4) the right dot |
| へ | 1 | A short rise, then a long fall to the right; almost identical to katakana ヘ |
| ほ | 4 | (1) Left vertical; (2, 3) two horizontals on the right; (4) a vertical ending in a loop |

### The *M* Row to ん

| Character | Strokes | Order |
|---|---|---|
| ま | 3 | (1, 2) Two horizontals; (3) a vertical crossing both and ending in a loop |
| み | 2 | (1) A zigzag with a loop; (2) a short curved stroke on the right |
| む | 3 | (1) Horizontal; (2) a vertical that loops and curves up to the right; (3) a dot at the right |
| め | 2 | (1) A short diagonal; (2) a long stroke that crosses it and curves round, with no loop |
| も | 3 | (1) The hooked vertical, like a し; (2, 3) the two horizontals crossing it |
| や | 3 | (1) A curved stroke, right then down; (2) a short dot above it; (3) a long diagonal down to the left |
| ゆ | 2 | (1) A vertical that loops round to the right; (2) a curved vertical through the middle |
| よ | 2 | (1) Short horizontal; (2) a vertical ending in a loop |
| ら | 2 | (1) A short dot at the top; (2) a vertical that curves round to the left |
| り | 2 | (1) Short left stroke flicking up; (2) a longer right stroke curving down to the left |
| る | 1 | Right, a sharp zigzag down, then a curve ending in a small loop |
| れ | 2 | (1) Vertical; (2) a zigzag that crosses it and kicks outward at the end |
| ろ | 1 | Like る without the loop: it ends open |
| わ | 2 | (1) Vertical; (2) a zigzag that crosses it and ends in a rounded curve |
| を | 3 | (1) Horizontal; (2) a diagonal that curves back; (3) a final curve below |
| ん | 1 | Down to the left, then up and down again, ending with a flick to the right |

> **Warning:** く, し, つ, て, の, ひ, へ, る, ろ and ん are each one stroke, however complicated they look. Writing them in two or more pieces is the most common beginner error, and it shows.

### Watching the Strokes

Words describe strokes; animations show them. These free tools let you watch any character being written:

| Resource | What it offers |
|---|---|
| [Jisho](https://jisho.org) | Search any kana or kanji and view its stroke order diagram |
| [Kanji alive](https://kanjialive.com) | Animated stroke order with audio, mainly for kanji |
| [Tofugu's Learn Hiragana](https://www.tofugu.com/japanese/learn-hiragana/) | Illustrated mnemonics and stroke order for every character |
| [Kakijun](https://kakijun.jp) | A Japanese-language stroke order reference |

A routine that works: watch the animation once, write the character 10 times from memory, check it, and repeat over three days.

## A Two-Week Plan

**Week one: learn the shapes.** Spread the chart over the week so each row gets a day or two of attention:

- Days 1–2: the vowels and the *k* row (あいうえお、かきくけこ)
- Days 3–4: the *s* and *t* rows (さしすせそ、たちつてと)
- Days 5–6: the *n* and *h* rows (なにぬねの、はひふへほ)
- Day 7: the *m*, *y*, *r* and *w* rows and ん

Why spread the rows out and keep coming back to old ones? Two of the best-supported findings in memory research: reviews spaced over several days beat the same amount of cramming (Cepeda et al., 2006), and recalling a character before you check it beats looking at it again (Karpicke and Roediger, 2008). [Section 1.2](../01-how-to-learn-japanese/02-study-and-immersion.md) explains both. In practice, cover the romanization and say each character before you look, every day, including the rows you learned earlier in the week. Flashcards and quiz apps do this for you.

**Week two: read.** As soon as you know the characters, stop leaning on romanization. Going cold turkey is uncomfortable for about a week, and then it clicks. Good reading material at this stage includes picture books for children (絵本, *ehon*), [NHK News Web Easy](https://www3.nhk.or.jp/news/easy/), which adds furigana to every kanji, and anime aimed at young children with Japanese subtitles turned on.

Useful tools along the way:

| Tool | Best for |
|---|---|
| [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net) (free on desktop and Android) | Flashcard drilling with spaced repetition |
| [Dr. Moku](https://drmoku.com) | Visual mnemonic charts |
| [Tofugu's Learn Hiragana](https://www.tofugu.com/japanese/learn-hiragana/) | Free online mnemonics |
| [Genki I](https://genki.japantimes.co.jp/en/) | A widely used textbook that teaches hiragana in its first chapter |

## Summary

- Hiragana is a phonetic script of 46 basic characters, each standing for a syllable built on one of five unchanging vowels (*a, i, u, e, o*), plus ん.
- Dakuten (゛) voice a consonant (か *ka* to が *ga*) and handakuten (゜) turn the *h* row into *p* (は *ha* to ぱ *pa*).
- A small ゃ, ゅ or ょ after an *i*-column character makes one combined syllable (yōon), and a small っ doubles the following consonant; size changes the word.
- Long vowels are written by adding a vowel (おかあさん *okāsan*), with long *e* usually spelled えい and long *o* usually spelled おう.
- As particles, は is read *wa*, へ is read *e* and を is read *o*.
- Correct stroke order makes writing legible and helps you tell similar characters apart; many hiragana that look complex, such as く, し, の and ん, are a single stroke.
