# Chapter 3 — The Writing System

Japanese is famous for having three writing systems at once, and learners often hear that as a warning. It is better heard as a promise. Each script has a clear job: hiragana writes grammar and native words, katakana writes words borrowed from abroad, and kanji carry meaning. Once you see the division of labor, a line of Japanese stops being a wall of symbols and becomes a sentence with a visible structure.

The first two scripts are surprisingly quick to learn. Hiragana and katakana each have 46 basic characters, every one standing for a single, regular syllable, and many learners can read both within a month. That is the moment Japanese opens up: you can sound out signs, menus and the furigana printed beside kanji, and every later chapter can drop the training wheels of romanization.

Kanji is the long road, and nobody pretends otherwise. But it is far more logical than it first looks. Kanji are built from a limited set of recurring components, their readings follow patterns, and there are well-tested methods for learning them steadily. This chapter gives you the map and the tools to start the journey, and a few landmarks you will spot the moment you arrive in Japan.

## In This Chapter

- Read and write all 46 basic hiragana, with the dakuten and handakuten marks that turn them into more than 70 sounds
- Handle the spelling details that change meaning: small ゃゅょ combinations, the small っ and long vowels
- Read は, へ and を correctly when they work as particles
- Read and write katakana, including the long vowel mark ー and the extra combinations used for foreign sounds
- Decode katakana loanwords, and spot the false friends that only look like English
- Use radicals to make sense of kanji, and tell on-yomi from kun-yomi
- Follow the rules of stroke order for kana and kanji
- Choose a kanji study method and set up a daily spaced repetition habit with WaniKani or Anki
