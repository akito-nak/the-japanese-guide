# Trains and Transit — The Network That Runs on Time

You step off the plane, follow the signs to the railway and find a wall of ticket machines, a forest of colored lines on a map and a gate that beeps at everyone except you. Ten minutes later a train glides in to the second, its doors line up exactly with the painted marks on the platform, and a calm recorded voice tells you where it is going. Japan moves by rail, and once you can read the system it becomes one of the great pleasures of being there.

The railway is also a superb classroom. Station signs, announcements and ticket machines repeat the same few dozen words thousands of times a day, so the vocabulary on this page is some of the fastest-paying Japanese you will ever learn.

## The Main Types of Rail and Road Transport

Japan's network is run by dozens of companies rather than one national operator, which is why a single journey can cross two or three ticket gates. The table sorts them into the categories you will meet.

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| JR | jei āru | Japan Railways group | Six regional passenger companies; run the Shinkansen and many city lines |
| 私鉄 | shitetsu | Private railway | Company-owned lines; Tokyo and Osaka each have several large ones |
| 地下鉄 | chikatetsu | Subway | Tokyo Metro, Toei, Osaka Metro and others |
| 新幹線 | shinkansen | Bullet train | High-speed intercity lines |
| モノレール | monorēru | Monorail | Airport links and some urban routes |
| バス | basu | Bus | Fills the gaps, and essential in rural areas |

## IC Cards: The Card That Does Everything

An **IC card** (ICカード, *ai shī kādo*) is a rechargeable contactless card that you tap at the ticket gate instead of buying a ticket. The fare is worked out and deducted when you tap out at your destination, so you never need to read the fare chart above the ticket machines.

Ten major cards have been interoperable since 2013, so a card bought in one region works on trains, subways and most city buses in the others. The ones you are most likely to meet are below.

| Card | Issued by | Home region |
|---|---|---|
| Suica (スイカ) | JR East | Tokyo and eastern Japan |
| PASMO (パスモ) | Private railways and subways | Tokyo |
| ICOCA (イコカ) | JR West | Osaka, Kyoto and western Japan |
| TOICA (トイカ) | JR Central | Nagoya area (JR lines) |
| manaca (マナカ) | Nagoya subway and private lines | Nagoya area |
| Kitaca, SUGOCA, nimoca, はやかけん | JR Hokkaido, JR Kyushu, Nishitetsu, Fukuoka subway | Hokkaido and Fukuoka areas |

As of 2026, visitors have three easy options. Suica, PASMO and ICOCA can be added to Apple Wallet on an iPhone bought anywhere. JR East's Welcome Suica Mobile app (launched in March 2025) sets up a visitor's Suica in Apple Wallet before you fly. Visitors without an iPhone can buy a physical Welcome Suica, which has no deposit and expires after 28 days, at airport and major station counters. Most Android phones sold outside Japan lack the FeliCa chip that Japanese gates use, so mobile IC cards generally don't work on them.

> **Warning:** IC cards can't always carry you across the boundary between two companies' card areas in a single trip (from the Tokyo area into the Nagoya area, for example), and some rural lines don't accept them at all. For long journeys, buy a proper ticket.

Using a card follows the same rhythm everywhere:

1. Tap the card on the reader at the entrance gate (改札, *kaisatsu*). The gate flashes and lets you through.
2. Tap again at the exit gate. The fare is deducted and the remaining balance is shown.
3. If your balance is too low to exit, the gate closes. Top up at the 乗り越し精算機 (*norikoshi seisanki*, fare adjustment machine) beside the exit gates.

The same card pays at convenience stores, vending machines, station lockers and many shops, which is why people carry one even when they rarely ride the train.

## Train Vocabulary

These words appear on every departure board and in every announcement. Learn the train types first: boarding a 快速 when you wanted to stop at a small station is a rite of passage, but once is enough.

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 駅 | eki | Station |
| 改札 | kaisatsu | Ticket gate |
| ホーム | hōmu | Platform |
| 番線 | bansen | Track number (3番線, track 3) |
| 電車 | densha | Train (electric) |
| 路線 | rosen | Line |
| 〜行き | -iki / -yuki | Bound for ~ |
| 乗り換え | norikae | Transfer |
| 各駅停車 / 普通 | kakueki teisha / futsū | Local (stops at every station) |
| 快速 | kaisoku | Rapid (skips some stations) |
| 急行 | kyūkō | Express |
| 特急 | tokkyū | Limited express (often needs an extra ticket) |
| 始発 | shihatsu | First train of the day |
| 終電 | shūden | Last train of the night |
| 回送 | kaisō | Not in service |
| 遅延 | chien | Delay |
| 振替輸送 | furikae yusō | Alternative transport during a disruption |
| 指定席 | shiteiseki | Reserved seat |
| 自由席 | jiyūseki | Non-reserved seat |
| グリーン車 | gurīnsha | Green Car (first class) |
| 人身事故 | jinshin jiko | Accident involving a person on the tracks; the line stops |

> **Note:** The exact speed ranking of 快速, 急行 and 特急 varies by company, so on an unfamiliar line check the list of stops on the platform display rather than trusting the name alone.

## Reading a Train Map

Station names are written in kanji, and on a crowded map that can feel like reading a phone book in the dark. Help is built in: stations in Tokyo, Osaka and many other cities carry a **station number**, a line letter plus a number (JY01 is Tokyo station on the Yamanote line, G03 a station on the Ginza subway line). Match the number on the map to the number on the platform and you can navigate before you can read a single name.

Every journey needs three pieces of information, and signs present them in this order: the line (路線名, *rosenmei*), the destination station (駅名, *ekimei*) and the platform (番線). Learning the kanji for the stations you use every day is still worth it; they become your first real-world kanji.

The Tokyo lines below are the ones most visitors ride first.

| Line | Notable stations | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 山手線 (Yamanote) | Tokyo, Shinjuku, Shibuya, Harajuku, Akihabara | The light-green loop that links the major hubs |
| 中央線 (Chūō) | Tokyo, Shinjuku, Kichijoji | Orange; runs west across the city |
| 東海道線 (Tōkaidō) | Tokyo, Shinagawa, Yokohama | Southwest toward Yokohama |
| 総武線 (Sōbu) | Akihabara, Ochanomizu, Shinjuku | Yellow local trains running east-west |
| 丸ノ内線 (Marunouchi) | Shinjuku, Tokyo, Ikebukuro | Red Tokyo Metro line |

Shibuya, Shinjuku and Ikebukuro are also terminals for private railways that fan out into the suburbs, which is why those stations are so enormous.

## Platform and Train Announcements

Announcements are formal and formulaic, which makes them easy to learn: the same template is used with different station names. The verb 参ります (*mairimasu*) is the humble form of 来る, "to come", one of the keigo forms from [section 3.4](../03-politeness/04-honorific-japanese.md) in daily use.

On the platform you will hear:

> 三番線に新宿行きの快速が参ります。黄色い線の内側までお下がりください。\
> *Sanbansen ni Shinjuku-iki no kaisoku ga mairimasu. Kiiroi sen no uchigawa made o-sagari kudasai.*\
> A rapid train bound for Shinjuku is arriving on track 3. Please stand back behind the yellow line.

> まもなく2番線に電車が参ります。\
> *Mamonaku nibansen ni densha ga mairimasu.*\
> A train will shortly arrive on track 2.

> ドアが閉まります。ご注意ください。\
> *Doa ga shimarimasu. Go-chūi kudasai.*\
> The doors are closing. Please be careful.

On the train itself, the key phrases are these:

> 次は渋谷、渋谷です。\
> *Tsugi wa Shibuya, Shibuya desu.*\
> The next station is Shibuya, Shibuya.

> この電車は東京行きです。\
> *Kono densha wa Tōkyō-iki desu.*\
> This train is bound for Tokyo.

> まもなく横浜に到着します。\
> *Mamonaku Yokohama ni tōchaku shimasu.*\
> We will shortly arrive at Yokohama.

> 終点、大宮です。お忘れ物のないようご注意ください。\
> *Shūten, Ōmiya desu. O-wasuremono no nai yō go-chūi kudasai.*\
> This is the final stop, Omiya. Please make sure you have all your belongings.

Transfer information starts with お乗り換えの方は (*o-norikae no kata wa*, "passengers changing trains…"), followed by the lines you can change to. On major lines the announcement is repeated in English, but catching the Japanese first is excellent listening practice.

## The Shinkansen

The **Shinkansen** (新幹線, "new trunk line") is Japan's high-speed rail network, opened in 1964 between Tokyo and Osaka. It is fast, smooth and famously punctual, and the lines below cover most of the country as of 2026.

| Line | Route | Top speed |
|---|---|---|
| 東海道新幹線 (Tōkaidō) | Tokyo to Shin-Osaka via Nagoya and Kyoto | 285 km/h |
| 山陽新幹線 (San'yō) | Shin-Osaka to Hakata (Fukuoka) via Hiroshima | 300 km/h |
| 東北新幹線 (Tōhoku) | Tokyo to Shin-Aomori via Sendai and Morioka | 320 km/h |
| 上越新幹線 (Jōetsu) | Tokyo to Niigata | 275 km/h |
| 北陸新幹線 (Hokuriku) | Tokyo to Tsuruga via Nagano, Kanazawa and Fukui | 260 km/h |
| 九州新幹線 (Kyūshū) | Hakata to Kagoshima-Chūō | 260 km/h |
| 北海道新幹線 (Hokkaidō) | Shin-Aomori to Shin-Hakodate-Hokuto | 260 km/h |

The Hokkaido line's extension to Sapporo is under construction and is not expected to open until well into the 2030s.

On the Tōkaidō line, three services share the tracks, and their names are worth knowing because tickets and passes treat them differently.

| Name | Meaning | Stops |
|---|---|---|
| のぞみ (Nozomi) | "Hope" | Fewest; Tokyo to Shin-Osaka in about two and a half hours |
| ひかり (Hikari) | "Light" | A few more stops |
| こだま (Kodama) | "Echo" | Every station |

You can buy tickets at a 自動券売機 (*jidō kenbaiki*, ticket machine), at the **midori no madoguchi** (みどりの窓口, "green window"), the staffed JR ticket office, or online through JR's apps, such as SmartEX for the Tōkaidō, San'yō and Kyūshū lines.

The **Japan Rail Pass** is a pass for foreign visitors that gives unlimited travel on most JR trains for seven, 14 or 21 days. After a large price rise in October 2023 and another on October 1, 2026, the ordinary seven-day pass costs ¥53,000 through overseas agencies. Bought through the official online service, it stays at ¥50,000 for a limited time, according to the JR Group's [April 2026 price notice](https://global.jr-central.co.jp/en/news/_pdf/2026/0001.pdf) (as of October 2026). It no longer pays for itself on a simple Tokyo–Kyoto round trip, so add up your actual fares first; regional JR passes are often better value. Nozomi and Mizuho trains need an extra supplement ticket on top of the pass.

## The Last Train

Most city lines stop running between midnight and about 1 a.m., and the **shūden** (終電, last train) shapes Japanese nightlife. Plans are made around it, friends drift away from the table at 11:30, and 終電を逃す (*shūden o nogasu*, to miss the last train) is a phrase every office worker knows too well.

If you miss it, a taxi is the obvious answer, but taxi fares carry a surcharge of about 20% late at night. Many people wait for the 始発 instead: in a 24-hour karaoke box, a family restaurant or a 漫画喫茶 (*manga kissa*, manga and internet café), which rents out small private booths by the hour. Check last-train times in a route app before the evening starts.

## Etiquette on Trains

Japanese train etiquette is quietly strict. Nobody will lecture you, but the carriage notices everything, so the table is worth taking seriously.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Line up at the marks on the platform | Eat on crowded commuter trains (the Shinkansen is fine) |
| Let passengers off before boarding | Take phone calls (send a message, or step off at a station) |
| Offer priority seats (優先席, *yūsenseki*) to those who need them | Put your bag on a seat when the train is busy |
| Put your phone on silent (マナーモード, *manā mōdo*) | Play audio without headphones |
| Wear your backpack on your front in a crowd | Push to get on or off |

Many urban lines have 女性専用車両 (*josei sen'yō sharyō*, women-only cars) during rush hours, marked in pink on the platform and the carriage. Men are expected to use other cars, and staff will steer anyone who wanders in toward the next carriage.

Tokyo's morning rush, roughly 7:30 to 9:30 on the major lines, is intense. A few of the busiest stations have long used platform staff, nicknamed 押し屋 (*oshiya*, pushers), to help the last passengers squeeze aboard so the doors can close. Everyone finds it uncomfortable and everyone puts up with it, which says a good deal about Japanese social life.

## Buses

Buses matter more the farther you go from the big cities, and payment varies by region. In much of central Tokyo you board at the front and pay a flat fare (均一料金, *kin'itsu ryōkin*) as you get on. Elsewhere you board at the middle or rear door, take a numbered ticket called a 整理券 (*seiriken*), and pay as you get off according to the fare shown next to your number on the display at the front. IC cards work on most city buses; tap on and off at the readers.

> 整理券をお取りください。\
> *Seiriken o o-tori kudasai.*\
> Please take a numbered ticket.

> 五番の方はいくらですか。\
> *Goban no kata wa ikura desu ka.*\
> How much is it for ticket number 5?

Press the stop button (降車ボタン, *kōsha botan*) before your stop. At some rural request stops the driver will only pull in if you raise a hand (手を挙げる, *te o ageru*) as the bus approaches.

## Taxis

Japanese taxis are spotless, honest and not cheap. A red 空車 (*kūsha*, vacant) sign behind the windshield means the cab is free; 賃走 (*chinsō*, on a fare) means it is taken, and 迎車 (*geisha*, picking up) means it is on its way to a booked customer. The rear left door is opened and closed by the driver with a lever, so step back and let it swing.

> 東京駅までお願いします。\
> *Tōkyō-eki made onegai shimasu.*\
> To Tokyo station, please.

> ここで降ります。\
> *Koko de orimasu.*\
> I'll get off here.

> 領収書をください。\
> *Ryōshūsho o kudasai.*\
> A receipt, please.

Private ride-hailing is tightly restricted in Japan. A limited "Japan-style ride-share" scheme, run through taxi companies, began in April 2024, but as of 2026 apps such as GO and Uber are used mainly to call licensed taxis. Have your destination written in Japanese; drivers know their city well but may not read a Romanized address.

## Asking the Way

Route apps make Japan easy to navigate: Google Maps handles transit well, and the Japanese favorites Yahoo!乗換案内 (*Yahoo! Norikae Annai*), NAVITIME and Jorudan's Japan Transit Planner show platforms, fares and last trains. Sooner or later, though, you will want to ask a person, and these four questions cover most situations.

> 改札はどこですか。\
> *Kaisatsu wa doko desu ka.*\
> Where is the ticket gate?

> 浅草に行くには、どの電車に乗ればいいですか。\
> *Asakusa ni iku ni wa, dono densha ni noreba ii desu ka.*\
> Which train should I take to get to Asakusa?

> この電車は品川に止まりますか。\
> *Kono densha wa Shinagawa ni tomarimasu ka.*\
> Does this train stop at Shinagawa?

> 乗り換えは何番線ですか。\
> *Norikae wa nanbansen desu ka.*\
> Which track do I transfer to?

## Summary

- Japan's rail network is run by many companies (JR, private railways and subways), so one journey can involve more than one gate and fare.
- An IC card such as Suica, PASMO or ICOCA pays fares and small purchases nationwide; visitors can use Welcome Suica on a physical card or in Apple Wallet (as of 2026).
- Train types run from 各駅停車 (all stops) through 快速 and 急行 to 特急, and checking the stop list prevents most wrong-train mistakes.
- Announcements follow fixed templates such as 次は〜です and 〜番線に〜行きが参ります, which makes them excellent listening practice.
- The Shinkansen links most major cities; the Japan Rail Pass (¥50,000 for seven days online, ¥53,000 through agencies, as of October 2026) excludes Nozomi without a supplement and is worth it only for several long trips.
- Plan evenings around the 終電, keep quiet on trains, and let the taxi driver open the door.
