# Shopping and Money — Life in a Shopper's Paradise

The price tag says 3,980円. You hand over a ¥5,000 note, the cashier counts your change back to you coin by coin, places it on a little tray, adds the receipt, bows and asks whether you have a point card. You understood none of it, but you got the onigiri. A few words of shopping Japanese turn that small blur of ritual into a conversation, and few places reward the effort like Japan's shops.

Shopping in Japan runs from the 100-yen shop, where almost everything costs ¥110 with tax, to seven-floor department stores and back-alley vintage dealers. The prices on this page are as of 2026.

## Money Basics

Japan's currency is the yen (円, *en*; the English "yen" comes from an older spelling). Cash is still common, but it is no longer king: by the government's count, about 58% of consumer spending in 2025 was cashless, mostly by credit card.

### Coins and Notes

New banknotes entered circulation in July 2024, and the older designs remain legal tender, so you will see both.

| Japanese | Reading | Value | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1円 | ichi-en | ¥1 | Aluminum, light enough to float on water |
| 5円 | go-en | ¥5 | Brass with a hole; a favorite shrine offering because ご縁 (*go-en*) also means "a bond" |
| 10円 | jū-en | ¥10 | Bronze, with the Phoenix Hall of Byōdō-in |
| 50円 | gojū-en | ¥50 | Silver-colored, with a hole |
| 100円 | hyaku-en | ¥100 | The everyday coin |
| 500円 | gohyaku-en | ¥500 | Two-tone design since 2021; the largest coin |
| 千円 | sen-en | ¥1,000 | Note: Kitasato Shibasaburō (new), Noguchi Hideyo (old) |
| 五千円 | gosen-en | ¥5,000 | Note: Tsuda Umeko (new), Higuchi Ichiyō (old) |
| 一万円 | ichiman-en | ¥10,000 | Note: Shibusawa Eiichi (new), Fukuzawa Yukichi (old) |

A ¥2,000 note also exists, but you are more likely to see one in a museum case than in your change.

### Counting in Ten-Thousands

Japanese counts large numbers in units of 万 (*man*, ten thousand), not thousands. ¥30,000 is 三万円 (*sanman-en*, "three ten-thousand yen"), and ¥150,000 is 十五万円 (*jūgoman-en*). Until this becomes automatic, mentally move the comma: put it four digits from the right instead of three, and the Japanese number falls out.

> この時計は二万五千円です。\
> *Kono tokei wa niman gosen-en desu.*\
> This watch is 25,000 yen.

Since 2021 shops must display the tax-inclusive total (税込, *zeikomi*), though many also show the pre-tax price (税抜, *zeinuki*) in larger type. Consumption tax (消費税, *shōhizei*) is 10%, or 8% for food and non-alcoholic drinks you take away.

### ATMs and Cashless Payment

Foreign cards work reliably at 7-Eleven's Seven Bank ATMs and at Japan Post Bank (ゆうちょ) ATMs in post offices, and at most ATMs in Lawson and FamilyMart stores. Many ordinary bank ATMs still reject foreign cards.

| Method | Examples | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| IC card | Suica, PASMO, ICOCA | Konbini, vending machines, many shops |
| QR code | PayPay, Rakuten Pay, d払い, au PAY | Very common since 2018; mostly need a Japanese account |
| Credit card | Visa, Mastercard, JCB, Amex | Widely accepted in cities |
| Contactless | Apple Pay, Google Pay, tap-to-pay cards | At most chain stores |

> **Tip:** Small restaurants, temples, rural shops and festival stalls may still be cash only. Carry some yen, and look for the sticker by the register, or ask カードは使えますか (*kādo wa tsukaemasu ka*, can I use a card?).

## Shopping Vocabulary

These phrases cover most transactions. Shop staff speak in polished keigo ([section 3.4](../03-politeness/04-honorific-japanese.md)), but your plain です and ます forms are exactly right as a customer.

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| いくらですか | ikura desu ka | How much is it? |
| 〜はありますか | ~ wa arimasu ka | Do you have ~? |
| 試着してもいいですか | shichaku shite mo ii desu ka | May I try this on? |
| 見ているだけです | mite iru dake desu | I'm just looking |
| レジはどこですか | reji wa doko desu ka | Where is the checkout? |
| 袋はいりません | fukuro wa irimasen | I don't need a bag |
| 領収書をください | ryōshūsho o kudasai | A receipt, please |
| カードで払えますか | kādo de haraemasu ka | Can I pay by card? |
| 現金でお願いします | genkin de onegai shimasu | Cash, please |
| 税込みですか | zeikomi desu ka | Does that include tax? |
| 返品できますか | henpin dekimasu ka | Can I return this? |

And these are the questions the cashier will ask you:

> ポイントカードはお持ちですか。\
> *Pointo kādo wa o-mochi desu ka.*\
> Do you have a point card?

> 袋はご利用ですか。\
> *Fukuro wa go-riyō desu ka.*\
> Would you like a bag?

Since July 2020 shops charge a few yen for plastic bags, which is why the bag question comes up at every checkout. The easy answers are 大丈夫です (*daijōbu desu*, I'm fine, no thanks) or はい、お願いします.

## Types of Stores

### Konbini

The **konbini** (コンビニ, convenience store) is the backbone of daily life. Most are open 24 hours, and they do far more than sell snacks:

- Hot food: 肉まん (*nikuman*, steamed meat buns), おでん (*oden*), 唐揚げ (*karaage*)
- Onigiri, sandwiches, bento and desserts that are genuinely good
- Drinks, including alcohol
- Toiletries, stationery, underwear and phone chargers
- ATMs, photocopying and printing, bill payment
- Concert and event tickets
- Parcel shipping (宅急便, *takkyūbin*)

| Chain | Known for |
|---|---|
| セブン-イレブン (7-Eleven) | The largest chain, strong ready-made food, Seven Bank ATMs |
| ファミリーマート (FamilyMart) | Hot snacks such as Famichiki fried chicken |
| ローソン (Lawson) | Desserts and bakery; also runs the separate Lawson Store 100 chain |

Konbini food gets a closer look in [section 7.2](../07-customs-and-daily-life/02-food.md).

### Supermarkets

For everyday groceries, people go to the スーパー (*sūpā*): Aeon (イオン), Life (ライフ), Seiyu (西友) and many regional chains. In the evening, look for stickers marked 割引 (*waribiki*, discount) or 半額 (*hangaku*, half price) on fresh food and bento as closing time approaches.

### Department Stores

The デパート (*depāto*) offers floor after floor of fashion and homeware, immaculate service and prices to match: Isetan (伊勢丹), Takashimaya (高島屋), Mitsukoshi (三越) and Daimaru (大丸) are the big names. The real treasure is underground. The **depachika** (デパ地下, "department-store basement") is a food hall of sweets, prepared dishes and beautifully boxed gifts, and the free samples alone make it worth a visit.

### 100-Yen Shops

The **hyaku-en shop** (100円ショップ, *hyaku-en shoppu*) sells most items for ¥100 before tax, with some lines at ¥300 or more. Quality is often startlingly good, especially for kitchenware, stationery, storage and seasonal decorations. Daiso (ダイソー), Seria (セリア) and Can Do (キャンドゥ) are the big chains, and their product labels make cheap, endless reading practice.

### Don Quijote

ドン・キホーテ (*Don Kihōte*), or ドンキ (*Donki*), is a discount store crammed floor to ceiling with electronics, cosmetics, snacks, costumes and luggage. Many branches stay open late or around the clock, and most offer tax-free shopping. Its jingle, which chants ドン、ドン、ドン、ドンキ over and over, will follow you home.

### Vintage and Secondhand

Japan's secondhand culture is world-class, because goods are well looked after. 古着屋 (*furugiya*, vintage clothing shops) cluster in Tokyo's Shimokitazawa and Osaka's アメリカ村 (*Amerika-mura*). BOOKOFF (ブックオフ) sells used books, manga, games and CDs nationwide, and its ¥110 shelves are a gold mine for graded reading.

## Tax-Free Shopping

**Menzei** (免税, tax exemption) lets visitors who are staying in Japan for less than six months recover the 10% consumption tax on goods they take home. The rules change on November 1, 2026, so check which system applies to your trip.

The basics are the same under both systems. Purchases at one participating store on one day must total at least ¥5,000 before tax, and you must show your passport at the time of purchase. Look for the 免税 or "Tax-Free" sign; electronics chains such as Yodobashi Camera (ヨドバシカメラ) and Bic Camera (ビックカメラ), department stores, drugstores and Don Quijote all take part.

| | Until October 31, 2026 | From November 1, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| At the store | Tax is removed at the register | You pay the full price, tax included |
| Consumables (food, cosmetics) | Sealed in a special bag; limit of ¥500,000 | No sealing; no separate category or limit |
| At departure | Customs may check your goods | Customs checks your goods at a kiosk before check-in, within 90 days of purchase |
| The refund | Not needed | Paid after customs confirms the export |

> 免税でお願いします。\
> *Menzei de onegai shimasu.*\
> Tax-free, please.

> **Warning:** Under the new system, the refund depends on carrying the goods out of Japan and completing the customs check before you check in your luggage. Allow extra time at the airport, and don't use up consumables bought tax-free before you leave.

## Electronics

Tokyo's Akihabara (秋葉原) is the famous electronics district, and Osaka's 日本橋 (*Nipponbashi*, nicknamed Den Den Town) and Nagoya's 大須 (*Ōsu*) have their own followings. The big chains are below.

| Chain | Notes |
|---|---|
| ヨドバシカメラ (Yodobashi Camera) | Huge stores, wide selection, generous points |
| ビックカメラ (Bic Camera) | Similar range; many stores near major stations |
| ヤマダ電機 (Yamada Denki) | Japan's largest electronics retailer |
| エディオン (EDION) | Strong in western Japan |
| ノジマ (Nojima) | Competitive chain around Tokyo |

With the weak yen of the mid-2020s, cameras, lenses and some gadgets can cost less than abroad, and used camera shops are superb. Check that the warranty is international and the device works on your home voltage: Japan runs at 100 volts.

## Drugstores

The ドラッグストア (*doraggu sutoa*, drugstore) sells cosmetics, over-the-counter medicine, snacks and, often, cheap groceries. Matsumoto Kiyoshi (マツモトキヨシ), Welcia (ウエルシア), Tsuruha (ツルハ) and Cosmos (コスモス) are the large chains. Visitors stock up on Japanese sunscreen and skincare, and on a few cult favorites:

- 龍角散 (*Ryūkakusan*): herbal throat lozenges and powder
- バンテリン (*Vantelin*): muscle and joint pain gel
- 正露丸 (*Seirogan*): a stomach remedy with an unforgettable smell

For prescription medicine and medical help, see [section 6.4](04-emergencies.md).

## Markets

Markets are the best place to see what is in season (旬, *shun*) and to practice asking これは何ですか.

| Market | Location | What to expect |
|---|---|---|
| 豊洲市場 (Toyosu Shijō) | Tokyo | The wholesale fish market; viewing decks over the early-morning tuna auction (the closest one by advance lottery) |
| 錦市場 (Nishiki Ichiba) | Kyoto | A covered arcade of pickles, sweets and street snacks |
| 黒門市場 (Kuromon Ichiba) | Osaka | Seafood and produce, "Osaka's kitchen" |
| アメ横 (Ameyoko) | Tokyo (Ueno) | Bustling open-air market street, packed before New Year |

## Bargaining

Prices in Japanese shops are fixed, and asking for a discount in an ordinary store causes polite confusion rather than a deal. There are a few exceptions:

- Flea markets (フリーマーケット, often フリマ, *furima*) and antique fairs, where gentle negotiation is normal
- Large electronics purchases, where staff may match a competitor's price or add points if you ask politely: もう少し安くなりませんか (*mō sukoshi yasuku narimasen ka*, could it be a little cheaper?)
- Outlet malls (アウトレット), where the discount is already in the price

## Seasonal Sales

Japanese retail runs on a calendar, and the big sales are predictable.

| Season | When | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 初売り (hatsuuri) and winter sales | From January 1 or 2 | New Year sales, with lucky bags |
| バレンタインデー | February 14 | Women traditionally give chocolate; men return the favor on White Day, March 14 |
| ゴールデンウィーク | Late April to early May | Holiday crowds and some sales |
| 夏のセール | July | Summer clearance |
| ブラックフライデー | November | Imported in the mid-2010s and now everywhere |

The star of the January sales is the **fukubukuro** (福袋, lucky bag): a sealed bag of goods sold at a fixed price, usually worth more than you pay, whose contents you discover only when you get home. For popular brands, people queue from before dawn, and many stores now sell them online or by lottery.

## Summary

- Japanese counts large sums in 万 (ten thousand): ¥30,000 is 三万円, and moving the comma four digits from the right makes reading prices easy.
- New banknotes entered circulation in July 2024 alongside the old ones; prices must be shown tax-inclusive (税込), with consumption tax at 10% (8% for take-away food).
- Japan is now majority cashless (about 58% of spending in 2025), but small shops and stalls may take cash only, and Seven Bank and Japan Post ATMs accept foreign cards.
- Konbini, depachika, 100-yen shops and drugstores are the places where daily life happens, and each has its own vocabulary.
- Visitors can shop tax-free (免税) on purchases of ¥5,000 or more per store per day; from November 1, 2026, the tax is paid first and refunded after a customs check at departure.
- Prices are fixed in ordinary shops, and January's 福袋 lucky bags are a national sport.
