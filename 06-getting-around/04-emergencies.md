# Emergencies — Phrases You Hope You Never Need

Your phone screeches with an alarm you have never heard before, and everyone in the café looks up at once. Or a friend slips on a temple step and can't put weight on their ankle, and the only person nearby speaks no English. Japan is one of the safest countries in the world, with low crime, excellent hospitals and public services that work. Emergencies still happen everywhere, and a handful of words, said or recognized at the right moment, can make a real difference.

Save this page to your phone, and practice the short phrases aloud until they come without thinking. You will almost certainly never need most of them, and that is exactly the point of learning them in advance.

> **Note:** This section describes Japan's emergency numbers, services and alert systems as of 2026, to help with the language. It isn't medical or safety advice: in an emergency, follow the instructions of the police, fire and medical services and of local officials.

## Emergency Numbers

Two numbers matter most, and they are worth memorizing today: 110 for the police and 119 for fire and ambulance.

| Number | Service | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| 110 (ひゃくとおばん, *hyaku-tō-ban*) | Police (警察, *keisatsu*) | Crime, traffic accidents, danger to someone |
| 119 (いちいちきゅう, *ichi-ichi-kyū*) | Fire and ambulance (消防・救急, *shōbō, kyūkyū*) | Fires and medical emergencies |
| 118 | Japan Coast Guard | Emergencies at sea |
| #9110 | Police consultation line | Non-urgent police matters |
| #7119 | Ambulance advice line (in many prefectures) | "Should I call an ambulance or go to a clinic?" |
| 050-3816-2787 | Japan Visitor Hotline (JNTO) | 24-hour help for visitors in English, Chinese and Korean |

Many 110 and 119 call centers can bring an interpreter onto the line, but availability varies, and the first words you hear will be in Japanese. When you dial 119, the operator's first question decides which vehicle is sent:

> 火事ですか。救急ですか。\
> *Kaji desu ka. Kyūkyū desu ka.*\
> Is it a fire, or a medical emergency?

> 救急です。\
> *Kyūkyū desu.*\
> It's a medical emergency.

The next question is almost always the address: 住所はどこですか (*jūsho wa doko desu ka*, what is the address?). Keep your accommodation's address written in Japanese in your phone, and if you are outside, read out the nearest landmark, shop name or the number on a utility pole. Calling an ambulance is free for genuine emergencies, although a few areas now charge a fee when the hospital judges the call was not urgent.

## Medical Emergencies

### Getting Help

These sentences are short on purpose. In an emergency, nobody needs your best keigo.

> 救急車を呼んでください！\
> *Kyūkyūsha o yonde kudasai!*\
> Please call an ambulance!

> 意識がありません。\
> *Ishiki ga arimasen.*\
> They are unconscious.

> 息ができません。\
> *Iki ga dekimasen.*\
> I can't breathe.

> 胸が痛いです。\
> *Mune ga itai desu.*\
> I have chest pain.

> 足に怪我をしました。\
> *Ashi ni kega o shimashita.*\
> I've injured my leg.

> えびにアレルギーがあります。\
> *Ebi ni arerugī ga arimasu.*\
> I'm allergic to shrimp.

> 血圧の薬を飲んでいます。\
> *Ketsuatsu no kusuri o nonde imasu.*\
> I take blood-pressure medication.

### At the Hospital or Clinic

Hospitals and clinics are organized by department (科, *-ka*), and the department names are written on the signs at the entrance. The table lists the ones you are most likely to need.

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 救急外来 | kyūkyū gairai | Emergency department |
| 内科 | naika | Internal medicine (general illness) |
| 外科 | geka | Surgery (injuries, wounds) |
| 整形外科 | seikei geka | Orthopedics (bones and joints) |
| 小児科 | shōnika | Pediatrics |
| 皮膚科 | hifuka | Dermatology |
| 眼科 | ganka | Ophthalmology (eyes) |
| 耳鼻咽喉科 | jibi inkōka | Ear, nose and throat |
| 歯科 | shika | Dentistry |
| 薬局 | yakkyoku | Pharmacy |
| 保険証 | hokenshō | Health insurance card |
| 処方箋 | shohōsen | Prescription |

Describing symptoms uses a small set of patterns: the body part plus が痛いです (it hurts), or a set phrase.

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ここが痛いです | koko ga itai desu | It hurts here (point to the spot) |
| 熱があります | netsu ga arimasu | I have a fever |
| 吐き気がします | hakike ga shimasu | I feel sick (nauseous) |
| 下痢をしています | geri o shite imasu | I have diarrhea |
| めまいがします | memai ga shimasu | I feel dizzy |
| 妊娠しています | ninshin shite imasu | I'm pregnant |
| 旅行保険に入っています | ryokō hoken ni haitte imasu | I have travel insurance |

Japan's healthcare is excellent, but visitors aren't covered by the national insurance scheme and pay the full cost of treatment, and some hospitals charge visitors more than residents. Travel insurance that covers medical treatment is strongly recommended, and the Japan Tourism Agency publishes a searchable list of hospitals that accept foreign patients.

## Crime and the Police

Violent crime against visitors is rare in Japan. The problems people actually meet are lost property, the occasional pickpocket in a crowd, overcharging in some nightlife districts and, on crowded trains, groping.

### Asking for Help

> 助けて！\
> *Tasukete!*\
> Help!

> 警察を呼んでください！\
> *Keisatsu o yonde kudasai!*\
> Please call the police!

> 財布をすられました。\
> *Saifu o suraremashita.*\
> My wallet was pickpocketed.

> パスポートをなくしました。\
> *Pasupōto o nakushimashita.*\
> I've lost my passport.

**Chikan** (痴漢) means groping or molestation, usually on crowded trains, and the person who does it. It is a crime, and shouting 痴漢です！ (*Chikan desu!*) draws immediate attention; station staff and police take reports seriously. Women-only cars (see [section 6.1](01-trains-and-transit.md)) exist largely because of it.

### The Kōban

A **kōban** (交番) is a small neighborhood police box staffed by local officers, found near stations and on busy corners all over Japan. It is the first place to go for lost property, directions and non-urgent reports.

> 忘れ物をしました。\
> *Wasuremono o shimashita.*\
> I've left something behind.

> 財布は届いていますか。\
> *Saifu wa todoite imasu ka.*\
> Has a wallet been handed in?

> 盗難届を出したいです。\
> *Tōnan todoke o dashitai desu.*\
> I'd like to report a theft.

Japan's lost-property system is famously effective, and wallets are often returned with the cash still inside. If you leave something on a train, ask station staff or contact the railway company's lost-property office the same day; items found in Tokyo eventually go to the Metropolitan Police lost-property center.

## Natural Disasters

Earthquakes, typhoons, heavy rain, tsunami and volcanic eruptions are part of life in Japan, and the country's warning systems and building codes are among the best in the world. 自然災害 (*shizen saigai*) means natural disaster.

### Earthquakes

Japan records more than a thousand felt earthquakes in a typical year, most of them small. If a strong one strikes, the standard guidance is:

1. Protect yourself where you are: get under a sturdy table and hold on, or cover your head (身を守る, *mi o mamoru*).
2. Stay away from windows, shelves and anything that can fall.
3. Don't run outside during the shaking; falling glass and signs are a major danger.
4. When the shaking stops, open a door to secure an exit, check for fire, and follow announcements.

If you are outside, move away from buildings, walls, vending machines and utility poles, and protect your head.

The **Kinkyū jishin sokuhō** (緊急地震速報, Earthquake Early Warning) is an alert from the Japan Meteorological Agency, sent to phones, TV and radio when strong shaking is expected. It sounds a distinctive rising alarm, often a few seconds before the shaking arrives, though close to the epicenter there may be no warning at all. Those seconds are for getting under the table, not for checking the news.

Japanese reports measure earthquakes on two scales. Magnitude (マグニチュード) is the energy released at the source, while **shindo** (震度) is the strength of shaking at a particular place, measured on the Japan Meteorological Agency's scale from 0 to 7, with levels 5 and 6 split into lower (弱, *jaku*) and upper (強, *kyō*). A single earthquake has one magnitude but a different shindo in every town; shindo is the number that tells you what it felt like where you are.

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 地震 | jishin | Earthquake |
| 震度 | shindo | Seismic intensity (shaking at a place) |
| 震源 | shingen | Hypocenter, the source of an earthquake |
| 余震 | yoshin | Aftershock |
| 津波 | tsunami | Tsunami |
| 避難 | hinan | Evacuation |
| 避難場所 | hinan basho | Evacuation area (an open space or high ground to flee to) |
| 避難所 | hinanjo | Evacuation shelter (somewhere to stay, often a school) |

Tsunami alerts come in three grades:

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 津波注意報 | tsunami chūihō | Tsunami advisory |
| 津波警報 | tsunami keihō | Tsunami warning |
| 大津波警報 | ōtsunami keihō | Major tsunami warning |

> **Warning:** If you are near the coast and feel a strong earthquake or hear a tsunami warning, move to high ground or the upper floors of a tall, solid building immediately. Don't wait to see the water; the first wave is often not the largest.

### Typhoons and Heavy Rain

Typhoons (台風, *taifū*) can arrive from early summer to autumn, and they are most frequent in August and September. A big one is forecast days in advance: trains and flights are cancelled, shops close early and convenience stores sell out of bread and water. Stay indoors once the storm zone (暴風域, *bōfūiki*) reaches you.

Since 2021, evacuation information has used a five-step **keikai reberu** (警戒レベル, alert level) scale. The three you need to know are below; the older term 避難勧告 (*hinan kankoku*, evacuation advisory) was abolished in that reform, so ignore it if you see it in older materials.

| Level | Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 高齢者等避難 | kōreisha tō hinan | Older and disabled people should evacuate now |
| 4 | 避難指示 | hinan shiji | Evacuation order: everyone in the area should evacuate |
| 5 | 緊急安全確保 | kinkyū anzen kakuho | Disaster imminent or happening: take the best action to save your life |

Other words you will see in forecasts:

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 大雨 | ōame | Heavy rain |
| 暴風 | bōfū | Violent wind |
| 洪水 | kōzui | Flood |
| 土砂災害 | dosha saigai | Landslide disaster |
| 警報 | keihō | Warning |
| 注意報 | chūihō | Advisory |

### Alerts on Your Phone

Phones on Japanese networks receive emergency alerts automatically, including earthquake warnings and messages relayed through J-Alert (全国瞬時警報システム, *zenkoku shunji keihō shisutemu*), the national system for disasters and missile warnings. A phone from abroad may not receive these reliably, so visitors should install Safety tips, a free app supervised by the Japan Tourism Agency that sends earthquake, tsunami, weather and evacuation alerts in 15 languages.

## Fire

> 火事だ！\
> *Kaji da!*\
> Fire!

> 逃げて！\
> *Nigete!*\
> Get out! Run!

> 中に人がいます。\
> *Naka ni hito ga imasu.*\
> There's someone inside.

The fire number is the same as the ambulance number: 119.

## Lost Children and Lost Adults

迷子 (*maigo*) means a lost child, and by extension anyone who is lost.

> 子どもがいなくなりました。\
> *Kodomo ga inaku narimashita.*\
> My child is missing.

> 道に迷いました。\
> *Michi ni mayoimashita.*\
> I'm lost.

Stations, department stores and theme parks have staff who handle lost children, and they make announcements over the public address system in a fixed formula:

> 迷子のお知らせをいたします。5歳の男の子をお預かりしております。\
> *Maigo no oshirase o itashimasu. Gosai no otoko no ko o o-azukari shite orimasu.*\
> This is an announcement about a lost child. We are looking after a five-year-old boy.

## Before You Travel

A little preparation makes every phrase on this page easier to use:

1. Save 110, 119 and the Japan Visitor Hotline in your phone.
2. Keep a photo of your passport in secure cloud storage.
3. Buy travel insurance that covers medical treatment and evacuation.
4. Save your accommodation's address in Japanese.
5. Install the Safety tips app and an offline Japanese translation pack (camera translation reads signs).
6. Check official travel advisories, especially during typhoon season.

## Summary

- Call 110 for the police and 119 for fire or an ambulance; the 119 operator first asks 火事ですか、救急ですか, so answer 救急です for a medical emergency.
- The Japan Visitor Hotline (050-3816-2787) helps visitors around the clock, and a kōban is the place for lost property and non-urgent police help.
- Short patterns such as 〜が痛いです, 〜にアレルギーがあります and 〜をなくしました cover most medical and police situations.
- In an earthquake, protect yourself in place and don't run outside; shindo measures the shaking where you are, from 0 to 7.
- Since 2021, evacuation alerts use levels: 避難指示 (level 4) means everyone should evacuate; near the coast, a strong quake or tsunami warning means go to high ground at once.
- Visitors pay the full cost of medical care, so travel insurance and the Safety tips alert app are worth having before you land.
