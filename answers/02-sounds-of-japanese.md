# Chapter 2 — The Sounds of Japanese

## 2.1 How Japanese Sounds Work

1. ねこ *ne-ko* (2); ほん *ho-n* (2); きって *ki-t-te* (3); にっぽん *ni-p-po-n* (4); とうきょう *to-o-kyo-o* (4); がっこう *ga-k-ko-o* (4); しんぶん *shi-n-bu-n* (4); おばあさん *o-ba-a-sa-n* (5).
2. McDonald's: 3 syllables, 6 morae (*ma-ku-do-na-ru-do*). Christmas: 2 syllables, 5 morae (*ku-ri-su-ma-su*). Strike: 1 syllable, 5 morae (*su-to-ra-i-ku*). Karaoke: 4 syllables in English ("carry-OH-kee") and 4 morae in Japanese (*ka-ra-o-ke*): the count is the same, but Japanese gives every beat equal weight and keeps the vowels pure.
3. English counts syllables, and *Tōkyō* has two vowel peaks, "Toe" and "kyoe". Japanese counts morae, and each long vowel takes two beats: *to-o-kyo-o*. きょ (*kyo*) is one mora because the small ょ combines with き into a single sound; it is not *ki-yo*.
4. (a) ご (*go*), as in ごりら (*gorira*). (b) お (*o*), the second half of the long vowel, not *kyo*. (c) ぱ (*pa*). A word ending in ん loses because no Japanese word begins with ん, so the next player cannot continue.
5. You will hear differences in voice pitch, speed, how clearly the vowels are said and, in *sushi*, how much the *u* is devoiced. Hearing many voices teaches you what all their versions have in common, which is the principle of high-variability phonetic training (Logan, Lively and Pisoni, 1991; Thomson, 2018); replaying one recording teaches you that one speaker.

## 2.2 Vowels and Consonants

6. In "SAH-kay", the *e* has become the English diphthong "ay", and the first syllable is stressed; Japanese *sa-ke* has a short, pure *e* as in "bed", and two even beats. In "SOW-coe", both *o* glide into "oo"; Japanese *so-ko* has two short *o* with no glide. To fix both, hold your mouth still for the whole vowel, ideally checking in a mirror.
7. さんぽ *sanpo*: *m* (before *p*). みんな *minna*: *n*. りんご *ringo*: *ng*. ほん *hon*: the soft nasal at the back of the mouth (end of a word). しんぶん *shinbun*: *m* for the first ん (before *b*), the back nasal for the final one. まんが *manga*: *ng*. でんわ *denwa*: the back nasal (before *w*).
8. きねん *ki-ne-n*, three morae, "commemoration"; きんえん *ki-n-e-n*, four morae, "no smoking". The apostrophe shows that the *n* is the moraic ん, a beat of its own, and is not the start of the syllable *ne*.
9. です: the *u* (*des*). すき: the *u* (*s'ki*). あさ: none. した: the *i* (*sh'ta*). ひと: the *i* (*h'to*). ちかてつ: the *i* (*ch'katetsu*). なまえ: none, since its consonants are voiced and its vowels are not *i* or *u*. きく: the first *i* (*k'ku*), between two *k*; the final *u* may also go quiet at the end of a phrase.
10. つ is *ts*, as at the end of "cats": keep the *t* at the start of the word, *tsu-na-mi*. ふ is made by blowing gently through slightly parted lips, with no teeth touching the lip, so it is softer than English *f*.
11. A single quick tap of the tongue tip just behind the upper teeth. The *tt* in American "butter" or "a lotta" is very close. An English *r*, with the tongue curled back, gives away a foreign accent at once.

## 2.3 Long Vowels and Double Consonants

12. おばさん *o-ba-sa-n* (4), aunt or middle-aged woman; おばあさん *o-ba-a-sa-n* (5), grandmother or elderly woman. ここ *ko-ko* (2), here; こうこう *ko-o-ko-o* (4), high school. きて *ki-te* (2), come (please); きって *ki-t-te* (3), postage stamp. しゅじん *shu-ji-n* (3), husband; しゅうじん *shu-u-ji-n* (4), prisoner.
13. *byōin* (4: *byo-o-i-n*), hospital; *biyōin* (5: *bi-yo-o-i-n*), beauty salon; *zasshi* (3: *za-s-shi*), magazine; *kōhī* (4: *ko-o-hi-i*), coffee; *chiisai* (4: *chi-i-sa-i*), small; *chotto* (3: *cho-t-to*), a little.
14. In the bar, *biru* (ビル) is a building, so you asked for a building; you meant *bīru* (ビール), beer. In the office, *bīru* is beer, so you asked where the beer is; you meant *biru*, the building.
15. The kana えい spell an older pronunciation, but in ordinary speech the sequence has become a long *e*: *se-n-se-e*. Other words: とけい (*tokei*, clock), えいが (*eiga*, movie), けいこ (*keiko*, practice), きれい (*kirei*, pretty).
16. Any four of: train with sentences as well as isolated words (Hirata, 2004a); include several speaking rates, slow and fast (Hirata, Whitehurst and Cullings, 2007); use many different speakers; train every kind of length contrast, long vowels, double consonants and combined sounds, since training on one type did not carry over to untrained types (Tajima and colleagues, 2008); get immediate feedback on each answer; keep sessions short and frequent.
17. Model answer: most learners hear the difference in some clips but not others, especially in fast speech or when the word is buried in a sentence. The exercise is high-variability listening practice: it trains you to judge length relative to each speaker's speed and voice, which is the skill the studies in [section 2.3](../02-sounds-of-japanese/03-long-vowels-and-double-consonants.md) found hardest to learn.

## 2.4 Pitch Accent and Intonation

18. 箸が HL-L; 橋が LH-L; 端が LH-H; 雨が HL-L; 飴が LH-H.
19. 学生 [0]: heiban, がくせいが LHHH-H. 本 [1]: atamadaka, ほんが HL-L. 先生 [3]: nakadaka, せんせいが LHHL-L. 山 [2]: odaka, やまが LH-L. 電話 [0]: heiban, でんわが LHH-H.
20. 花 is odaka (LH-L) and 鼻 is heiban (LH-H). On the word alone both are LH, because in odaka the fall comes after the last mora, where there is nothing left to fall onto. With a particle, the difference appears: after 花 the particle drops (*hana ga*, LH-L), after 鼻 it stays high (LH-H).
21. Rule 1: the first two morae differ in pitch, either HL or LH. Rule 2: once the pitch falls, it stays low for the rest of the word, so a word has at most one fall. HLH would need the pitch to rise again after falling, which rule 2 does not allow.
22. 行く。 with falling intonation is a statement, "I'm going." 行く？ with a rise on the last mora is a question, "Are you going?" In いい天気ですね, ね invites agreement: "Nice weather, isn't it?" In もう九時ですよ, よ marks information the listener may not have noticed: "It's already nine o'clock, you know."
23. 日本語 (にほんご) is heiban [0]: LHHH, with a following particle staying high. 先生 (せんせい) is [3]: LHHL. In the sentence, the Prosody Tutor should show a fall after *sense* in 先生, the voice rising again and staying up through 日本語を, and a final fall after *ma* in 話します (*hanashimasu*). Your display may differ slightly in detail; the falls are what to look for.
24. Model plan: Day 1, listen three times with the transcript and mark where the pitch falls. Day 2, shadow with the transcript, a fraction of a second behind. Day 3, shadow without the transcript. Day 4, record yourself shadowing and compare with the original, listening for falls in the right places, even rhythm and the length of long vowels and double consonants. Day 5, record again and compare with day 4. Section 1.2 explains the evidence for shadowing (Hamada, 2016).
25. Shport found that 16 intermediate American learners, never taught pitch accent, produced the correct pattern only 56% of the time and discriminated patterns only 46% of the time, close to chance, and tended to give every word a fall. Pitch accent does not come from exposure alone, so learn each word's pattern from audio, with a particle after it, at the moment you learn the word.
