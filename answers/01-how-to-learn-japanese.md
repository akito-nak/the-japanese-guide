# Chapter 1 — How to Learn Japanese

## 1.1 Why Japanese

1. (a) I eat sushi. (b) My older sister eats sushi. (c) My friend eats sushi (or "My friends eat sushi"). Nothing changes: 食べます (*tabemasu*) has the same form for every person. Only the word before は changes.
2. (a) I read a book. (b) I read books. The Japanese is the same, 本を読みます (*hon o yomimasu*); only the situation, or a number with a counter such as 一冊 (*issatsu*) or 三冊 (*sansatsu*), tells you how many.
3. おばさん (*obasan*): aunt; おばあさん (*obāsan*): grandmother; きて (*kite*): come; きって (*kitte*): stamp. The pairs show vowel length (a long *ā*) and double consonants (the held beat of small っ).
4. Any three of: "Are you going?", "Is he (or she) going?", "Are they going?", "Shall we go?", "Am I going?" The situation tells you: who you are talking to, who was just mentioned, and the tone of voice.
5. Yesterday, friend with, ate, sushi, [topic], was delicious: 昨日 友達と 食べた 寿司は おいしかった (*Kinō tomodachi to tabeta sushi wa oishikatta*).
6. Easier: any four of five unchanging vowels, kana that spell sounds regularly, only two truly irregular verbs, verbs that don't change for the person, and no articles, plurals, grammatical gender or tones. Real hurdles: kanji, word order and grammar far from English, politeness levels, omitted subjects and context, and listening at speed.
7. It describes full-time classes aimed at professional working proficiency, the level of a diplomat who negotiates in Japanese. Travel and everyday conversation come far sooner, especially with a daily habit.
8. ごちそうさまでした (*gochisōsama deshita*), "thank you for the meal".

## 1.2 Study and Immersion: The Middle Path

9. Comprehensible input is language you hear or read and mostly understand, with a little that stretches you. A native talk show in week two is almost entirely incomprehensible, so it gives you sound without meaning and teaches little.
10. Study, input, output, review (then back to study).
11. A strong card puts the word in a short sentence you met in real Japanese, with audio, for example front 駅で友達に会います。(*Eki de tomodachi ni aimasu.*), back "I'm meeting a friend at the station." It shows that the person you meet takes に (or と), never を. Add a production card too: front "I'm meeting a friend at the station", back the Japanese.
12. Every new card returns for review over the following weeks, so 60 new cards on Monday produce a pile of hundreds of reviews within days, and many learners give up. Add 10 to 20 new cards a day instead.
13. One group tested themselves on the word pairs repeatedly; the other kept restudying them. A week later, the testing group recalled about 80% and the restudying group about 36%. Close your notes and try to recall them before you reread them.
14. The training uses many different words spoken by many different voices, male and female, at different speeds, with feedback on every answer. Variety forces your ear to learn what all versions of a sound have in common, instead of memorizing one speaker's voice. Japanese contrasts worth training: short against long vowels (おばさん, おばあさん), single against double consonants (きて, きって) and pitch accent patterns (箸, 橋).
15. At 98%: 8 unknown words (one in 50). At 95%: 20.
16. Listen once while reading the transcript; shadow it aloud with the transcript, a fraction of a second behind the speaker; then shadow it without the transcript. Repeat over several days, and record yourself now and then to compare.
17. 手紙 (*tegami*) means "letter", the kind you write and post. Hand + paper is a useful hint, but it could as easily have meant "napkin" or "handout": the parts narrow the meaning down without pinning it. A word's meaning has to be learned as a word, which is why kanji are best learned inside real vocabulary and sentences, with both the kanji and the context as clues.
18. A review by Pashler and colleagues (2008) found virtually no evidence that matching teaching to a "learning style" improves learning. Suggest a mix instead: pictures and kanji for the eyes, audio and shadowing for the ears, speaking for the mouth, with spaced retrieval for everything.

## 1.3 Apps, Courses and Tools

19. Anki: review. italki: output. NHK News Web Easy: input (reading, with audio). A learner podcast: input (listening). Genki: study. A dictionary such as Jisho, Takoboto or Shirabe Jisho serves every part, because you look words up whatever you are doing.
20. (a) *arigatou*. (b) *kitte*. (c) *honn* (*hon* followed by another letter also works, but *nn* is always safe). (d) *chotto*.
21. F7 turns the input into katakana. The space bar converts kana to kanji; Enter accepts the choice.
22. Type *x* or *l* before the vowel: *xa* or *la* gives ぁ. For a small っ on its own, type *xtu* or *ltu*.

## 1.4 The JLPT Map

23. N5, N4, N3, N2, N1. N5: about 100 kanji and 800 words; N3: about 650 kanji and 3,750 words. They are estimates because the JLPT has not published official word or kanji lists since 2010, so the figures come from the old test's lists and from textbook and app authors, who disagree at the edges.
24. Speaking and writing. To train speaking: a weekly tutor session, a language exchange or daily shadowing. To train writing: a short journal entry or chat message in Japanese, corrected by a tutor or partner.
25. The JFT-Basic, the Japan Foundation's computer-based test, held roughly monthly and accepted for the Specified Skilled Worker visa.
26. Answers will vary. A model: "I'm a beginner who knows hiragana. In twelve months I want to pass N5 and be halfway to N4. I can give 45 minutes a day. Study: this book and Genki. Input: Nihongo con Teppei and Tadoku.org graded readers. Output: one italki session a week and five minutes of shadowing a day. Review: Anki, every day."
