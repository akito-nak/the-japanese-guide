# Matsuri — Japan's Festival Soul

On an ordinary summer evening in an ordinary neighborhood, the drums start. Paper lanterns light up between the houses, stalls appear selling grilled squid and shaved ice, children in cotton kimono chase each other with toy fans, and around the corner a crowd of sweating, shouting people is heaving a golden shrine through the streets on their shoulders. Japan's quiet neighborhoods have a second, louder life, and it comes out at festival time.

A **matsuri** (祭り, often お祭り, *omatsuri*) is a festival, and most have their roots in Shinto or Buddhist practice: honoring a local deity, praying for a good harvest or protection from disease, welcoming the spirits of ancestors. Nobody has an exact count of how many take place each year, but Japan has some 80,000 Shinto shrines and most of them hold at least one festival, so if you spend a summer in Japan you will almost certainly stumble into one.

The **mikoshi** (神輿) is the heart of many shrine festivals. It is a portable shrine into which the deity is believed to be ritually transferred so that it can tour the neighborhood it protects. Teams of carriers bounce and sway it through the streets, and the shaking is part of the point: it is said to invigorate the deity.

> わっしょい、わっしょい！\
> *Wasshoi, wasshoi!*\
> Heave-ho, heave-ho! (the mikoshi carriers' chant)

## The Festival Year

The festival calendar follows the seasons, and many events are national, celebrated in every town in some form. The sections below follow the year from spring.

### Hanami: Cherry Blossom Viewing

*Season:* late March to early May, moving from south to north (Okinawa's cherries bloom as early as January)\
*Famous spots:* Ueno Park and Shinjuku Gyoen in Tokyo, Maruyama Park in Kyoto, Hirosaki Castle in Aomori

**Hanami** (花見, "flower viewing") is the custom of gathering under the cherry blossoms (桜, *sakura*) to eat, drink and enjoy them while they last. It isn't a matsuri in the religious sense, but it is one of the most beloved events of the year. Every spring the news follows the 桜前線 (*sakura zensen*, cherry-blossom front) as it moves north like a weather system, and companies send junior staff out early to hold a picnic spot with a blue tarp.

The blossoms last only a week or two, and their brevity is part of their appeal. The idea of **mono no aware** (物の哀れ), a gentle, bittersweet awareness of the impermanence of things, is the classic lens for seeing them, and falling petals are its most famous image.

> 桜が満開ですね。\
> *Sakura ga mankai desu ne.*\
> The cherry blossoms are in full bloom, aren't they?

> もう散り始めましたね。\
> *Mō chiri-hajimemashita ne.*\
> They've already started to fall.

> **Note:** Rules vary by park. Shinjuku Gyoen bans alcohol, many parks limit tarps or reserved spaces, and some charge admission, so check before you plan a party.

### Hina Matsuri: Girls' Day

*When:* March 3

Families with daughters display a tiered set of ornamental dolls (雛人形, *hina ningyō*) representing the imperial court, to wish for the girls' health and happiness. Tradition says the dolls should be put away promptly after the day, or the daughter will marry late, which is the kind of superstition everyone half-believes.

### Kodomo no Hi: Children's Day

*When:* May 5, the last day of Golden Week

Once known as Boys' Day, the national holiday has been Children's Day (こどもの日) since 1948. Families fly carp-shaped streamers called 鯉のぼり (*koinobori*): carp swim upstream against the current, so they stand for strength and determination.

### Gion Matsuri

*When:* throughout July; float processions on July 17 and 24\
*Where:* Kyoto (Yasaka Shrine)

Gion Matsuri is one of 日本三大祭り (*Nihon sandai matsuri*), Japan's three great festivals, together with Osaka's Tenjin Matsuri and Tokyo's Kanda Matsuri. It began in 869 as a ritual to appease the spirits blamed for an epidemic, and it fills the whole of July. Its highlight is the 山鉾巡行 (*yamaboko junkō*), the procession of towering wooden floats, the largest of which weigh around 12 tons and are hauled by teams of men through the streets of central Kyoto. On the evenings before each procession, the streets around the floats close to traffic and fill with stalls and crowds.

### Tenjin Matsuri

*When:* July 24 and 25\
*Where:* Osaka (Ōsaka Tenmangū shrine)

Osaka's great summer festival honors Sugawara no Michizane, the scholar enshrined as the god of learning. On the second evening, a river procession of around 100 boats carries the deity along the Ōkawa River while fireworks burst overhead.

### Tanabata: The Star Festival

*When:* July 7, or August 7 in some regions; Sendai's famous version runs from August 6 to 8

Tanabata (七夕, "the evening of the seventh") retells a legend of two lovers, Orihime (the star Vega) and Hikoboshi (Altair), who are separated by the Milky Way and allowed to meet only once a year. People write wishes on strips of colored paper (短冊, *tanzaku*) and tie them to bamboo branches, and shopping streets hang enormous paper streamers.

> 日本語が上手になりますように。\
> *Nihongo ga jōzu ni narimasu yō ni.*\
> May my Japanese get better. (a typical Tanabata wish)

### Obon

*When:* around August 13 to 16 in most of Japan; July in some areas, including parts of Tokyo

**Obon** (お盆) is the Buddhist festival when the spirits of ancestors are believed to return home for a few days. Families clean and visit graves (お墓参り, *ohaka mairi*), light welcoming fires, make offerings and, at the end, send the spirits back; in some places with lanterns floated down rivers (灯籠流し, *tōrō nagashi*).

Obon is also the season of **bon odori** (盆踊り), simple folk dances performed in a circle around a raised stage with a drum, in parks and school grounds across the country. Anyone can join in, and nobody expects you to know the steps: watch the person in front of you, copy the hands, and by the third round you will have it. The week is also one of the busiest travel periods of the year, as people return to their hometowns (帰省, *kisei*); book trains early.

### Awa Odori

*When:* August 12 to 15\
*Where:* Tokushima, on Shikoku, with offshoots across Japan

Awa Odori (阿波踊り) is the most famous of the bon dances: groups of dancers called 連 (*ren*) flow through the streets to shamisen, flutes and drums, chanting えらいやっちゃ、えらいやっちゃ、ヨイヨイヨイヨイ ("What a fuss, what a fuss!"). Its unofficial motto is a song lyric that every visitor learns:

> 踊る阿呆に見る阿呆、同じ阿呆なら踊らにゃ損々。\
> *Odoru ahō ni miru ahō, onaji ahō nara odoranya son son.*\
> The dancers are fools and the watchers are fools; if we're all fools anyway, you lose out if you don't dance.

### Autumn Festivals and Kōyō

Autumn brings harvest festivals at local shrines and the season of 紅葉 (*kōyō*, autumn leaves), when parks, temples and mountain paths fill with people admiring the reds and golds from October in Hokkaido to early December in the south. Kyoto in late November is one of Japan's great sights.

### Oshōgatsu: New Year

*When:* January 1 to 3, with preparations from late December

New Year (お正月, *oshōgatsu*) is the most important holiday of the year, and much of the country closes. Families gather to eat お節料理 (*osechi ryōri*), lacquered boxes of New Year dishes whose ingredients carry wishes for health, wealth and long life, and make **hatsumōde** (初詣), the first shrine or temple visit of the year.

- 年越しそば (*toshikoshi soba*): soba eaten on New Year's Eve; the long noodles stand for a long life.
- お年玉 (*otoshidama*): New Year money given to children in decorated envelopes.
- 初日の出 (*hatsuhinode*): the first sunrise of the year, which some people climb mountains to see.
- 年賀状 (*nengajō*): New Year postcards. Japan Post delivers cards posted by about December 25 on January 1 itself, though fewer people send them each year.

> あけましておめでとうございます。今年もよろしくお願いします。\
> *Akemashite omedetō gozaimasu. Kotoshi mo yoroshiku onegai shimasu.*\
> Happy New Year. I look forward to your continued friendship this year.

### Setsubun

*When:* around February 3, the day before the traditional start of spring

On Setsubun (節分), households drive out bad luck by throwing roasted soybeans, often at a family member wearing a demon mask, while shouting a set phrase:

> 鬼は外！福は内！\
> *Oni wa soto! Fuku wa uchi!*\
> Demons out! Good fortune in!

Many people also eat 恵方巻 (*ehōmaki*), a thick uncut sushi roll eaten in silence while facing the year's lucky direction.

## What to Wear: Yukata

The **yukata** (浴衣) is a light, unlined cotton kimono worn in summer: at festivals and fireworks displays, in onsen towns and as loungewear at ryokan. Renting or buying one is welcomed, not seen as costume, and a festival is the perfect excuse.

- It is tied with a sash called an 帯 (*obi*).
- Wrap the left side over the right, so the right panel is underneath. Right over left is the way the dead are dressed for burial.
- Wooden sandals called 下駄 (*geta*) complete the outfit; their clatter is a sound of summer.
- Ryokan provide yukata to wear in the inn and to the bath.

## Summary

- Matsuri are local festivals, mostly rooted in Shinto or Buddhist practice; carrying the mikoshi (portable shrine) through the streets is the heart of many.
- Japan's three great festivals are Kyoto's Gion Matsuri (July), Osaka's Tenjin Matsuri (July 24–25) and Tokyo's Kanda Matsuri (May).
- Hanami celebrates the brief cherry blossoms, linked to mono no aware, the bittersweet awareness that beautiful things pass.
- Obon (mid-August in most areas) welcomes ancestral spirits home and brings bon odori dances that anyone can join.
- New Year centers on family, osechi food and hatsumōde, the first shrine visit; Setsubun's bean-throwing chant is 鬼は外！福は内！
- At festivals, people wear yukata wrapped left over right and eat from yatai stalls: takoyaki, yakisoba, kakigōri and more.
