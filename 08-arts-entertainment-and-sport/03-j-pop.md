# J-Pop — Japan's Soundtrack

In 2017 someone uploaded a seven-minute song from 1984 to YouTube with a still photo of a smiling woman as the thumbnail. The algorithm started recommending it, and within a few years "Plastic Love" by Takeuchi Mariya had been played tens of millions of times by listeners who had never heard of her, most of whom could not understand a word of the lyrics. Many of them, sooner or later, wanted to.

Japanese popular music is one of the most powerful pulls toward the language, and for good reason. **J-pop** (Jポップ) is the broad name for Japanese popular music since about 1990. Before it came **kayōkyoku** (歌謡曲), a softer, orchestrated popular style that ruled from the 1960s through the 1980s. The new label marked a shift toward Western pop and rock structures, while the lyrics, melodies and sensibility stayed unmistakably Japanese.

## Why J-Pop Is Great for Language Learning

Songs do things for a learner that textbooks cannot:

- **The lyrics are real Japanese.** You can read them, look up every word and sing along.
- **The writing is often literary.** Songwriters use poetic vocabulary, classical echoes and emotional nuance that textbooks skip.
- **Repetition is built in.** A chorus drills a phrase into your memory more effectively than a flashcard.
- **Feelings make words stick.** Vocabulary tied to a song you love is vocabulary you keep.
- **The charts are a cultural window.** What Japan is listening to tells you what Japan is feeling.

> **Tip:** Look up lyrics on Uta-Net (uta-net.com) or J-Lyric (j-lyric.net), which publish the official Japanese text. Keep a dictionary open as you read: songwriters love beautiful, rarely used kanji.

## The Eras of Japanese Popular Music

### Kayōkyoku: 1960s to 1980s

Kayōkyoku was lush orchestration, enka-tinged melodies and big, emotional vocals: the soundtrack of postwar growth, and the music your Japanese friends' parents and grandparents grew up with.

- **美空ひばり (*Misora Hibari*, 1937–1989)**: often called the greatest singer in Japanese history. She began singing publicly as a child just after the war and recorded well over a thousand songs. Her last single, 「川の流れのように」 (*Kawa no Nagare no Yō ni*, "Like the Flow of the River," 1989), is still one of the most loved songs in Japan.
- **山口百恵 (*Yamaguchi Momoe*, born 1959)**: the defining idol of the 1970s. She retired in 1980, at 21 and at the height of her fame, to marry the actor Miura Tomokazu, one of the most famous career decisions in Japanese entertainment.
- **沢田研二 (*Sawada Kenji*, "Julie," born 1948)**: Japan's androgynous, theatrical glam-rock star.
- **キャンディーズ (Candies) and ピンク・レディー (Pink Lady)**: the girl trio and duo of the late 1970s whose matching costumes and choreography set the template for idol groups ever since.

### New Music: 1970s and 1980s

ニューミュージック (*nyū myūjikku*) was the name for singer-songwriters and bands who blended Western folk and rock with Japanese pop and wrote their own material:

- **松任谷由実 (*Matsutōya Yumi*, "Yuming," born 1954, first recording as 荒井由実 *Arai Yumi*)**: sophisticated, literary and melancholic, a star for five decades. Her 「ルージュの伝言」 (*Rouge no Dengon*) is the opening song of Studio Ghibli's *Kiki's Delivery Service*.
- **山下達郎 (*Yamashita Tatsurō*, born 1953)**: master of polished, groove-heavy pop. His 「クリスマス・イブ」 (*Christmas Eve*, 1983) has entered the Oricon top 100 every holiday season since 1987, a run of 40 consecutive years (as of the 2025 holiday season) recognized by Guinness World Records.
- **大滝詠一 (*Ōtaki Eiichi*, 1948–2013)**: pop craftsman and producer whose 1981 album *A Long Vacation* defined a breezy summer sound.

### City Pop: Late 1970s to Late 1980s

**City pop** (シティポップ) is the name now given to the slick, funky, nostalgic pop of Japan's boom years: music for driving along the coast at night in a new car. It went viral around the world in the late 2010s, some 30 years after it was made, through YouTube recommendations, DJ sets and samples.

| Artist | Song | Year |
|---|---|---|
| 竹内まりや (Takeuchi Mariya) | Plastic Love | 1984 |
| 松原みき (Matsubara Miki) | 真夜中のドア〜stay with me | 1979 |
| 杏里 (Anri) | Remember Summer Days | 1983 |
| 山下達郎 (Yamashita Tatsurō) | Sparkle | 1982 |
| 亜蘭知子 (Aran Tomoko) | Midnight Pretenders | 1983 |
| 大貫妙子 (Ōnuki Taeko) | 4:00 A.M. | 1978 |

「真夜中のドア」 topped global viral streaming charts in 2020, four decades after its release. If you have not yet heard "Plastic Love," it is a fine reward for finishing this section.

### The Million-Seller 1990s

The 1990s were the commercial peak of the Japanese music industry, with CD sales that are hard to believe today:

- **宇多田ヒカル (*Utada Hikaru*, born 1983)**: born in New York and raised between there and Tokyo, she brought R&B phrasing into Japanese pop. Her debut album *First Love* (1999) sold more than 7.6 million copies and remains the best-selling album in Japanese history. Her 「光」 (*Hikari*, 2002) is the theme of the video game *Kingdom Hearts*, and 「花束を君に」 (*Hanataba o Kimi ni*, 2016) was the theme of an NHK morning drama.
- **浜崎あゆみ (*Hamasaki Ayumi*, born 1978)**: the "Empress of J-pop," dominant from the late 1990s through the 2000s and one of the best-selling solo artists in Japanese history. Her lyrics about independence, pain and identity spoke for a generation.
- **小室哲哉 (*Komuro Tetsuya*)**: the producer behind globe, TRF, 安室奈美恵 (*Amuro Namie*) and many more, and the most commercially successful hitmaker of the decade.
- **SPEED**: an Okinawan teen group whose energetic hits were the sound of 1997 and 1998.
- **SMAP**: the most famous male idol group in Japanese history, whose break-up at the end of 2016 was treated as a national event.
- **Mr.Children (ミスチル, *Misuchiru*)**: one of Japan's best-loved rock bands, melodic and lyrically ambitious, still filling stadiums.
- **B'z**: the hard-rock duo that is the best-selling act in Oricon history, with a huge following at home and little profile in the West, a reminder of how self-sufficient Japan's music market is.

### The 2000s and 2010s

The industry reinvented itself around idols, live shows and the internet:

- **AKB48 (from 2005)**: producer 秋元康 (*Akimoto Yasushi*)'s idol system changed the business, with daily theater shows, fan voting, handshake events and a huge multi-team structure that spread across Asia. Its 選抜総選挙 (*senbatsu sōsenkyo*, the popularity "general election") was a genuine cultural event in the early 2010s.
- **嵐 (*Arashi*, debut 1999)**: a five-member idol group that became one of the most powerful forces in Japanese entertainment. It went on hiatus at the end of 2020, reunited for a farewell tour in spring 2026 and disbanded when it ended in May 2026.
- **きゃりーぱみゅぱみゅ (*Kyary Pamyu Pamyu*, born 1993)**: the kawaii art-pop icon of Harajuku, produced by 中田ヤスタカ (*Nakata Yasutaka*), with surreal videos recognized worldwide.
- **Perfume (formed 2000)**: the electronic trio from Hiroshima, also produced by Nakata, famous for precise choreography and technology-driven live shows. It entered an open-ended hiatus, which the members called a "cold sleep," at the start of 2026.

### Contemporary J-Pop: 2010s to Now

Streaming and social media changed who becomes a star. Many of today's biggest artists began online:

- **YOASOBI (formed 2019)**: the duo of producer Ayase and singer ikura, who turn short stories into songs. Their debut 「夜に駆ける」 (*Yoru ni Kakeru*, "Racing into the Night") became a streaming phenomenon, and 「アイドル」 (*Idol*, 2023) topped Billboard's Global Excl. US chart, a first for a Japanese-language song.
- **米津玄師 (*Yonezu Kenshi*, born 1991)**: started out as a Vocaloid producer under the name ハチ (*Hachi*) and became one of Japan's biggest solo artists. 「Lemon」 (2018) became one of the most-streamed and downloaded Japanese songs ever, and 「KICKBACK」 (2022) for the anime *Chainsaw Man* was a worldwide hit. He writes, produces and illustrates his own work.
- **あいみょん (*Aimyon*, born 1995)**: a singer-songwriter with a direct, conversational style; her lyrics about love and daily life are excellent intermediate reading.
- **Official髭男dism (*Higedan*, formed 2012)**: piano-led pop rock built around singer 藤原聡 (*Fujiwara Satoshi*)'s remarkable range. 「Pretender」 (2019) and 「I LOVE...」 (2020) were enormous hits.
- **King Gnu (formed 2013)**: art rock meets pop, with darker themes and adventurous structures. 「白日」 (*Hakujitsu*, 2019) is one of the most-streamed Japanese songs of its era.
- **Vaundy (born 2000)**: a singer-producer who writes, arranges and designs everything himself, with a string of hits such as 「怪獣の花唄」 (*Kaijū no Hanauta*) and 「踊り子」 (*Odoriko*).

## Vocaloid — The Synthesized Singer

**Vocaloid** (ボーカロイド, *bōkaroido*, often ボカロ *bokaro*) is singing-synthesizer software: you type in a melody and lyrics, and a voice built from recordings of a real singer performs them. It is not artificial intelligence, but it gave anyone with a computer a tireless vocalist. In 2007 the company Crypton Future Media released a voice with a character attached, 初音ミク (*Hatsune Miku*), a teal-haired 16-year-old who became a global icon and now "performs" in holographic concerts.

An entire ecosystem grew around her:

- Fans uploaded original songs to **ニコニコ動画 (*Niconico Dōga*)**, a Japanese video site launched in 2006, whose comments scroll across the screen in real time (more on that in [section 8.5](05-vtubers-and-streaming.md)).
- **ボカロP (*bokaro-P*)**, Vocaloid producers, built huge followings under pseudonyms.
- Human singers covered the songs online; these cover singers are called 歌い手 (*utaite*).
- Many of today's stars came out of this world. Yonezu Kenshi and YOASOBI's Ayase began as Vocaloid producers, and the singer **Ado** began as an utaite.

## Anime Songs

Anime theme songs, アニソン (*anison*, short for アニメソング), are a genre and an industry of their own. Opening and closing themes (主題歌, *shudaika*) can launch a career overnight.

| Era | Artists | Notable work |
|---|---|---|
| 1960s–70s | Various | Robot and children's anime themes |
| 1980s–90s | 影山ヒロノブ (Kageyama Hironobu), 高橋洋子 (Takahashi Yōko) | *Dragon Ball Z*, *Neon Genesis Evangelion* |
| 2000s | 水樹奈々 (Mizuki Nana), JAM Project, 坂本真綾 (Sakamoto Maaya) | Countless series themes; Mizuki is also a top voice actor |
| 2010s | LiSA, Aimer, fripSide | *Demon Slayer*, *Fate*, *A Certain Scientific Railgun* |
| 2020s | YOASOBI, Ado, King Gnu | *Oshi no Ko*, *One Piece Film: Red*, *Jujutsu Kaisen* |

Three artists every anime fan meets sooner or later:

- **Aimer (エメ, *Eme*)**: a husky-voiced singer whose anime and film themes include 「Ref:rain」 and 「Torches」.
- **LiSA (リサ, *Risa*)**: the reigning queen of anime songs. 「紅蓮華」 (*Gurenge*, 2019) from *Demon Slayer* became a national hit, and 「炎」 (*Homura*, 2020) from the *Mugen Train* film won the Japan Record Award.
- **Ado (born 2002)**: a vocalist who never shows her face. She broke through in 2020 with 「うっせぇわ」 (*Usseewa*, roughly "Shut up!"), a snarling anthem of youth frustration, and sang every song of the singer Uta in *One Piece Film: Red* (2022). Her range runs from delicate to terrifying within a single line.

## Songs for Language Learning

The best song to study is one you love, but these make good starting points at each level:

| Level | Song | Artist | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Beginner | さんぽ (*Sanpo*) | 井上あずみ (Inoue Azumi), from *My Neighbor Totoro* | Simple words, cheerful and slow |
| Beginner | ふるさと (*Furusato*) | Traditional school song (1914) | Slow, classic, sung by every Japanese child |
| Intermediate | 花束を君に | Utada Hikaru | Emotional, with clear diction |
| Intermediate | Lemon | Yonezu Kenshi | Clear lyrics and a memorable hook |
| Intermediate | 夜に駆ける | YOASOBI | Modern language, built on a story |
| Advanced | 白日 | King Gnu | Poetic, harder vocabulary |
| Advanced | 春泥棒 (*Haru Dorobō*) | ヨルシカ (Yorushika) | Literary, kanji-heavy lyrics |
| Advanced | Plastic Love | Takeuchi Mariya | Nuanced, nostalgic, mixed with English |

> 夜に駆ける、って、どういう意味？\
> *Yoru ni kakeru, tte, dō iu imi?*\
> What does "yoru ni kakeru" mean?

A suggested listening path:

1. Start with the Studio Ghibli soundtracks by 久石譲 (*Hisaishi Joe*), instrumental and vocal, which nearly every Japanese person knows.
2. Add Utada Hikaru, the clearest gateway to modern J-pop emotion.
3. Try city pop (Takeuchi Mariya, Anri, Yamashita Tatsurō) for history and global context.
4. Explore YOASOBI and Yonezu Kenshi for contemporary, literary J-pop.
5. Follow whatever you love from there.

## Summary

- J-pop is the broad name for Japanese popular music since about 1990; it grew out of kayōkyoku, the orchestrated popular song of the 1960s to 1980s.
- City pop, the slick pop of the late 1970s and 1980s, found a global audience in the late 2010s, with "Plastic Love" and 「真夜中のドア」 as its best-known songs.
- Utada Hikaru's *First Love* (1999), with more than 7.6 million copies sold, is the best-selling album in Japanese history; B'z is the best-selling act in Oricon history.
- Vocaloid software and the singer character Hatsune Miku (2007) created an online scene of producers and cover singers that launched stars such as Yonezu Kenshi, Ayase of YOASOBI and Ado.
- Anime songs (anison) are a major genre of their own, and songs at the right level are one of the most motivating ways to build vocabulary.
