# Baseball and Sumo — Japan's Two National Pastimes

One sport arrived from America in 1872 and became a national obsession within a generation. The other is so old that the first chronicle of Japan, the 古事記 (*Kojiki*, 712), tells of gods deciding the fate of a land with a trial of strength. Baseball is the sport Japan chose and made its own; sumo is the sport that was always there. Between them they hold a great deal of the country's sporting heart.

You do not need to love sport to get value from this section. Baseball and sumo fill the news every day, their vocabulary leaks into ordinary speech (a business plan can be a 逆転ホームラン, a come-from-behind home run), and an evening at a ballpark or a tournament is one of the best live experiences Japan offers.

## 野球 — Baseball

### How Baseball Became Japanese

Baseball arrived in 1872, taught by the American teacher Horace Wilson at a school in Tokyo that later became part of the University of Tokyo. Within two decades it had spread to schools and universities across the country, and within a generation nobody thought of it as an American game anymore.

Part of the reason was fit. Baseball rewards patience, precision, discipline within a group and individual excellence within a team, all values that schools and companies in modern Japan prized. The duel between pitcher and batter, the geometry of fielding and the long wait for the right pitch felt familiar even when the game was new.

Professional baseball began in 1936, and the current two-league system dates from 1950. By the 1960s 野球 (*yakyū*, baseball) was the country's dominant spectator sport, and professional baseball, プロ野球 (*puro yakyū*), has never fully given up that position.

### Abe Isoo — The Father of Japanese Baseball

If Horace Wilson brought the ball and bat, 安部磯雄 (*Abe Isoo*, 1865–1949) did more than anyone to give the Japanese game its character, and he is widely called 日本野球の父 (*Nihon yakyū no chichi*, the father of Japanese baseball).

Born in Fukuoka, Abe studied at 同志社 (*Dōshisha*) in Kyoto and then at Hartford Theological Seminary in the United States in the early 1890s. He returned to Japan a committed Christian, a social reformer and an enthusiast for baseball. As a professor at the school that became 早稲田大学 (*Waseda Daigaku*, Waseda University), he managed its baseball club from its founding in 1901 until 1926.

Abe saw baseball as moral education: a way to build character, discipline and cooperation. Under his leadership Waseda played the first 早慶戦 (*Sōkeisen*), its rivalry game with Keio University, in 1903, and in 1905, in the middle of the Russo-Japanese War, he took his players to California to play American colleges such as Stanford. It was the first overseas tour by a Japanese baseball team, and the players came home with new techniques that spread through the Japanese game. Abe was also central to founding the Tokyo Big6 university league, still played today.

Baseball was only one side of him. Abe was a Christian pacifist and one of the founders of Japan's first socialist party, the Social Democratic Party (社会民主党), in 1901; the government banned it within days. He later served in the Diet and led the moderate socialist movement for years.

His influence is mostly invisible now, the way a building's foundations are invisible. But the idea that playing the game properly matters as much as winning, so visible at Kōshien, owes a great deal to Abe's generation of educators.

### Giants Versus Tigers — The Great Rivalry

The rivalry between the 巨人 (*Kyojin*, Giants) and 阪神 (*Hanshin*, the Tigers) is Japan's Yankees and Red Sox, with the added charge of the Tokyo–Osaka rivalry behind it.

- **The Giants** are based in Tokyo and owned by the Yomiuri newspaper group. With 22 Japan Series titles, they are the most decorated team in Japanese baseball, and plenty of people outside Tokyo love to hate them.
- **The Tigers** play at historic Kōshien Stadium in Nishinomiya, between Osaka and Kobe. They are defined more by their passionate fans than by their trophy case.

When the Tigers won the Japan Series in 2023, their first title in 38 years, Osaka went wild. Fans have a habit of jumping into the 道頓堀 (*Dōtonbori*) canal to celebrate, and the city now prepares for it with police lines and safety warnings.

### 甲子園 — High School Baseball's Cathedral

**Kōshien** (甲子園), Hanshin's stadium, is also the stage for national high school baseball, one of the most emotionally charged events in Japanese sport. Twice a year it hosts:

- 選抜高等学校野球大会 (*Senbatsu Kōtō Gakkō Yakyū Taikai*), "Spring Kōshien," in March, with invited teams.
- 全国高等学校野球選手権大会 (*Zenkoku Kōtō Gakkō Yakyū Senshuken Taikai*), "Summer Kōshien," in August, the national championship, with one or two champions from each prefecture.

NHK broadcasts every game. Small rural schools face urban powerhouses, and the whole country watches. Losing teams scoop up infield dirt to take home as a keepsake, 甲子園の土を持ち帰る (*Kōshien no tsuchi o mochikaeru*). Players cry, coaches cry, the crowd cries. A pitcher who dominates the summer tournament becomes a national hero overnight.

> 甲子園に行きたい。\
> *Kōshien ni ikitai.*\
> I want to go to Kōshien.

Every high school player in Japan has said that sentence. 高校野球 (*kōkō yakyū*, high school baseball) has a culture all its own:

- Many players shave their heads (丸刈り, *marugari*) as a sign of commitment, although the custom is fading. Keio High School won the 2023 summer title with players who wore their hair as they liked, which was widely discussed.
- The heavy workload of young pitchers has long been criticized. Since 2020 a pitcher has been limited to 500 pitches in any one week of a tournament.
- 「栄冠は君に輝く」 (*Eikan wa Kimi ni Kagayaku*, "The Crown of Glory Shines on You") is the summer tournament's anthem.

### Japan and the Major Leagues

Japan has sent players to Major League Baseball since 村上雅則 (*Murakami Masanori*) pitched for the San Francisco Giants in 1964, but the modern era is something else entirely:

- **野茂英雄 (*Nomo Hideo*, born 1968)**: broke through with the Los Angeles Dodgers in 1995. His twisting "tornado" delivery and immediate success opened the door for every Japanese player since.
- **イチロー (*Ichiro*, Suzuki Ichirō, born 1973)**: 3,089 MLB hits and 10 Gold Gloves with a precise, relentless style. In 2025 he became the first Japanese player elected to the National Baseball Hall of Fame, with 393 of 394 votes.
- **松井秀喜 (*Matsui Hideki*, born 1974)**: "Godzilla," a Giants star who became World Series MVP with the New York Yankees in 2009.
- **大谷翔平 (*Ōtani Shōhei*, born 1994)**: a two-way player, 投手 (*tōshu*, pitcher) and 打者 (*dasha*, batter), at an elite level, something not seen since Babe Ruth. In 2021 he won nine games and hit 46 home runs. His 10-year, $700 million contract with the Dodgers in December 2023 was the largest in North American sports history at the time, and in 2024 he became the first player to hit 50 home runs and steal 50 bases in one season. As of 2025 he has four MVP awards, all unanimous, and two World Series titles with the Dodgers (2024 and 2025). In Japan, his games are broadcast live in the morning and early afternoon, and his at-bats lead the news.

### Baseball Fan Culture

Japanese baseball fans are some of the most organized in the world:

- **応援団 (*ōendan*)**: cheering sections in the outfield stands with trumpets, drums and coordinated chants that never stop while their team bats.
- **応援歌 (*ōenka*)**: fight songs, including one for each regular player, sung in unison whenever he comes to bat.
- **Umbrellas**: Yakult Swallows fans wave small plastic umbrellas and sing 「東京音頭」 (*Tōkyō Ondo*) whenever their team scores.
- **Jet balloons**: Hanshin fans release long balloons (ジェット風船, *jetto fūsen*) into the air in the seventh inning.
- **応援グッズ (*ōen guzzu*)**: cheering goods such as megaphones, inflatable bat-shaped sticks and towels in team colors.
- **「六甲おろし」 (*Rokkō Oroshi*)**: the Tigers' anthem, sung by tens of thousands at Kōshien.

## 相撲 — Sumo

### The Ancient Sport

**Sumo** (相撲) is not only a sport. It grew out of 神事 (*shinji*, Shinto ritual), and its ceremonies are still Shinto at heart. The *Kojiki* story of two gods deciding possession of a land through a trial of strength is often cited as its mythical origin, and wrestling at shrines and at court is recorded more than a thousand years ago.

Nearly everything in a sumo bout has meaning:

- The **dohyō** (土俵), the clay ring, is a sacred space, rebuilt by hand before each tournament and purified in a Shinto ceremony.
- The roof hanging over the ring at major tournaments is built in the style of a Shinto shrine.
- The referee (行司, *gyōji*) dresses in colorful robes in a medieval style, and the highest-ranked referees carry a short sword. By tradition it symbolizes his readiness to take responsibility with his life for a wrong call.
- Wrestlers throw salt (塩, *shio*) to purify the ring before each bout.
- Each 横綱 (*yokozuna*, grand champion) performs a ring-entering ceremony (土俵入り, *dohyō-iri*) derived from Shinto ritual.

### The Ranking System — 番付

Sumo's rankings, the **banzuke** (番付), are rigid and public. A new ranking sheet (番付表, *banzuke-hyō*) is published before every tournament, and every wrestler's position depends on his last result.

| Rank or division | Reading | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 横綱 | yokozuna | Grand champion, the pinnacle. Cannot be demoted; a yokozuna who can no longer win is expected to retire |
| 大関 | ōzeki | Champion, the second rank; demoted after two losing tournaments in a row |
| 関脇 | sekiwake | The third rank |
| 小結 | komusubi | The fourth rank |
| 前頭 | maegashira | The rank and file of the top division, numbered from 1 down to about 17 |
| 十両 | jūryō | The second division; together with the top division, the only salaried wrestlers (関取, *sekitori*) |
| 幕下 | makushita | The third division; unsalaried trainees |
| 三段目 | sandanme | The fourth division |
| 序二段 | jonidan | The fifth division |
| 序ノ口 | jonokuchi | The sixth and lowest division |

The top division, **makuuchi** (幕内), covers yokozuna down to maegashira, a maximum of 42 wrestlers, and is what television shows in the late afternoon.

Promotion and demotion follow each tournament's record. A **kachikoshi** (勝ち越し), more wins than losses (eight or more out of 15), usually means promotion; a **makekoshi** (負け越し), more losses than wins, means demotion.

### The Six Grand Tournaments — 本場所

There are six **honbasho** (本場所), grand tournaments, a year:

| Tournament | Venue | Month |
|---|---|---|
| 初場所 (Hatsu Basho) | 両国国技館 (Ryōgoku Kokugikan), Tokyo | January |
| 春場所 (Haru Basho) | Edion Arena Osaka | March |
| 夏場所 (Natsu Basho) | Ryōgoku Kokugikan, Tokyo | May |
| 名古屋場所 (Nagoya Basho) | IG Arena, Nagoya (from 2025) | July |
| 秋場所 (Aki Basho) | Ryōgoku Kokugikan, Tokyo | September |
| 九州場所 (Kyūshū Basho) | 福岡国際センター (Fukuoka Kokusai Center) | November |

Each tournament lasts 15 days, and wrestlers in the top two divisions fight once a day. The wrestler with the best record wins the 優勝 (*yūshō*, championship), usually with 12 or 13 wins; a perfect 15–0 is rare and celebrated. Ties at the top are settled by a playoff on the final day.

The Ryōgoku Kokugikan in Tokyo is the spiritual home of the sport. Its box seats, 枡席 (*masuseki*), are small tatami-floored squares with cushions, sold as a box for a group, where you eat, drink and watch from the floor.

### A Bout: What's Actually Happening

A bout (取組, *torikumi*) often lasts only a few seconds, but everything before the charge is theater:

1. **土俵入り (*dohyō-iri*)**: before the top-division bouts begin, wrestlers enter the ring together wearing ornate embroidered aprons (化粧廻し, *keshō-mawashi*).
2. **四股 (*shiko*)**: the iconic leg-stamping, said to drive evil spirits into the ground.
3. **塩まき (*shio-maki*)**: throwing salt to purify the ring.
4. **仕切り (*shikiri*)**: the wrestlers crouch, glare at each other and rise, again and again, in a psychological duel of up to four minutes in the top division.
5. **立ち合い (*tachi-ai*)**: the simultaneous charge, the most important moment of the bout. A false start is called 待った (*matta*, "wait") and the wrestlers reset.

You win by forcing your opponent out of the ring or by making any part of his body other than the soles of his feet touch the ground. There are no weight classes and no rounds.

The Japan Sumo Association recognizes 82 winning techniques, **kimarite** (決まり手), plus a handful of non-technique results such as stepping out by accident. A few cover most bouts:

| 下手投げ | shitatenage | Underarm throw |
| 突き出し | tsukidashi | Thrust out |

> *Yorikiri de ōzeki no kachi.*\

### Legendary Yokozuna

Yokozuna is the most prestigious title in Japanese sport. Only 75 wrestlers have held it (as of 2026), and each defined an era:

- **双葉山 (*Futabayama*, 1912–1968)** won 69 consecutive bouts between 1936 and 1939, a record that still stands. When the streak ended, he sent a telegram to a mentor: 「イマダモクケイタリエズ」 (*imada mokkei tari-ezu*), "I have not yet become a wooden rooster," a reference to a Zhuangzi parable about a fighting cock so composed it seemed carved from wood.
- **千代の富士 (*Chiyonofuji*, 1955–2016)**, "the Wolf," was lean and muscular in a sport of giants and dominated the 1980s with technique and speed, winning 31 championships. His tearful retirement in 1991, 「体力の限界…」 ("I have reached the limit of my strength…"), is one of the most quoted moments in Japanese sport.
- **曙 (*Akebono*, 1969–2024)**, born in Hawaii, became the first foreign-born yokozuna in 1993, after a long public debate about whether a foreigner could embody the rank's dignity. His success opened the way for those who followed.
- **朝青龍 (*Asashōryū*, born 1980) and 白鵬 (*Hakuhō*, born 1985)** led an era of Mongolian dominance from the mid-2000s. Hakuhō's 45 championships, won before he retired in 2021, are the all-time record. He left the Japan Sumo Association in 2025.
- **照ノ富士 (*Terunofuji*, born 1991)** reached ōzeki in 2015, then fell as low as jonidan, the fifth division, through knee injuries and illness. He fought his way back up and was promoted to yokozuna in 2021, one of the great comebacks in Japanese sport. He retired in January 2025.

### Sumo and Contemporary Japan

For years fans worried about the lack of a Japanese-born yokozuna. 稀勢の里 (*Kisenosato*) was promoted in 2017 and retired in 2019, and no Japanese wrestler followed him until 2025, when 大の里 (*Ōnosato*) became the 75th yokozuna in record time, just two years after his professional debut. He was the second promotion that year, after the Mongolian-born 豊昇龍 (*Hōshōryū*) became the 74th in January.

Sumo is often called Japan's 国技 (*kokugi*, national sport), and the Tokyo arena is literally the Kokugikan, "hall of the national sport." Yet the top ranks have long included wrestlers from Mongolia, Georgia, Bulgaria, Hawaii and, recently, Ukraine: 安青錦 (*Aonishiki*) won his first championship and was promoted to ōzeki in November 2025. What "national" means has been a live question for decades.

The Japan Sumo Association has worked to reach younger fans through social media and online highlights, and visitors from abroad now fill many seats. Some stables allow visitors to watch morning practice (朝稽古, *asageiko*) if they arrive quietly and follow the rules, an unforgettable sight.

### Watching Sumo: What to Know

At a tournament:

- **枡席 (*masuseki*)**: box seats on tatami, the classic experience.
- **椅子席 (*isu-seki*)**: ordinary chairs higher up, cheaper and easier on the knees.
- **当日券 (*tōjitsuken*)**: a limited number of same-day tickets go on sale each morning.
- Lower-division bouts start in the morning; the top division's ring-entering ceremony is in the late afternoon, so arrive by about 3:30 p.m. to see the whole top-division program.
- 千秋楽 (*senshūraku*), the final day, has the most drama and a closing ceremony.

On television, NHK broadcasts every day of all six tournaments live, with its calm, knowledgeable commentary a small institution of its own.

Moments not to miss:

- **横綱土俵入り (*yokozuna dohyō-iri*)**: the grand champion's ring-entering ceremony, with a sword bearer and a dew sweeper.
- **The final-day showdown**: the bout, or playoff, that decides the championship.
- **懸賞金 (*kenshōkin*)**: before popular bouts, the yobidashi parade sponsors' banners around the ring, and the winner receives the envelopes of prize money.

## What the Two Sports Mean to Japan

The two sports occupy different emotional territory.

Baseball is Japan as it chose to become: the Meiji-era embrace of the foreign, turned into something deeply its own. It carries regional pride (Hanshin against the Giants), youth and possibility (Kōshien) and, lately, global triumph (Ohtani).

Sumo is Japan as it has long been: ritual, Shinto, salt and topknots, carrying centuries in every bout. And through its foreign champions it keeps asking what being Japanese means.

> 野球は国民的娯楽、相撲は国技。\
> *Yakyū wa kokuminteki goraku, sumō wa kokugi.*\
> Baseball is the national pastime; sumo is the national sport.

## Summary

- Baseball reached Japan in 1872; educators such as Abe Isoo, "the father of Japanese baseball," shaped it into a game of character and discipline, and NPB today has 12 teams in the Central and Pacific Leagues.
- The Giants–Tigers rivalry mirrors Tokyo against Osaka, and the spring and summer high school tournaments at Kōshien are national events broadcast in full by NHK.
- Japanese players from Nomo and Ichiro to Ohtani, a two-way star with four unanimous MVP awards as of 2025, have transformed Major League Baseball.
- Sumo grew out of Shinto ritual; wrestlers are ranked on the banzuke from yokozuna down through six divisions, and a winning record (kachikoshi) of eight or more wins out of 15 means promotion.
- Six 15-day grand tournaments (honbasho) are held each year, three in Tokyo and one each in Osaka, Nagoya and Fukuoka; the best record wins the yūshō.
