# Traditional Music — Breath, Silence and Strings

Every January, in department stores, train stations and New Year television specials all over Japan, the same gentle duet for koto and shakuhachi floats out of the speakers. It is 春の海 (*Haru no Umi*, "The Sea in Spring"), written in 1929, and for most Japanese people it *is* the sound of New Year. You may not know its name yet, but if you spend a January in Japan you will hum it by the 3rd.

Japanese traditional music does not follow the rules of Western classical music. Its scales are different, its rhythms often follow breath rather than a fixed beat, and silence carries as much weight as sound. It is still alive at shrines, festivals, theaters, concert halls and school sports days, and its vocabulary turns up in cultural reading passages, news stories and New Year conversation.

## The Aesthetics of Japanese Music

Three ideas help before you meet a single instrument.

間 (*ma*), the meaningful pause you met in [section 8.1](01-traditional-arts.md), is central. The silence between notes is deliberate. Japanese music breathes, and a performer who rushes through the gaps has missed the point.

Wabi-sabi applies to sound too. A breathy, wavering flute note or a voice that cracks at the peak of a phrase is not a failure; it is expression.

**自然 (*shizen*, nature)** is a constant source. Many pieces imitate or evoke rain, wind, water, birds and insects, and the shakuhachi in particular is prized for a tone that sounds like breath itself.

## 雅楽 — Court Music

**Gagaku** (雅楽, "elegant music") is the ancient court music of the imperial household. It arrived from China and Korea in the 7th and 8th centuries, was organized under an official music bureau in 701, and has been performed at court ever since, which is why it is often described as the oldest surviving orchestral tradition in the world.

### What It Sounds Like

To ears raised on Western music, gagaku is slow, spacious and strange. A single phrase can stretch over many breaths, and sustained chords hang in the air like incense. Give it a few minutes and it produces a remarkable sense of suspended time.

### Instruments

The gagaku ensemble combines winds, strings and percussion:

| Instrument | Reading | Type | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| 笙 | shō | Wind | Mouth organ of 17 bamboo pipes; plays sustained chords |
| 篳篥 | hichiriki | Wind | Small double-reed pipe; carries the melody |
| 龍笛 | ryūteki | Wind | Transverse "dragon flute" |
| 琵琶 | biwa | String | Short-necked lute |
| 箏 | sō / koto | String | 13-string zither |
| 太鼓 | taiko | Percussion | Large hanging drum |
| 鞨鼓 | kakko | Percussion | Small barrel drum that leads the ensemble |
| 鉦鼓 | shōko | Percussion | Small bronze gong |

### Where to Hear It

The Music Department of the Imperial Household Agency (宮内庁式部職楽部, *Kunaichō Shikibushoku Gakubu*) performs at imperial ceremonies and gives public concerts, usually in spring and autumn. Major shrines and temples such as 春日大社 (*Kasuga Taisha*) in Nara, 四天王寺 (*Shitennō-ji*) and 住吉大社 (*Sumiyoshi Taisha*) in Osaka also hold gagaku and court-dance performances at their festivals.

## 能楽 — The Music of Noh

Noh theater (see [section 8.1](01-traditional-arts.md)) has its own small ensemble, the 囃子 (*hayashi*), of one flute and three drums:

| Instrument | Reading | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 能管 | nōkan | Flute with a piercing, eerie high tone |
| 小鼓 | kotsuzumi | Small hourglass drum held at the shoulder |
| 大鼓 | ōtsuzumi | Larger hourglass drum held at the hip |
| 太鼓 | taiko | Stick drum set on a stand |

The drummers also shout calls (掛け声, *kakegoe*) such as 「ヨー」 and 「ホー」 that mark the rhythm, and they are as much part of the music as the drums.

The vocal part is 謡 (*utai*), a highly stylized chant somewhere between song and speech, with texts drawn from classical poetry and literature.

## 箏 — Koto

The **koto** (箏) is Japan's iconic string instrument, a long zither of about 180 cm with, traditionally, 13 strings. The player plucks with picks (爪, *tsume*) worn on three fingers of the right hand and bends notes by pressing the strings with the left hand behind the movable bridges.

八橋検校 (*Yatsuhashi Kengyō*, 1614–1685), a blind musician, established the classical solo koto tradition. The piece attributed to him, 六段の調 (*Rokudan no Shirabe*, "Music in Six Sections"), remains one of the defining works of Japanese music and a standard of every koto student.

### The Modern Koto

The 20th century pushed the koto in new directions:

- **宮城道雄 (*Miyagi Michio*, 1894–1956)**: a blind koto master who absorbed Western influences, invented a 17-string bass koto and composed *Haru no Umi*, the New Year piece.
- **沢井忠夫 (*Sawai Tadao*) and 沢井一恵 (*Sawai Kazue*)**: a husband-and-wife pair who carried the koto into contemporary and experimental music.
- Today the koto also appears in jazz, pop and film scores.

## 三味線 — Shamisen

The **shamisen** (三味線) is a three-stringed, long-necked lute with a skin-covered body, played with a large plectrum (撥, *bachi*). Its ancestor, the Okinawan 三線 (*sanshin*), came from China through the Ryūkyū Kingdom, and the instrument reached mainland Japan in the 16th century. It became the voice of Edo popular culture: kabuki, puppet theater, geisha entertainment and folk song.

### Types and Uses

Shamisen come in three neck widths, each tied to its own genres:

| Type | Neck | Music |
|---|---|---|
| 細棹 (hosozao) | Thin | 長唄 (*nagauta*, "long song"), the music of kabuki dance |
| 中棹 (chūzao) | Medium | 地歌 (*jiuta*), 民謡 (*min'yō*, folk song) and 小唄 (*kouta*, short songs) |
| 太棹 (futozao) | Thick | 義太夫節 (*gidayū-bushi*, puppet-theater narrative) and Tsugaru-jamisen |

### Tsugaru-Jamisen

The 津軽三味線 (*Tsugaru-jamisen*) of the Tsugaru region in northern Aomori is, for many modern listeners, the most thrilling style of all: fast, percussive and improvised, with the plectrum slapped against the skin. It developed from the playing of blind itinerant musicians and sounds closer to a rock guitar solo than to anything you would expect from a "traditional" instrument.

The duo 吉田兄弟 (*Yoshida Kyōdai*, the Yoshida Brothers) and the soloist 上妻宏光 (*Agatsuma Hiromitsu*) are well-known players who mix Tsugaru-jamisen with rock, jazz and electronic music.

## 尺八 — Shakuhachi

The **shakuhachi** (尺八) is an end-blown bamboo flute. Its name is its length: 一尺八寸 (*isshaku hassun*), one *shaku* and eight *sun*, about 54.5 cm. The player blows across a sharp edge at the top, as you might blow across a bottle, and controls pitch with the angle of the lips as much as with the five finger holes. The result is breathy, haunting and deeply human.

In the Edo period the shakuhachi was the instrument of the monks of the 普化宗 (*Fuke-shū*), who played it as meditation rather than performance, a practice called 吹禅 (*suizen*, "blowing Zen"). Its solo repertoire, 本曲 (*honkyoku*, "original pieces"), is often slow and meditative.

The shakuhachi has since found a home in jazz, ambient music and film scores. Riley Lee, an American-born grandmaster now based in Australia, and 柿堺香 (*Kakizakai Kaoru*) are internationally known performers in both classical and contemporary styles.

## 演歌 — Enka

**Enka** (演歌) is Japan's sentimental ballad genre, often compared to the blues or to classic country music. The word dates back to the Meiji period, when 演説歌 (*enzetsuka*, "speech songs") carried political messages, but enka as a distinct popular style took shape in the 1960s and 1970s.

### Characteristics

You will recognize enka within a few bars:

- Minor keys and a five-note scale that sounds instantly old-fashioned to Japanese ears.
- **Kobushi** (こぶし), the signature vocal ornament: a quick, wavering rise and fall on a single syllable, more abrupt than Western vibrato.
- Lyrics about lost love, the cold north, sake, harbors and fishing villages, the loneliness of the road (旅, *tabi*), a mother's love and stoic endurance.
- Kimono or formal suits and a dramatic stage presence.

### Key Artists

Five names every enka fan knows:

| Artist | Notes |
|---|---|
| 美空ひばり (Misora Hibari) | The towering figure of postwar song, across both 歌謡曲 (*kayōkyoku*) and enka |
| 北島三郎 (Kitajima Saburō) | "Sabu-chan," one of the best-loved male enka singers |
| 都はるみ (Miyako Harumi) | Famous for a powerful voice and intense kobushi |
| 細川たかし (Hosokawa Takashi) | His 「矢切の渡し」 (*Yagiri no Watashi*, 1983) is a classic |
| 天童よしみ (Tendō Yoshimi) | Celebrated for an exceptionally strong voice |

Enka is strongly associated with older listeners, and the year-end music show 紅白歌合戦 (*Kōhaku Uta Gassen*) still gives it a prominent place. Being able to mention a Misora Hibari song is a lovely way to connect with an older Japanese person.

### Enka Vocabulary

Enka lyrics are dramatic but linguistically rich, full of literary and slightly old-fashioned words:

| Word | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 旅 | tabi | Journey, travels |
| 演歌 | enka | Enka (演 "perform" + 歌 "song") |
| 望郷 | bōkyō | Longing for home |
| 哀愁 | aishū | Pathos, wistful sadness |
| 盃 | sakazuki | Sake cup |
| 涙 | namida | Tears |
| 故郷 | furusato | Hometown, birthplace |

> 故郷を離れて、もう十年。\
> *Furusato o hanarete, mō jūnen.*\
> Ten years now since I left my hometown.

## 民謡 — Folk Song

**Min'yō** (民謡) is Japan's regional folk music: work songs, festival songs, fishermen's chants and harvest songs. Almost every prefecture has its own, often sung at summer Bon dances (see [section 7.3](../07-customs-and-daily-life/03-matsuri.md)).

| Region | Famous song | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Okinawa | 安里屋ユンタ (*Asadoya Yunta*) | From the Yaeyama Islands; Okinawan scale and sanshin |
| Akita | 秋田音頭 (*Akita Ondo*) | Lively, comic festival song |
| Hokkaido | ソーラン節 (*Sōran Bushi*) | Herring fishermen's work song |
| Kagoshima | おはら節 (*Ohara Bushi*) | Kagoshima's best-known folk song |
| Niigata | 佐渡おけさ (*Sado Okesa*) | From Sado Island |

*Sōran Bushi* has had a second life. A high-energy rock-dance version, created at a Hokkaido junior high school in the early 1990s, spread across the country, and students now perform it at sports days and school festivals everywhere. The fishermen's chant 「ヤーレン、ソーラン、ソーラン」 is recognizable to almost anyone who went to school in Japan.

## 太鼓 — Taiko Drumming

**Taiko** (太鼓) means "drum," and in Japan it ranges from quiet shrine accompaniment to full-body ensemble performance. 和太鼓 (*wadaiko*, Japanese drums) are played at festivals, in noh and gagaku, and in religious ceremonies.

The modern ensemble style, with many drummers playing together on stage, took off after the war. The 鼓童 (*Kodō*) ensemble from Sado Island, founded in 1981, made it famous around the world with performances of extraordinary stamina.

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 長胴太鼓 | nagadō-daiko | Long-bodied barrel drum, the classic big drum |
| 桶胴太鼓 | okedō-daiko | Lightweight stave drum, easy to carry |
| 締太鼓 | shime-daiko | Small rope- or bolt-tensioned drum with a high pitch |
| 鉦 | kane | Small hand gong played with the drums |
| 撥 / バチ | bachi | Drumsticks |

## Traditional Music in Your Reading

Traditional music is a favorite topic for cultural reading passages in textbooks and intermediate to advanced exams, and for NHK features around New Year and the festival season. The terms that come up most are worth recognizing on sight:

> 雅楽、能楽、歌舞伎、民謡、三味線、箏、尺八、演歌、太鼓\
> *gagaku, nōgaku, kabuki, min'yō, shamisen, koto, shakuhachi, enka, taiko*\
> court music, noh, kabuki, folk song, shamisen, koto, shakuhachi, enka, drums

Even passive recognition pays off: a passage about a shrine festival or a regional song is far less intimidating when you know the instruments by name.

## Summary

- Japanese traditional music values ma (meaningful silence), natural sounds and expressive imperfection over steady Western meter.
- Gagaku is the imperial court music, organized at court in 701 and performed there ever since; noh has its own ensemble of one flute and three drums.
- The koto is a 13-string zither; Yatsuhashi Kengyō founded its classical tradition and Miyagi Michio's *Haru no Umi* (1929) is the sound of New Year.
- The shamisen is a three-stringed lute central to Edo culture, with the fast, percussive Tsugaru-jamisen as its most exciting modern style; the shakuhachi is an end-blown bamboo flute once played as Zen meditation.
- Enka is the sentimental ballad genre beloved by older listeners, marked by kobushi ornaments and themes of travel, longing and sake; min'yō are regional folk songs, and *Sōran Bushi* lives on as a school dance.
