# How Japanese Evolved — From Man'yōgana to Emoji

Why does Japanese use three scripts at once? Why does 行 have three different on-readings? Why does the particle は sound like "wa"? Every one of these quirks is a fossil: a trace of a moment, sometimes more than a thousand years ago, when the language changed. Learn the history and the quirks stop being arbitrary.

Modern Japanese is the result of some 1,300 years of written history: borrowing, adapting, reforming and inventing. This section follows that story from the first poems written in borrowed Chinese characters to the emoji on your phone.

## The Oldest Japanese

Japanese had no writing system of its own. When Chinese writing arrived, scribes first wrote in Chinese, then began bending Chinese characters to write Japanese. The oldest surviving Japanese texts date from the 8th century:

- 『古事記』 (*Kojiki*, Records of Ancient Matters, 712): Japan's oldest surviving chronicle, written in a Japanized Chinese style, with songs and names spelled out in sound.
- 『日本書紀』 (*Nihon Shoki*, Chronicles of Japan, 720): a more formal official history, written mostly in classical Chinese.
- 『万葉集』 (*Man'yōshū*, Collection of Ten Thousand Leaves): the oldest anthology of Japanese poetry, compiled in the late 8th century; its latest datable poem is from 759.

The Man'yōshū gave its name to **man'yōgana** (万葉仮名): kanji used purely for their sound, ignoring their meaning. To write the word やま (mountain), a scribe might use 夜麻, characters meaning "night" and "hemp". It worked, but it was slow, and a single word could take several complex characters.

## Classical Japanese

The written language of the Heian period (794–1185) became the basis of **bungo** (文語, classical written Japanese), the standard for formal writing until the late 19th century. Its grammar differs enough from modern Japanese that Japanese high school students study it as a separate subject, 古文 (*kobun*, classical texts).

| Classical | Modern equivalent | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| あり | ある | to exist, be |
| なり | だ / である | is (copula) |
| けり | 〜た (often "it turns out") | past, with a sense of realization |
| ず | ない | not |
| ぬ | 〜た / 〜てしまった | completed action |
| べし | べきだ | should, must |

> **Note:** ぬ is a famous trap. As an ending meaning "completed", it looks identical to ぬ the attributive form of the negative ず, as in 知らぬ (not knowing). Context, and the form of the verb before it, tell them apart.

Classical grammar still surfaces in modern Japanese: in haiku and tanka, school songs, the national anthem, signs (立入るべからず, no entry), and proverbs such as 知らぬが仏 (*shiranu ga hotoke*, ignorance is bliss) and 急がば回れ (*isogaba maware*, more haste, less speed).

## The Kana Revolution

During the Heian period, writers simplified man'yōgana into two new scripts that wrote sounds quickly.

Hiragana grew out of cursive, flowing forms of whole characters:

| Hiragana | From | Hiragana | From |
|---|---|---|---|
| あ | 安 | え | 衣 |
| い | 以 | お | 於 |
| う | 宇 | か | 加 |

Formal writing by men was still expected to be in Chinese, while hiragana was used for poetry, letters and diaries and was strongly associated with women writers; it was sometimes called 女手 (*onnade*, "women's hand"). The great works of Heian literature were written largely in kana, including 『源氏物語』 (*The Tale of Genji*) by the court lady Murasaki Shikibu in the early 11th century, often called the world's first novel.

Katakana came from Buddhist monks, who took a small piece of a character as a quick reading aid when annotating Chinese texts: ア from the left side of 阿, イ from the left side of 伊, カ from part of 加.

Over the following centuries, writers learned to mix kanji with kana in a single text. Kanji carried content words; kana carried grammar such as particles and verb endings. By the Kamakura period (1185–1333), this mixed style was established, and it is still the basis of written Japanese. Katakana's modern main job, writing foreign loanwords, came much later.

## Layers of Chinese Readings

Japanese did not borrow from Chinese all at once. Different waves of contact, from different regions and centuries, brought different pronunciations of the same character. That is why many kanji have more than one 音読み (*on'yomi*, Chinese-derived reading).

| Layer | When it arrived | Source | 明 | 行 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 呉音 (*go-on*) | 5th–6th centuries | southern China, often via Korea; strong in Buddhist words | みょう (明日, *myōnichi*) | ぎょう (行列, *gyōretsu*, a line) |
| 漢音 (*kan-on*) | 7th–9th centuries | Tang-dynasty Chang'an, brought back by envoys and students | めい (明確, *meikaku*) | こう (銀行, *ginkō*, bank) |
| 唐音 (*tō-on*) | from the 12th century | Song, Ming and Qing China, through Zen monks and traders | みん (明朝, *minchō*, Ming-style typeface) | あん (行灯, *andon*, paper lantern) |

So when you meet 行 as ぎょう in one word and こう in another, you are hearing two different centuries of Chinese.

## The Meiji Language Revolution

The Meiji period (1868–1912) changed Japanese more rapidly than any time since the invention of kana. Japan was modernizing at speed, and it needed words for concepts it had never had to name.

### New Kanji Compounds

Translators and scholars answered with **wasei kango** (和製漢語, Japan-made Sino-Japanese words): new compounds built from kanji, or old Chinese words given new Western meanings.

| Concept | Japanese | Reading | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| philosophy | 哲学 | tetsugaku | coined by the scholar Nishi Amane |
| science | 科学 | kagaku | |
| society | 社会 | shakai | an old word, repurposed |
| economy | 経済 | keizai | from 経世済民, "governing the world and relieving the people" |
| individual | 個人 | kojin | |
| liberty | 自由 | jiyū | an old word given a political meaning |
| democracy | 民主主義 | minshu shugi | |
| telephone | 電話 | denwa | "electric speech" |
| culture | 文化 | bunka | an old word, repurposed |

Many of these words then traveled back to China, where they are standard vocabulary today. Chinese 民主 (democracy) and 經濟 (economy, the Japanese 経済) in their modern senses passed through Japanese first.

### Loanwords

For other words, especially in technology, food and daily life, Japanese borrowed the sound and wrote it in katakana. These borrowings are called **gairaigo** (外来語, loanwords from Western languages), and they began well before Meiji.

| Word | From | When it arrived |
|---|---|---|
| パン (pan, bread) | Portuguese *pão* | 16th century, with Portuguese traders and missionaries |
| コーヒー (kōhī, coffee) | Dutch *koffie* | Edo period, through the Dutch trading post at Nagasaki |
| ビール (bīru, beer) | Dutch *bier* | Edo period |
| アルバイト (arubaito, part-time job) | German *Arbeit* | Meiji and after, through students |
| テレビ (terebi, TV) | English "television" | 1950s; broadcasting began in 1953 |
| スマホ (sumaho, smartphone) | English "smartphone" | 2010s |

### A Standard Language

The Meiji government also built a national language, 国語 (*kokugo*), based on educated Tokyo speech. Schools taught it everywhere, and regional dialects were actively discouraged in the classroom; in Okinawa and elsewhere, children caught speaking dialect could be made to wear a punishment tag, the 方言札 (*hōgen fuda*). Literacy rose, and newspapers and novels spread the new standard.

At the same time came **genbun itchi** (言文一致, "unifying speech and writing"), a movement to write in something close to the spoken language instead of classical bungo. Futabatei Shimei's novel 『浮雲』 (*Ukigumo*, Drifting Clouds, 1887) is a landmark of the movement. Before it, formal writing and everyday speech were far apart; afterward, they converged, and the です・ます and である styles of [section 4.1](../04-spoken-japanese/01-spoken-vs-written.md) took shape.

## Postwar Script Reforms

After World War II, the government simplified the writing system in a series of reforms. They explain much of what you learned in [chapter 1](../01-writing-system/README.md).

| Year | Reform | What it did |
|---|---|---|
| 1946 | 現代かなづかい (*gendai kanazukai*) | Respelled kana by modern pronunciation: ゐ and ゑ dropped out of use, replaced by い and え; but the particles は, へ and を kept their old spellings |
| 1946 | 当用漢字 (*tōyō kanji*) | Limited kanji for general use to 1,850 characters |
| 1949 | 当用漢字字体表 | Officially adopted simplified character forms (新字体, *shinjitai*), such as 国 for 國 |
| 1981 | 常用漢字 (*jōyō kanji*) | Replaced the 1946 list with 1,945 characters as a guide rather than a limit |
| 2010 | 常用漢字 revision | Expanded the list to 2,136 characters, the list still in use as of 2026 |

That is why the topic particle は is still written with the kana for "ha": the 1946 reform modernized spelling everywhere except the particles, which kept their historical forms.

## The Postwar Loanword Boom

After 1945, English became by far the largest source of new loanwords, and each era left its own layer.

- 1940s and 1950s, the occupation and recovery: ジープ (jeep), ベビーブーム (baby boom).
- 1960s and 1970s, mass media and consumer goods: テレビ, コンピューター (computer), スーパー (supermarket).
- 1980s and 1990s, the bubble economy and its collapse: バブル (bubble), リストラ (restructuring, meaning layoffs), セクハラ (sexual harassment, a buzzword winner in 1989).
- 2000s and 2010s, the internet age: パワハラ (power harassment, coined around 2001), スマホ, シェア (share), バズる (to go viral).

Loanwords now arrive faster than ever, and [section 5.2](02-contemporary-japanese.md) follows the latest ones.

## Japanese Words That Went Global

Borrowing runs both ways. These Japanese words are now part of English:

| Japanese | Reading | English |
|---|---|---|
| 津波 | tsunami | tsunami |
| 侍 | samurai | samurai |
| 将軍 | shōgun | shogun |
| 禅 | zen | Zen |
| 柔道 / 空手 | jūdō / karate | judo / karate |
| 俳句 | haiku | haiku |
| 盆栽 | bonsai | bonsai |
| 布団 | futon | futon |
| 寿司 | sushi | sushi |
| 旨味 | umami | umami |
| かわいい | kawaii | kawaii |

## The Emoji Story

絵文字 (*emoji*) combines 絵 (*e*, picture) and 文字 (*moji*, character). The best-known early set was designed in 1999 by Shigetaka Kurita for NTT Docomo's i-mode mobile internet service: 176 simple 12-by-12-pixel icons. (An earlier set of about 90 symbols had appeared on a J-Phone handset in 1997.) Emoji entered the Unicode standard in 2010 and spread worldwide after Apple added an emoji keyboard to iOS in 2011. Kurita's original set is now in the collection of the Museum of Modern Art in New York.

It is a fitting end point. Japanese began by borrowing a writing system from abroad, and it has now exported one of the world's most widely used sets of symbols back.

## Where Japanese Is Heading

A few trends are clear as of 2026.

- Gendered speech styles are fading among younger speakers ([section 4.1](../04-spoken-japanese/01-spoken-vs-written.md) and [section 4.3](../04-spoken-japanese/03-role-language.md)).
- English loanwords keep multiplying. Some people worry about "katakana overload", especially in government and business documents; others see ordinary borrowing, as Japanese has always done.
- Input methods have changed how people write. Most Japanese is now typed phonetically and converted to kanji by software, so many people can read far more kanji than they can write by hand.
- Japan's population is shrinking, but interest in the language abroad is high, carried by anime, games, music and travel.

The language has been changing for as long as it has been written down, and it will keep changing. Your job as a learner is to learn it as it is now, and to enjoy the traces of everything it has been.

## Summary

- The oldest Japanese texts, the Kojiki (712), Nihon Shoki (720) and Man'yōshū (late 8th century), wrote Japanese with Chinese characters, often used only for their sounds (man'yōgana).
- Hiragana developed from cursive forms of whole characters and katakana from parts of characters, both during the Heian period (794–1185).
- Kanji have several on-readings because Chinese pronunciations arrived in waves: go-on, kan-on and later tō-on.
- In the Meiji period, Japan coined kanji compounds such as 哲学 and 経済 for Western ideas, built a Tokyo-based standard language and began writing in a style close to speech (genbun itchi).
- Postwar reforms in 1946 respelled kana and limited kanji; the current jōyō list, revised in 2010, has 2,136 characters.
- Borrowing runs both ways: Japanese took パン, コーヒー and スマホ from abroad and gave the world words such as tsunami, kawaii and emoji.
