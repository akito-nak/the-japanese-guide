# Kanji — The Deep End of the Pool

Write かみ (*kami*) on a piece of paper and ask a Japanese friend what it means. They will ask you right back: which *kami*? It could be hair, a god, paper or "upper", and spoken Japanese leans on context to tell them apart. Written Japanese has a better tool:

| Kanji | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 髪 | かみ | hair |
| 神 | かみ | god, spirit |
| 紙 | かみ | paper |
| 上 | かみ | upper, above (in some words) |

Kanji (漢字, "Han characters") are the characters Japanese borrowed from Chinese, starting more than 1,500 years ago, and adapted over the centuries since. Each one carries a meaning, and most carry one or more readings. Japanese has a great many words that sound the same, and kanji let the eye tell them apart instantly, which is a large part of why the language never switched to kana alone. They are beautiful, occasionally maddening and absolutely essential to reading real Japanese.

How many do you need? The Japanese government's list of kanji for everyday use, the **jōyō kanji** (常用漢字), has contained 2,136 characters since its 2010 revision, and it is what schools teach and newspapers mostly stick to. That number sounds enormous, but you do not need all of it to start reading, and every kanji you learn makes the next one easier, because kanji are built from a limited set of recurring parts.

## Anatomy of a Kanji

### Radicals

Most kanji are built from smaller components, and one of those components is the kanji's **radical** (部首, *bushu*): the part used to file it in a dictionary. Traditional dictionaries use a set of 214 radicals inherited from the Chinese Kangxi dictionary of 1716.

Radicals do two jobs for a learner:

1. **Lookup.** Kanji dictionaries group characters by radical, and many apps let you search by components.
2. **Meaning hints.** The radical often, though not always, signals the general area of meaning. Anything with the water radical 氵 probably has something to do with water.

Many radicals change shape when they squeeze into the side or top of a kanji: 水 (water) becomes 氵 on the left, 人 (person) becomes 亻, 手 (hand) becomes 扌 and 心 (heart) becomes 忄. Learning these compressed forms is what turns kanji from random squiggles into decodable parts.

## Stroke Order for Kanji

The seven rules from [section 1.1](01-hiragana.md) all apply to kanji, and here every one of them earns its keep:

| Rule | Example | How it applies |
|---|---|---|
| Top to bottom | 三 (three) | Top line, middle line, bottom line |
| Left to right | 川 (river) | Left stroke, center, right |
| Horizontal before vertical | 十 (ten) | The horizontal, then the vertical crossing it |
| Left-falling before right-falling | 人 (person) | The stroke sweeping down to the left, then the one to the right |
| Center before sides | 小 (small) | The center vertical, then the left dot, then the right dot |
| Frame, contents, then close | 国 (country) | Left side, top and right side, the contents, then the bottom last |
| One stroke means one movement | 口 (mouth) | The top and right side of the box are a single stroke that turns a corner |

### Enclosures

Enclosing shapes come up constantly, and each type has its own order:

- **A full box (口, 国, 回).** Left side; top and right side in one stroke; the contents; the bottom line last, sealing the box.
- **A frame open at the bottom (円, 同, 内).** Left side; top and right side in one stroke, ending in a hook; then the contents. There is nothing to seal.
- **A frame open on the right (区, 医).** The top line; then the contents; then the left side and bottom in one L-shaped stroke, last of all.

### Four Worked Examples

**日 (sun, day), 4 strokes.** (1) Left vertical; (2) top and right side in one stroke; (3) the middle horizontal; (4) the bottom horizontal, closing the box.

**山 (mountain), 3 strokes.** (1) The tall center vertical; (2) the left vertical, which turns right along the bottom in the same stroke; (3) the right vertical. Center first, as rule 5 says.

**木 (tree), 4 strokes.** (1) Horizontal; (2) the vertical crossing it; (3) the diagonal down to the left; (4) the diagonal down to the right.

**国 (country), 8 strokes.** (1) Left side; (2) top and right side; (3 to 7) the five strokes of 玉 inside, ending with its small dot; (8) the bottom line to close.

## On-Readings and Kun-Readings

Here is where most beginners get confused, so take it slowly. Most kanji have two kinds of reading:

| Type | Origin | Typically used |
|---|---|---|
| **On-yomi** (音読み, "sound reading") | An approximation of the Chinese pronunciation when the kanji was borrowed | In compounds of two or more kanji |
| **Kun-yomi** (訓読み, "meaning reading") | A native Japanese word that was matched to the kanji's meaning | When a kanji stands alone or is followed by hiragana |

Dictionaries conventionally write on-yomi in katakana and kun-yomi in hiragana, which is a handy reminder of which is which.

Three examples show the pattern:

- **山 (mountain).** Kun-yomi やま (*yama*), the word "mountain" on its own. On-yomi さん (*san*) or ざん (*zan*), as in 富士山 (ふじさん, *Fujisan*, Mount Fuji) and 火山 (かざん, *kazan*, volcano).
- **水 (water).** Kun-yomi みず (*mizu*), water on its own. On-yomi すい (*sui*), as in 水曜日 (すいようび, *suiyōbi*, Wednesday) and 水泳 (すいえい, *suiei*, swimming).
- **人 (person).** Kun-yomi ひと (*hito*), a person. On-yomi じん (*jin*) or にん (*nin*), as in 外国人 (がいこくじん, *gaikokujin*, foreigner) and 三人 (さんにん, *sannin*, three people).

> **Tip:** As a first guess, read a kanji standing alone or followed by hiragana with its kun-yomi, and a kanji in a compound with its on-yomi. The guess is right often enough to be useful, and the exceptions are learned word by word.

The hiragana trailing after a kanji, as in 食べる (たべる, *taberu*, to eat) or 飲みます (のみます, *nomimasu*, drink), is called **okurigana** (送り仮名). It carries the grammatical ending, so the kanji can stay the same while the verb changes form.

A few words ignore both systems and give a whole compound a reading of its own. 明日 (*ashita*, tomorrow) is read as one word, not as the sum of 明 (bright) and 日 (day). These special readings go on your list as vocabulary.

## Compounds: Where It Gets Fun

Most kanji vocabulary comes as **jukugo** (熟語), compounds of two or more kanji whose meanings combine, usually logically, sometimes poetically:

| Compound | Parts | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 電車 | electricity + vehicle | でんしゃ *densha* | train |
| 新聞 | new + hear | しんぶん *shinbun* | newspaper |
| 図書館 | drawing + writing + building | としょかん *toshokan* | library |
| 大学 | big + learning | だいがく *daigaku* | university |
| 飛行機 | fly + go + machine | ひこうき *hikōki* | airplane |
| 自動車 | self + move + vehicle | じどうしゃ *jidōsha* | car |
| 入口 | enter + mouth | いりぐち *iriguchi* | entrance |
| 出口 | exit + mouth | でぐち *deguchi* | exit |
| 地震 | earth + shake | じしん *jishin* | earthquake |

食べ物 (たべもの, *tabemono*, food) shows a mixed type: eat + okurigana + thing, all kun-yomi.

> **Tip:** Learn 入口 and 出口 before your first trip to Japan. They are on every station, shop and building, and in a big station they are the difference between leaving in two minutes and touring the basement for 20.

## Kanji and Kana Together

Ordinary Japanese mixes all three scripts in a single sentence, each doing the job it is best at:

> 私は毎日コーヒーを飲みます。\
> *Watashi wa mainichi kōhī o nomimasu.*\
> I drink coffee every day.

| Part | Script | Job |
|---|---|---|
| 私 | kanji | "I": a content word |
| は | hiragana | topic particle |
| 毎日 | kanji | "every day": a content word |
| コーヒー | katakana | "coffee": a loanword |
| を | hiragana | object particle |
| 飲みます | kanji plus hiragana | "drink": kanji stem, okurigana ending |

Kanji carry the meaning, hiragana carry the grammar, and katakana flag the borrowed words. Once your eye learns this rhythm, it uses the switches between scripts to find word boundaries, which is why Japanese needs no spaces.

## How Many Kanji, and Which

The JLPT (the Japanese-Language Proficiency Test, covered in [chapter 12](../12-the-jlpt/README.md)) has not published official kanji lists since its 2010 revision. Learners and textbook publishers use estimates based on the older test's lists and on past papers, roughly as follows:

| Level | Approximate kanji | What that lets you read |
|---|---|---|
| N5 | about 100 | Numbers, days of the week, basic signs |
| N4 | about 300 | Simple sentences and everyday words |
| N3 | about 650 | Graded readers, simple articles, menus |
| N2 | about 1,000 | A good share of newspaper text, with effort |
| N1 | about 2,000 | Most general text |

The full jōyō list is the realistic target for comfortable adult reading.

## Summary

- Kanji are meaning-carrying characters borrowed from Chinese; the jōyō list of 2,136 kanji (since 2010) is the standard set for everyday adult reading.
- Most kanji contain a radical, the component a dictionary files them under, which often hints at the meaning: 氵 for water, 亻 for people, 言 for speech.
- Kanji stroke order follows the same rules as kana, plus rules for enclosures: a full box like 国 is sealed with its bottom stroke last.
- Most kanji have an on-yomi from Chinese, typical in compounds (山 *san* in 富士山), and a kun-yomi native word, typical when the kanji stands alone or with okurigana (山 *yama*).
- Japanese sentences mix kanji for content words, hiragana for grammar and katakana for loanwords, and the switches between scripts mark the word boundaries.
- There are no official JLPT kanji lists; estimates run from about 100 kanji for N5 to about 2,000 for N1, and a first 100 everyday kanji gets you reading signs and menus.
