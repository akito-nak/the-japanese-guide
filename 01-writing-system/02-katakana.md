# Katakana — The Angular Cousin

Try reading this: ハンバーガー. Sound it out, character by character, and you get *han-bā-gā*. Say it a little faster and there it is: hamburger. Katakana looks intimidating for about a day, and then it starts handing you English words in disguise, and that moment never stops being satisfying.

Katakana (カタカナ) writes exactly the same sounds as hiragana, with a different look and a different job. Where hiragana is round and flowing, katakana is angular and sharp; think of hiragana as handwriting and katakana as block capitals. Because you already know the sounds from [section 1.1](01-hiragana.md), learning katakana is mostly a matter of matching new shapes to old friends, and most learners find it faster the second time round.

## When Katakana Is Used

Katakana's main job is words that came from outside Japan, but it does several other things too:

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| Loanwords | コーヒー (*kōhī*, coffee) |
| Foreign names and places | アメリカ (*Amerika*, America) |
| Emphasis, like italics in English | ダメ (*dame*, "no good!") written for impact instead of だめ |
| Plant and animal names in science | ヒト (*hito*, human, as a species) |
| Onomatopoeia and sound effects | ドキドキ (*dokidoki*, a pounding heart) |
| Slang and internet language | ガチ (*gachi*, seriously, for real) |
| Company and brand names | ソニー (*Sonī*, Sony), トヨタ (*Toyota*) |

Words borrowed from other languages are called **gairaigo** (外来語, "words that came from outside"), and Japanese has thousands of them, most from English. Every one you learn to recognize is a word you already half know.

## The Katakana Chart

The layout is identical to the hiragana gojūon chart, row for row:

|  | a | i | u | e | o |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| (vowel) | ア | イ | ウ | エ | オ |
| k | カ | キ | ク | ケ | コ |
| s | サ | シ | ス | セ | ソ |
| t | タ | チ | ツ | テ | ト |
| n | ナ | ニ | ヌ | ネ | ノ |
| h | ハ | ヒ | フ | ヘ | ホ |
| m | マ | ミ | ム | メ | モ |
| y | ヤ | | ユ | | ヨ |
| r | ラ | リ | ル | レ | ロ |
| w | ワ | | | | ヲ |
| n | ン | | | | |

ヲ exists but is almost never used: the object particle を is always written in hiragana.

Placing the two scripts side by side shows that some pairs are clearly related, because both scripts grew out of simplified kanji, sometimes the same one:

| Sound | Hiragana | Katakana |
|---|---|---|
| ka | か | カ |
| ki | き | キ |
| se | せ | セ |
| he | へ | ヘ |
| mo | も | モ |
| ya | や | ヤ |
| ri | り | リ |
| a | あ | ア |
| i | い | イ |
| e | え | エ |

The first seven pairs are close cousins; the last three grew from different kanji and share only a sound. Lean on the resemblances where they exist and treat the rest as new.

## Dakuten and Handakuten

The marks work exactly as they do in hiragana: dakuten (゛) voice the consonant and handakuten (゜) turn the *h* row into *p*.

| Base | With dakuten | Base | With dakuten or handakuten |
|---|---|---|---|
| カ ka | ガ ga | タ ta | ダ da |
| キ ki | ギ gi | チ chi | ヂ ji (rare) |
| ク ku | グ gu | ツ tsu | ヅ zu (rare) |
| ケ ke | ゲ ge | テ te | デ de |
| コ ko | ゴ go | ト to | ド do |
| サ sa | ザ za | ハ ha | バ ba, パ pa |
| シ shi | ジ ji | ヒ hi | ビ bi, ピ pi |
| ス su | ズ zu | フ fu | ブ bu, プ pu |
| セ se | ゼ ze | ヘ he | ベ be, ペ pe |
| ソ so | ゾ zo | ホ ho | ボ bo, ポ po |

## The Long Vowel Mark

Katakana writes long vowels with a dash, ー, called the **chōonpu** (長音符, long-sound mark), instead of adding a vowel character. You will see it on nearly every menu and sign. In vertical text it turns vertical too.

| Katakana | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| コーヒー | kōhī | coffee |
| ビール | bīru | beer |
| カー | kā | car |
| メール | mēru | email |
| クール | kūru | cool |
| ドア | doa | door (no long vowel) |

The dash changes meaning just as hiragana's long vowels do. ビル (*biru*) is a building; ビール (*bīru*) is beer. Order carefully.

## The Small ッ and Combinations

The small ッ doubles a consonant, exactly as small っ does in hiragana: ベッド (*beddo*, bed), カップ (*kappu*, cup), ネット (*netto*, the internet).

The yōon combinations (キャ, シュ, チョ and the rest) also work the same way. On top of those, katakana has extra combinations built with small vowels (ァ ィ ゥ ェ ォ) to imitate sounds that Japanese did not originally have:

| Characters | Romanization | Example word |
|---|---|---|
| キャ | kya | キャンプ (*kyanpu*, camp) |
| シャ | sha | シャワー (*shawā*, shower) |
| チャ | cha | チャンス (*chansu*, chance) |
| ジャ | ja | ジャズ (*jazu*, jazz) |
| ビュ | byu | ビュッフェ (*byuffe*, buffet) |
| ファ | fa | ファン (*fan*, fan) |
| フィ | fi | フィルム (*firumu*, film) |
| フェ | fe | フェリー (*ferī*, ferry) |
| ティ | ti | パーティー (*pātī*, party) |
| ディ | di | ディナー (*dinā*, dinner) |
| ウィ | wi | ウィキペディア (*Wikipedia*) |
| ヴァ | va | ヴァイオリン (*vaiorin*, violin) |

> **Note:** ヴ (*vu*) was created to write the English *v*, and it combines with small vowels to give ヴァ, ヴィ and so on. Many words are still more often written with the *b* sounds instead (バイオリン is more common than ヴァイオリン), and most speakers pronounce both as *b*.

## Telling Look-Alikes Apart

A few katakana are notorious for looking alike. They are all distinguishable once you know what to look for, and stroke direction is usually the key.

| Pair | How to tell them apart |
|---|---|
| シ / ツ | シ (*shi*): the two dots are stacked on the left and the long stroke rises from the bottom. ツ (*tsu*): the dots sit side by side along the top and the long stroke falls from the top right |
| ソ / ン | ソ (*so*): the long stroke falls from the top. ン (*n*): the long stroke rises from the bottom. The same logic as シ and ツ |
| ノ / ソ | ノ (*no*) is one stroke; ソ has an extra short dot on the left |
| ク / タ / ケ | タ (*ta*) is ク (*ku*) with an extra stroke inside; ケ (*ke*) has a horizontal that sticks out to the right |
| ア / マ | ア (*a*) has a short stroke dropping down from the middle; マ (*ma*) has a small stroke crossing at the bottom |
| ウ / ワ / フ | ウ (*u*) has a dot on top and a short left side; ワ (*wa*) has the short left side but no dot; フ (*fu*) has neither |
| チ / テ | チ (*chi*) has a slanted top stroke; テ (*te*) has a flat one |
| ロ / 口 | ロ (*ro*) is a katakana; 口 (*kuchi*, mouth) is a kanji. They are almost identical, and context tells you which is meant |

> **Tip:** For シ and ツ (and ソ and ン), imagine writing the long stroke. If it starts low and rises like a smile, it is シ or ン; if it starts high and falls like a waterfall, it is ツ or ソ.

Several katakana also have kanji twins, such as カ (*ka*) and 力 (*chikara*, power), エ (*e*) and 工 (*kō*, craft) and ニ (*ni*) and 二 (*ni*, two). Again, context sorts them out almost every time.

## Decoding Loanwords

Japanese syllables mostly end in a vowel, so English words get extra vowels added and consonants adjusted. Once you know the patterns, you can guess many words on sight: *l* becomes *r*, *v* often becomes *b*, a final consonant gains an *u* or *o*, and long words get clipped.

| Katakana | Romanization | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| テレビ | terebi | television |
| スマホ | sumaho | smartphone (clipped from スマートフォン) |
| アイスクリーム | aisu kurīmu | ice cream |
| マクドナルド | Makudonarudo | McDonald's |
| スターバックス | Sutābakkusu | Starbucks |
| エレベーター | erebētā | elevator |
| アパート | apāto | apartment |
| ガソリン | gasorin | gasoline |
| ハンバーガー | hanbāgā | hamburger |
| コンビニ | konbini | convenience store (clipped) |

Not every borrowing kept its English meaning, and some were assembled in Japan from English parts. These made-in-Japan coinages are called **wasei-eigo** (和製英語, "Japan-made English"), and they make some of the best false friends in the language:

| Katakana | Looks like | Actually means |
|---|---|---|
| マンション (*manshon*) | mansion | a condominium or apartment building |
| ノート (*nōto*) | note | a notebook |
| バイク (*baiku*) | bike | usually a motorcycle |
| スマート (*sumāto*) | smart | slim, stylish |
| カンニング (*kanningu*) | cunning | cheating on a test |
| サラリーマン (*sararīman*) | salary man | an office worker on a salary |

> **Warning:** Telling a Japanese friend you live in a マンション will not impress anyone; it is an ordinary apartment block. And complimenting a classmate's カンニング is a different compliment altogether.

## Loanwords from Other Languages

English dominates, but Japan has been borrowing for more than 400 years. Portuguese traders and missionaries arrived in the 16th century, Dutch scholars in the Edo period, and German medicine and French cooking later still:

| Katakana | From | Original word | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| パン | Portuguese | pão | bread |
| タバコ | Portuguese | tabaco | cigarettes |
| ランドセル | Dutch | ransel | a schoolchild's backpack |
| ビール | Dutch | bier | beer |
| アルバイト | German | Arbeit | a part-time job |
| リュックサック | German | Rucksack | a backpack |
| アンケート | French | enquête | a survey or questionnaire |
| クレヨン | French | crayon | a crayon |
| テンポ | Italian | tempo | tempo, pace |

## Katakana in Pop Culture

Manga, anime and games use katakana constantly, often for effect rather than for loanwords:

- Sound effects are almost always katakana: ドカン (*dokan*, boom), バン (*ban*, bang).
- Robots and aliens often speak in katakana to sound flat or mechanical.
- Foreign characters may have some of their lines in katakana to signal an accent.
- Attack and transformation names are frequently katakana, or kanji with katakana furigana.

The stylistic side of katakana, and the way it gives a character a "voice", comes back in [section 4.3](../04-spoken-japanese/03-role-language.md).

## Stroke Order

The rules from [section 1.1](01-hiragana.md) apply unchanged: top to bottom, left to right, and one stroke per pen movement. What changes is the feel. Katakana strokes are straight and angular, most characters have only two or three of them, and the corners are sharp rather than looped.

Stroke direction matters more in katakana than anywhere else, because it is the only reliable difference between the look-alike pairs. Learn シ, ツ, ソ and ン as movements, not as pictures, and you will never confuse them.

### The Vowels

| Character | Strokes | Order |
|---|---|---|
| ア | 2 | (1) Horizontal that turns down to the left; (2) a short stroke dropping from the middle |
| イ | 2 | (1) A diagonal down to the left; (2) a vertical from its middle |
| ウ | 3 | (1) A short dot at the top; (2) a short left vertical; (3) horizontal that turns down to the left |
| エ | 3 | (1) Top horizontal; (2) vertical; (3) bottom horizontal |
| オ | 3 | (1) Horizontal; (2) a vertical crossing it, with a hook; (3) a diagonal down to the left |

### The *K* to *H* Rows

## Learning Katakana Fast

You already know the sounds, so the job is recognition, and the fastest way to get it is to read real words from the first day:

1. Learn the shapes with mnemonics, a row or two at a time.
2. Read katakana wherever you find it: menus at cafés and Western-style restaurants, product packaging, signs.
3. Guess the English word before you look it up. You will be right surprisingly often.
4. Keep a running list of false friends. They are the most fun part.

Good places to practice include café menus, drink labels in a convenience store, sound effects in manga, and the item names in Japanese video games, which are often katakana.

With hiragana and katakana both under your belt, you can sound out every word in Japanese that is not written in kanji, which in practice means grammar, loanwords, names and a good share of signs and menus. Kanji is a longer road, and [section 1.3](03-kanji.md) starts down it.

## Summary

- Katakana writes the same sounds as hiragana, in an angular style, and is used mainly for loanwords (gairaigo), foreign names, sound effects, scientific names and emphasis.
- Long vowels in katakana are written with the long vowel mark ー (chōonpu), as in コーヒー *kōhī*, and the mark changes meaning: ビル is a building, ビール is beer.
- Extra combinations with small vowels, such as ファ, ティ and ウィ, let katakana imitate sounds Japanese did not originally have.
- シ and ン have long strokes that rise from the bottom, while ツ and ソ have long strokes that fall from the top; stroke direction is the reliable way to tell them apart.
- Many loanwords shifted meaning or were coined in Japan (wasei-eigo): a マンション is an apartment block and カンニング is cheating on a test.
- Japanese borrowed from Portuguese, Dutch, German and French as well as English, so パン (bread) comes from Portuguese and アルバイト (part-time job) from German.
