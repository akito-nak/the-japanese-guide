# Japanese History in Brief — 2,000 Years on a Few Pages

Open a Japanese driver's license, a bank form or a train pass and you may find a date like 令和8年. That is 2026, counted in the reign of the current emperor. The calendar itself is a piece of history, and so is almost everything else you meet as a learner: the three scripts, the politeness system, the words borrowed from Chinese, Portuguese, Dutch and English. Each layer of the language was laid down by a particular period.

You don't need a textbook's worth of dates to read Japanese well. You need a mental map: which era is which, what each one left behind and how to read the names when they turn up in a museum caption, a period drama or a news story. The timeline that follows gives you that map, with the kanji you will see on the signs.

## Timeline at a Glance

Japanese history is divided into named periods (時代, *jidai*). The early ones are named after archaeological sites or seats of government; from 1868 on, each period matches one emperor's reign. Dates for the early periods are approximate, and historians disagree on some boundaries.

| Period | Dates | What defined it |
|---|---|---|
| 縄文 (Jōmon) | c. 14,000–300 BCE | Hunter-gatherer culture, some of the world's oldest pottery |
| 弥生 (Yayoi) | c. 300 BCE–300 CE | Wet-rice farming and metal tools arrive from the continent |
| 古墳 (Kofun) | c. 300–538 | Giant burial mounds, early state formation |
| 飛鳥 (Asuka) | 538–710 | Buddhism arrives, Chinese-style government and law |
| 奈良 (Nara) | 710–794 | First permanent capital, the first histories and poetry anthologies |
| 平安 (Heian) | 794–1185 | Court culture in Kyoto, kana, classical literature |
| 鎌倉 (Kamakura) | 1185–1333 | First warrior government, Zen Buddhism |
| 室町 (Muromachi) | 1336–1573 | Ashikaga shoguns, Noh, the tea ceremony |
| 戦国 (Sengoku) | 1467–1615 | Civil war among regional lords, Europeans arrive |
| 江戸 (Edo) | 1603–1868 | Tokugawa peace, restricted foreign contact, merchant culture |
| 明治 (Meiji) | 1868–1912 | Rapid modernization, constitution, empire |
| 大正 (Taishō) | 1912–1926 | Party politics, early mass culture |
| 昭和 (Shōwa) | 1926–1989 | Militarism, war, defeat, occupation, economic miracle |
| 平成 (Heisei) | 1989–2019 | Bubble collapse, stagnation, the digital age |
| 令和 (Reiwa) | 2019– | The current era, under Emperor Naruhito |

The Sengoku and Edo rows overlap on purpose: the warring-states period is a description of what was happening (constant war), while the Edo period begins with a political event (Tokugawa Ieyasu becoming shogun in 1603). Historians end the Sengoku period anywhere from 1568 to 1615.

## Before Written History

### Jōmon (縄文) — c. 14,000–300 BCE

The Jōmon people hunted, fished and gathered, living in pit dwellings across the archipelago. They made some of the oldest known pottery in the world, and the period is named after its decoration: 縄文 (*jōmon*) means "cord pattern," from the rope impressions pressed into the clay.

Genetic studies suggest that modern Japanese people descend from a mixture of Jōmon ancestors and later migrants from the continent, with the Jōmon share higher among the Ainu of Hokkaido and the people of Okinawa.

### Yayoi (弥生) — c. 300 BCE–300 CE

Wet-rice farming arrived from the Korean Peninsula and China, together with bronze, iron and new settlers. Rice made surplus and hierarchy possible, and the Yayoi way of life gradually spread across most of the islands. Radiocarbon dating has pushed the start of rice farming in northern Kyushu back several centuries, so you will also see the Yayoi period begin around 1000 BCE in newer sources.

## Classical Japan — The Age of the Court

### Asuka (飛鳥) — 538–710

Buddhism reached the Yamato court from the Korean kingdom of Baekje, in 538 or 552 depending on which early record you follow, and it transformed Japanese art, architecture and thought. Prince Shōtoku (聖徳太子, *Shōtoku Taishi*) is traditionally credited with promoting Buddhism and Chinese models of government, including the Seventeen-Article Constitution of 604. Japan was now writing in Chinese characters, the first step toward the mixed kanji-and-kana system you learned in [chapter 1](../01-writing-system/README.md).

### Nara (奈良) — 710–794

Japan's first permanent capital, Heijō-kyō, was laid out at Nara on the model of the Chinese capital Chang'an. The temple Tōdai-ji (東大寺) and its giant bronze Buddha date from this period. So do the oldest surviving Japanese books: the histories 古事記 (*Kojiki*, 712) and 日本書紀 (*Nihon Shoki*, 720), and the poetry anthology 万葉集 (*Man'yōshū*), which used Chinese characters for their sounds in a system that later evolved into kana.

### Heian (平安) — 794–1185

The capital moved to Heian-kyō, today's Kyoto, which remained the imperial capital until 1869. This is the golden age of court culture:

- 源氏物語 (*Genji Monogatari*, The Tale of Genji) — Murasaki Shikibu's long prose tale of court life, written in the early 11th century and often called one of the world's first novels
- 枕草子 (*Makura no Sōshi*, The Pillow Book) — Sei Shōnagon's witty collection of observations and lists
- Hiragana and katakana — developed during the ninth and 10th centuries from simplified Chinese characters, letting writers record Japanese phonetically
- Who wrote what — official documents were written in Chinese, mostly by men, while much of the great literature in kana was written by women of the court

The Heian ideal of beauty was **もののあわれ** (*mono no aware*), the gentle sadness of things that pass: cherry blossoms that fall, autumn leaves, the fragility of beauty. You will still meet the idea in poetry, film and every spring's cherry-blossom season ([section 7.4](../07-customs-and-daily-life/04-seasons-and-nature.md)).

## The Age of Warriors

### Kamakura (鎌倉) — 1185–1333

After the Genpei War, the warrior Minamoto no Yoritomo set up a military government in Kamakura and was named shogun (将軍, *shōgun*) in 1192. This was Japan's first **幕府** (*bakufu*, shogunate), a government run by a military ruler while the emperor reigned as a ceremonial figure in Kyoto. That split lasted, with interruptions, for nearly 700 years. The warrior class who staffed it are the subject of [section 9.2](02-samurai-and-bushido.md).

Zen Buddhism (禅, *zen*) took root among the warriors and shaped martial culture, art and design. Mongol fleets invaded in 1274 and 1281 and were driven back, the second time with the help of a typhoon. That storm became the 神風 (*kamikaze*, divine wind), a name the wartime government revived in 1944 for its suicide pilots.

### Muromachi (室町) — 1336–1573

The Ashikaga shoguns ruled from the Muromachi district of Kyoto. Under their patronage, many arts that now feel quintessentially Japanese took shape ([section 8.1](../08-arts-entertainment-and-sport/01-traditional-arts.md)):

- 能楽 (*nōgaku*) — Noh theater
- 茶の湯 (*chanoyu*) — the tea ceremony
- 生け花 (*ikebana*) — flower arrangement
- 枯山水 (*karesansui*) — dry rock gardens
- 水墨画 (*suibokuga*) — ink-wash painting

The Ōnin War (応仁の乱, 1467–1477) wrecked Kyoto and broke central authority, opening the **戦国時代** (*Sengoku jidai*, Warring States period): more than a century of war among regional lords, the **大名** (*daimyō*).

### Sengoku (戦国) — 1467–1615

The most turbulent era is also the most romanticized, endlessly retold in drama, manga and games. Three men dominate it, and Japanese schoolchildren learn them as a set.

織田信長 (Oda Nobunaga, 1534–1582) was ruthless and innovative. He used firearms on a large scale, welcomed Portuguese missionaries and conquered much of central Japan. In 1582 his own general Akechi Mitsuhide attacked him at the temple Honnō-ji in Kyoto, where he died, probably by his own hand.

豊臣秀吉 (Toyotomi Hideyoshi, 1537–1598), born a commoner, completed the unification of Japan. His 刀狩 (*katanagari*, sword hunt) of 1588 disarmed the peasantry, and he invaded Korea in 1592 and 1597 without success. He died leaving a child heir and a fragile settlement.

徳川家康 (Tokugawa Ieyasu, 1543–1616), patient and strategic, won the Battle of Sekigahara (関ヶ原の戦い) in 1600, became shogun in 1603 and destroyed the last Toyotomi stronghold at Osaka in 1615. His family ruled Japan for the next 250 years.

A famous trio of poems sums up their characters with one cuckoo that won't sing. Nobunaga: 「鳴かぬなら殺してしまえホトトギス」 ("If it won't sing, kill it"). Hideyoshi: "make it sing." Ieyasu: "wait until it sings." The poems are later inventions, but every Japanese reader knows them.

Portuguese traders reached the island of Tanegashima in 1543 and brought firearms; the Jesuit Francis Xavier followed in 1549. Christianity spread quickly, then was banned by Hideyoshi and suppressed by the Tokugawa, who feared European influence. Portuguese left a few everyday words behind, among them パン (*pan*, bread) and probably the source of 天ぷら (*tenpura*).

## Edo — Peace, Isolation and City Culture

### Edo (江戸) — 1603–1868

The Tokugawa shoguns ruled from Edo, today's Tokyo. From the 1630s they restricted foreign contact: Japanese could not travel abroad, Christianity was outlawed, and trade with Europe was limited to the Dutch at Dejima in Nagasaki, alongside trade with China, Korea and the Ryukyu Kingdom. Later writers called this policy **鎖国** (*sakoku*, "closed country"); the term dates from 1801, and historians now stress that Japan was managed rather than sealed.

The long peace changed the country:

- The population grew to roughly 30 million by the 18th century, and Edo became one of the largest cities in the world
- 浮世絵 (*ukiyo-e*) woodblock prints flourished (Hokusai, Hiroshige)
- 歌舞伎 (*kabuki*) became the great popular theater
- Matsuo Bashō (1644–1694) raised haikai verse to an art, the ancestor of modern haiku
- Through Dutch books, scholars of 蘭学 (*rangaku*, "Dutch learning") studied Western medicine and science

Official ideology ranked society as 士農工商 (*shi-nō-kō-shō*): warriors, farmers, artisans, merchants. Historians now see this as a Confucian ideal more than a description of how society worked, and Japanese textbooks have dropped it, but you will still meet the phrase. In practice, the merchants of Edo and Osaka grew rich, and their culture prized **粋** (*iki*), a cool, understated urban stylishness.

In 1853 the American Commodore Matthew Perry arrived with his "black ships" (黒船, *kurofune*) and demanded that Japan open its ports. Within 15 years the shogunate had fallen.

## The Modern Era

### Meiji (明治) — 1868–1912

The **明治維新** (*Meiji Ishin*, Meiji Restoration) of 1868 returned formal power to the emperor and launched one of the fastest national transformations in history. Within a generation the samurai class was abolished ([section 9.2](02-samurai-and-bushido.md)) and Japan had:

- A constitution (1889) and an elected parliament (1890)
- Universal primary schooling, railways, the telegraph and universities
- A conscript army and modern navy
- Victories over China (1894–1895) and Russia (1904–1905), and colonies in Taiwan (1895) and Korea (annexed 1910)

The language changed too. A standard spoken Japanese based on educated Tokyo speech was promoted through schools, often at the expense of regional dialects ([section 4.2](../04-spoken-japanese/02-dialects.md)). Scholars coined thousands of kanji compounds for Western ideas, many later borrowed back into Chinese and Korean: 電話 (*denwa*, telephone), 鉄道 (*tetsudō*, railway), 社会 (*shakai*, society), 民主主義 (*minshu shugi*, democracy). The full story is in [section 5.3](../05-living-language/03-language-evolution.md).

### Taishō (大正) — 1912–1926

A short era of party politics, expanding suffrage (all men over 25 could vote from 1925), jazz cafés and Western fashion. The Great Kantō Earthquake of September 1, 1923 killed more than 100,000 people and destroyed much of Tokyo and Yokohama.

### Shōwa (昭和) — 1926–1945: Militarism and War

The military gained control over government policy. Japan seized Manchuria (1931), went to full-scale war with China in 1937 (including the Nanjing Massacre that December), and in December 1941 attacked Pearl Harbor and Southeast Asia, bringing the United States into the war.

Atomic bombs destroyed Hiroshima (August 6, 1945) and Nagasaki (August 9), and the Soviet Union declared war on August 8. On August 15, Emperor Hirohito's recorded radio address announced the surrender. For most listeners it was the first time they had heard the emperor's voice. What came next, from the occupation to the economic miracle and beyond, is the subject of [section 9.3](03-postwar-japan.md).

## The Era Calendar

Japan counts years in two ways: the Western calendar (西暦, *seireki*) and the Japanese calendar (和暦, *wareki*), in which years are numbered within an era. Each era has a name, the **元号** (*gengō*), and since 1868 the era has changed only when a new emperor takes the throne. The first year of an era is **元年** (*gannen*), not 1年.

> 令和元年は2019年です。\
> *Reiwa gannen wa nisen-jūkyū-nen desu.*\
> The first year of Reiwa is 2019.

| Era | Emperor | Years | Meaning of the name |
|---|---|---|---|
| 明治 (Meiji) | Mutsuhito | 1868–1912 | "Enlightened rule" |
| 大正 (Taishō) | Yoshihito | 1912–1926 | "Great righteousness" |
| 昭和 (Shōwa) | Hirohito | 1926–1989 | "Enlightened peace" |
| 平成 (Heisei) | Akihito | 1989–2019 | "Peace everywhere" |
| 令和 (Reiwa) | Naruhito | 2019– | "Beautiful harmony," the government's official English gloss |

To convert, add the era's offset: Meiji + 1867, Taishō + 1911, Shōwa + 1925, Heisei + 1988, Reiwa + 2018. As of 2026 the year is 令和8年. Government forms, driver's licenses, bank documents and many older people still use era years, so it pays to convert without thinking ([section 10.3](../10-japanese-for-media/03-news.md) drills this for news reading).

> **Note:** In Japan a deceased emperor is referred to by the name of his era, so Hirohito is 昭和天皇 (*Shōwa Tennō*, the Shōwa Emperor). Using the personal name sounds foreign, and the reigning emperor is 天皇陛下 (*Tennō Heika*, His Majesty the Emperor).

## How History Shaped the Language

Each period of history left a layer in the language you are learning:

| Historical layer | What it left in Japanese |
|---|---|
| Chinese influence (Asuka, Nara) | Kanji, on'yomi readings, thousands of Sino-Japanese words |
| Heian court culture | Hiragana and katakana, classical literary grammar |
| Warrior rule (Kamakura to Edo) | Martial vocabulary, the ethics of loyalty and obligation |
| Edo city culture | Urban slang, wordplay, the Tokyo speech that became the standard |
| Portuguese and Dutch contact | パン, タバコ, ガラス, ビール and other early loanwords |
| Meiji modernization | Kanji compounds for Western ideas, a standard language |
| Occupation and postwar reform | A flood of English loanwords, simplified kanji and kana spelling |
| Postwar pop culture | Manga and anime language, youth slang |

That layering is why a single Japanese sentence can feel ancient and brand-new at once.

## Summary

- Japanese history is divided into named periods; from 1868 each period matches one emperor's reign, and its name (元号, *gengō*) is used to count years.
- The first year of an era is 元年 (*gannen*); add 2018 to a Reiwa year, 1988 to a Heisei year and 1925 to a Shōwa year to get the Western year.
- From 1185 to 1867 Japan was largely ruled by shoguns (幕府, *bakufu*) while emperors reigned as ceremonial figures in Kyoto.
- The Heian court produced kana and classical literature such as The Tale of Genji; Muromachi produced Noh, the tea ceremony and ikebana; Edo produced ukiyo-e, kabuki and haikai.
- Oda Nobunaga, Toyotomi Hideyoshi and Tokugawa Ieyasu unified Japan after the Sengoku wars, and the Tokugawa ruled from Edo for 250 years under a policy of restricted foreign contact.
- The Meiji Restoration of 1868 modernized Japan within a generation and created the standard language and many of the kanji compounds used today.
