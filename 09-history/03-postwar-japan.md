# Postwar Japan — From Ruins to Renaissance

In the summer of 1945, most of Japan's large cities had been burned or bombed flat. Nineteen years later, Tokyo hosted the Olympics and the first bullet train ran between Tokyo and Osaka. By 1968 Japan had the second-largest economy in the capitalist world. Few countries have changed so fast, and the Japan you are learning to read and speak, with its loanwords, its simplified kanji, its anime and its convenience stores, was largely built in those decades.

The story runs from surrender and occupation through the economic miracle to the bubble, the long stagnation and the Reiwa era. Each stage gave Japanese a handful of words for talking about the recent past, and you will meet them constantly in news and conversation.

## The Surrender — August 15, 1945

Japan accepted the Allies' Potsdam terms after the atomic bombings of Hiroshima (August 6) and Nagasaki (August 9) and the Soviet declaration of war (August 8). The formal surrender was signed aboard the USS *Missouri* on September 2.

On August 15, Emperor Hirohito's recorded voice announced the decision over the radio. This was the **玉音放送** (*gyokuon hōsō*, "Jewel Voice Broadcast"). It was delivered in formal classical Japanese, poor reception made it harder still to follow, and many listeners understood that the war was over only from the announcer's explanation afterward. Its most quoted line is still recognized by almost every Japanese adult:

> 堪え難きを堪え、忍び難きを忍び…\
> *Tae-gataki o tae, shinobi-gataki o shinobi…*\
> Enduring the unendurable and bearing the unbearable…

August 15 is remembered as 終戦の日 (*shūsen no hi*, the day the war ended), marked each year by a national memorial ceremony. Notice the word choice: Japanese usually says 終戦 (*shūsen*, end of the war) rather than 敗戦 (*haisen*, defeat), a difference that is itself debated.

## The Occupation (1945–1952)

The Allied Occupation was run in practice by the United States under General Douglas MacArthur, the Supreme Commander for the Allied Powers, known in Japan as マッカーサー元帥 (*Makkāsā gensui*, Field Marshal MacArthur). It ended when the San Francisco Peace Treaty took effect on April 28, 1952.

### A New Constitution

The 日本国憲法 (*Nihon-koku Kenpō*, Constitution of Japan) was promulgated on November 3, 1946 and took effect on May 3, 1947. Both dates are now national holidays (文化の日 and 憲法記念日). Its best-known clause, **Article 9** (憲法第9条, *kenpō dai-kyū-jō*), renounces war:

> 日本国民は、正義と秩序を基調とする国際平和を誠実に希求し、国権の発動たる戦争と、武力による威嚇又は武力の行使は、国際紛争を解決する手段としては、永久にこれを放棄する。\
> Aspiring sincerely to an international peace based on justice and order, the Japanese people forever renounce war as a sovereign right of the nation and the threat or use of force as means of settling international disputes.

The second paragraph says that "land, sea, and air forces, as well as other war potential, will never be maintained." Japan nevertheless created the Self-Defense Forces (自衛隊, *Jieitai*) in 1954, and how far they may go has been argued ever since. As of 2026 the constitution has never been amended. The National Diet Library's [Birth of the Constitution of Japan](https://www.ndl.go.jp/constitution/e/) collects the original drafts and documents.

### Political and Social Reform

- The emperor became "the symbol of the State and of the unity of the people" (象徴天皇, *shōchō tennō*), and in January 1946 he publicly denied that he was divine
- Women won the vote in December 1945 and voted for the first time in April 1946
- Land reform transferred land from absentee landlords to the farmers who worked it
- Labor unions were legalized and the large business conglomerates (財閥, *zaibatsu*) were broken up, though they later regrouped in looser form
- Education was rebuilt on a 6-3-3-4 system: six years of elementary school, three of middle school, three of high school and four of university

### Language Reform

The reforms that shaped the written Japanese you learn today came in this period:

- 1946: The **当用漢字** (*tōyō kanji*) list limited official writing to 1,850 characters, and many characters got simplified forms (國 became 国, 學 became 学)
- 1946: 現代かなづかい (*gendai kanazukai*, modern kana usage) made kana spelling follow pronunciation, so the old けふ became きょう (*kyō*, today)
- Horizontal writing, left to right, spread through official documents and science
- 1981: The tōyō list was replaced by the jōyō kanji list, revised in 2010 to the current 2,136 characters ([section 1.3](../01-writing-system/03-kanji.md))

## Reconstruction and the Economic Miracle

### The 1950s — Survival to Recovery

The Korean War (1950–1953) was a turning point. American military orders, 特需 (*tokuju*, special procurement), poured money into Japanese factories. By 1956 a government white paper declared 「もはや戦後ではない」 ("It is no longer the postwar period"), a phrase that became a slogan.

Consumer dreams of the late 1950s had a name borrowed from the imperial regalia: the 三種の神器 (*sanshu no jingi*, the three sacred treasures):

- 白黒テレビ (*shirokuro terebi*) — a black-and-white TV
- 洗濯機 (*sentakuki*) — a washing machine
- 冷蔵庫 (*reizōko*) — a refrigerator

### The 1960s — High Growth

From the mid-1950s to 1973, Japan's economy grew at an average of about 10 percent a year. This is the **高度経済成長期** (*kōdo keizai seichō ki*, high-growth period), one of the fastest sustained expansions on record. In 1960 Prime Minister Ikeda Hayato promised to double national income in 10 years; it took about seven.

The 1964 Tokyo Olympics were the coming-out party. The 新幹線 (*shinkansen*, "new trunk line") opened between Tokyo and Shin-Osaka on October 1, 1964, nine days before the opening ceremony, at a top speed of 210 km/h. In 1968 Japan's economy passed West Germany's to become the second largest in the capitalist world.

The sacred treasures were upgraded too. The 1960s "new three sacred treasures" were the 3C: カラーテレビ (color TV), クーラー (*kūrā*, air conditioner) and カー (a car). The oil shock of 1973 ended the high-growth era.

### The 1980s — The Bubble

After the Plaza Accord of 1985 strengthened the yen, the Bank of Japan cut interest rates, and cheap money flowed into land and shares. The result was the **バブル経済** (*baburu keizai*, bubble economy) of roughly 1986 to 1991:

- The Nikkei stock index peaked at 38,915 on the last trading day of 1989
- Land prices soared so high that, by widely quoted estimates, the grounds of the Imperial Palace were worth more than all the real estate in California
- Japanese companies bought American icons: Sony bought Columbia Pictures and Mitsubishi Estate bought a controlling stake in Rockefeller Center, both in 1989
- Expense accounts, golf-club memberships and taxis home from all-night parties became the stuff of legend

The バブル期 (*baburu ki*, bubble era) is remembered with a mixture of nostalgia and embarrassment, and its fashions and disco music still turn up as retro parody.

## After the Bubble — The Lost Decades

The Bank of Japan began raising rates in 1989. Stock prices fell through 1990, land prices followed from 1991, and banks were left holding huge bad loans. Growth stalled and prices began to fall. Commentators called the 1990s the 失われた10年 (*ushinawareta jūnen*, the lost decade); as stagnation dragged on, it became the **失われた30年** (*ushinawareta sanjūnen*), the lost 30 years. Whether that label is still fair has been debated since the return of inflation in 2022.

The long slump left a vocabulary of its own:

- 就職氷河期 (*shūshoku hyōgaki*, the employment ice age) — roughly 1993 to 2005, when graduates found few permanent jobs. Many became フリーター (*furītā*, "freeters," people in part-time or temporary work) and never got onto the lifetime-employment ladder.
- 格差社会 (*kakusa shakai*, a gap society) — the widening gap between secure and insecure workers.
- 引きこもり (*hikikomori*, social withdrawal) — people who rarely leave home for six months or more. A Cabinet Office survey published in 2023 estimated about 1.46 million people aged 15 to 64 in that state.
- 少子化 (*shōshika*, declining birthrate) — Japan's population peaked around 2008. On October 1, 2025 it was about 123.2 million and falling, according to the [Statistics Bureau](https://www.stat.go.jp/data/jinsui/pdf/202510.pdf).

### 1995 — The Double Shock

Two disasters in 1995 shook Japan's confidence in its own safety:

- 阪神・淡路大震災 (*Hanshin-Awaji Daishinsai*, the Great Hanshin Earthquake), January 17 — about 6,400 people died and much of Kobe was destroyed. So many volunteers poured in that 1995 became known as ボランティア元年 (*borantia gannen*, year one of volunteering).
- 地下鉄サリン事件 (*chikatetsu sarin jiken*, the Tokyo subway sarin attack), March 20 — members of the Aum Shinrikyō cult released sarin gas on Tokyo subway trains, killing 13 people and injuring thousands.

### 2011 — 3.11

On March 11, 2011, the 東日本大震災 (*Higashi Nihon Daishinsai*, Great East Japan Earthquake), a magnitude-9.0 quake and the tsunami it caused, left about 18,000 people dead or missing and triggered the meltdowns at the Fukushima Daiichi nuclear plant. Japanese people often refer to it by the date alone, 3.11 (*san ten ichi ichi*). It is a generation-defining event, and it is why earthquake and evacuation vocabulary fills the news ([section 10.3](../10-japanese-for-media/03-news.md)) and emergency signs ([section 6.4](../06-getting-around/04-emergencies.md)).

## American and Western Influence

Nearly seven years of occupation, and decades of close alliance afterward, left American fingerprints all over daily life. Some imports came earlier and were only boosted by the occupation; others were reshaped into something distinctly Japanese.

| Import | The Japanese version |
|---|---|
| Baseball (野球, *yakyū*) | Introduced in the 1870s; the most popular team sport for a century ([section 8.6](../08-arts-entertainment-and-sport/06-baseball-and-sumo.md)) |
| Jazz | First arrived in the 1920s, boomed after the war, still thriving |
| Blue jeans | Japanese denim became famous for quality |
| Fast food | McDonald's opened in Ginza in 1971 |
| Christmas | A commercial and romantic holiday rather than a religious one |
| Valentine's Day | Reversed: women give chocolate to men, who reply on White Day (March 14) |
| Halloween | A huge costume street party since the 2010s |
| English loanwords | Thousands absorbed into everyday Japanese ([section 1.2](../01-writing-system/02-katakana.md)) |

Eating KFC (ケンタッキー, *Kentakkī*) at Christmas is a Japanese tradition that grew out of a 1974 advertising campaign; many families order their bucket weeks in advance.

## Japan's Cultural Exports

As Japan's factories conquered world markets, its culture followed:

| Era | What the world bought |
|---|---|
| 1970s–1980s | Cars, consumer electronics, the Walkman |
| 1980s–1990s | Video games (Nintendo, Sega, Sony), the first wave of manga in translation |
| 1990s–2000s | Pokémon, Dragon Ball, Studio Ghibli, anime on television worldwide |
| 2000s–2010s | J-pop, street fashion, Japanese food far beyond sushi |
| 2010s–2020s | Anime streaming, Vtubers, matcha, record tourism |

From around 2010 the government promoted this soft power as クールジャパン (*Kūru Japan*, Cool Japan), including an investment fund created in 2013. The results have been mixed: the culture mostly spread on its own, carried by fans.

## Japan in the Reiwa Era

The Reiwa era began on May 1, 2019, when Emperor Akihito abdicated, the first abdication in about two centuries. As of 2026:

- The population is shrinking and aging, and labor shortages have opened a long-running debate about immigration
- Tourism is a national strategy (観光立国, *kankō rikkoku*). Foreign visitors reached 31.9 million in 2019, collapsed during the pandemic, then hit a record 36.9 million in 2024 and about 42.7 million in 2025, according to [JNTO](https://statistics.jnto.go.jp/en/)
- Work-style reform (働き方改革, *hatarakikata kaikaku*) has capped overtime (残業, *zangyō*) by law since 2019
- The Tokyo 2020 Olympics were postponed by the pandemic and held without spectators in 2021

Young Japanese now look back on the Heisei era with fond irony: 平成レトロ (*Heisei retoro*, Heisei retro) revives flip phones, purikura photo stickers and 1990s fashion. Today's young adults are the Z世代 (*Z sedai*, Generation Z), digital natives who are questioning expectations about work, gender and marriage that the postwar generations took for granted.

## Summary

- Japan surrendered in August 1945; the emperor's radio address of August 15 (玉音放送) is remembered as 終戦の日, the day the war ended.
- The US-led occupation (1945–1952) produced the 1947 constitution, whose Article 9 renounces war, along with women's suffrage, land reform and a symbolic emperor.
- Postwar language reform introduced the 1,850 tōyō kanji, simplified character forms and modern kana spelling, the basis of the jōyō kanji and spelling learners use today.
- The high-growth period (高度経済成長期, mid-1950s to 1973) averaged about 10 percent growth a year and made Japan the second-largest capitalist economy by 1968.
- The bubble economy of about 1986–1991 collapsed into the "lost decades," leaving words such as 就職氷河期, 格差社会 and 引きこもり in everyday use.
- The 1995 Kobe earthquake and sarin attack and the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake (3.11) are defining shocks of the modern era.
