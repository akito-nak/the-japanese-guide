# Samurai and Bushido — The Code That Still Echoes

Japan has had no samurai for 150 years, yet a Japanese manager may still tell a team to show 覚悟 (*kakugo*, resolve), a kendo teacher still drills 残心 (*zanshin*, lingering awareness), and a rival who helps you out "sends you salt." The warrior class left its vocabulary, its ethics and its aesthetics all over modern Japanese, along with a heroic legend that is only partly true.

The **侍** (*samurai*) were the hereditary warrior class of premodern Japan: not just fighters but a ruling social group with its own education, etiquette and ideals. Knowing who they were, and how their image was later polished, explains a lot about why honor, obligation, discipline and "the way" (道, *dō*) carry so much weight in the language.

## Who the Samurai Were

The word *samurai* comes from the old verb さぶらう (*saburau*, written 侍う or 候う), "to serve" or "to wait upon." The first samurai were armed retainers serving noble houses in the Heian period. After Minamoto no Yoritomo founded the Kamakura shogunate in the 1180s, warriors became the ruling class ([section 9.1](01-overview.md)), and for the next seven centuries Japan was governed by military houses.

In the Edo period the official social order placed warriors at the top, above farmers, artisans and merchants:

1. 士 (*shi*) — warriors
2. 農 (*nō*) — farmers
3. 工 (*kō*) — artisans
4. 商 (*shō*) — merchants

As [section 9.1](01-overview.md) explains, this ranking was more ideal than reality: many samurai were poor and in debt, while some merchants were rich. What set samurai apart in daily life was the right to wear the **大小** (*daishō*, "big and small"), a pair of swords: the long 刀 (*katana*) and the short 脇差 (*wakizashi*). Commoners could own swords but could not wear the pair.

## Bushidō — The Way of the Warrior

**武士道** (*bushidō*) means "the way of the warrior." It was never a single written code. Medieval warriors had house rules and battle customs, Edo-period scholars wrote about warrior ethics in Confucian terms, and the word *bushidō* itself only became common in the 17th century.

The most quoted Edo text is 葉隠 (*Hagakure*, "Hidden by Leaves"), the sayings of the retainer Yamamoto Tsunetomo, recorded around 1716. Its most famous line is deliberately shocking:

> 武士道と云ふは、死ぬ事と見付けたり。\
> *Bushidō to iu wa, shinu koto to mitsuketari.*\
> I have found that the way of the warrior is to die.

The point is not a wish to die. A warrior who has already accepted death can act without hesitation or fear, wholly committed to duty.

### The Seven Virtues

The tidy list of virtues usually given for bushidō comes from a much later book: *Bushido: The Soul of Japan*, written in English by the educator Nitobe Inazō and published in 1900. Nitobe wanted to explain Japan to Western readers, and his idealized portrait shaped both foreign and Japanese ideas of the samurai.

| Kanji | Reading | Virtue |
|---|---|---|
| 義 | *gi* | Rectitude, moral justice |
| 勇 | *yū* | Courage |
| 仁 | *jin* | Benevolence |
| 礼 | *rei* | Courtesy, respect |
| 誠 | *makoto* | Sincerity, honesty |
| 名誉 | *meiyo* | Honor |
| 忠義 | *chūgi* | Loyalty |

Several of these kanji are everyday words you will meet again: 礼 in お礼 (*orei*, thanks), 誠 in 誠に (*makoto ni*, sincerely), the formal "truly" of polite apologies ([section 3.4](../03-politeness/04-honorific-japanese.md)).

> **Note:** Historians treat bushidō as partly a modern invention. Real warriors switched sides, broke oaths and fought for land and pay; the code of perfect loyalty was largely written in peacetime and polished in the Meiji era, when the samurai themselves were gone.

## Seppuku — Death With Honor

**切腹** (*seppuku*), also called 腹切り (*harakiri*), was ritual suicide by cutting open the abdomen. In Japanese, *seppuku* is the formal word; *harakiri* is blunter and more colloquial. It sounds extreme because it was, but it shows how the samurai ideal worked:

- Choosing death over capture or disgrace was seen as a way to preserve one's honor and protect one's family.
- The ritual was formalized, and an assistant, the **介錯人** (*kaishakunin*), beheaded the samurai at the decisive moment to end the suffering.
- In the Edo period, seppuku was also a sentence: a samurai ordered to commit seppuku died by his own hand instead of being executed like a commoner.

The most famous story built on it is 忠臣蔵 (*Chūshingura*), the tale of the 47 **浪人** (*rōnin*, masterless samurai). In 1701 their lord, Asano Naganori, was ordered to commit seppuku after drawing his sword on an official in the shogun's castle. Almost two years later his former retainers killed that official, Kira Yoshinaka, and were then themselves sentenced to seppuku. The story has been retold in kabuki, film and television for 300 years.

## Values That Survive in Modern Japan

### Haji (恥) — Shame

恥 (*haji*) is shame or disgrace. The anthropologist Ruth Benedict famously described Japan as a "shame culture" in 1946; scholars have since criticized the label as too simple, but concern with how one appears to others is a real thread in the language.

> 恥ずかしい！\
> *Hazukashii!*\
> How embarrassing!

### Giri (義理) and Ninjō (人情) — Duty and Feeling

**義理** (*giri*) is social obligation: what you owe to people who have helped you, your employer or your community. It drives the rhythm of gifts and return gifts in [section 7.1](../07-customs-and-daily-life/01-culture-and-norms.md). Its counterpart is **人情** (*ninjō*), human feeling and compassion. The clash between giri and ninjō is a classic theme of kabuki, bunraku and yakuza films. Even Valentine's Day has a giri: 義理チョコ (*giri choko*) is chocolate given out of obligation rather than love.

### Menmoku (面目) — Face

**面目** (*menmoku*, also read *menboku*) is one's standing in the eyes of others, close to the English "face." Japanese has a family of face expressions:

> 面目ない。\
> *Menmoku nai.*\
> I'm ashamed (I've lost face).
>
> 顔を潰す。\
> *Kao o tsubusu.*\
> To make someone lose face (literally "to crush their face").

### Kokki (克己) — Self-Mastery

克己 (*kokki*) means mastering oneself: controlling emotions, desires and reactions. The ideal lives on in the discipline expected in school clubs, martial arts and traditional arts such as 茶道 (*sadō*, the way of tea) and 剣道 (*kendō*, the way of the sword).

### Zanshin (残心) — Lingering Awareness

**残心** (*zanshin*, literally "remaining mind") is the awareness you keep after an action is finished. In kendo and kyūdō (archery), a strike or a shot without zanshin does not count as complete, because the fighter must stay alert and composed afterward. Teachers apply the idea to daily life too: finish a task and stay attentive.

## Famous Samurai

### Miyamoto Musashi (宮本武蔵, c. 1584–1645)

Japan's most famous swordsman fought, by his own account in his book, more than 60 duels without a loss. His 五輪書 (*Gorin no Sho*, The Book of Five Rings), written near the end of his life, is still read as a book of strategy, including by business readers. One line from it is a favorite of martial artists:

> 千日の稽古を鍛とし、万日の稽古を錬とす。\
> *Sennichi no keiko o tan to shi, mannichi no keiko o ren to su.*\
> A thousand days of practice forges; ten thousand days of practice refines.

### Takeda Shingen (武田信玄, 1521–1573)

The lord of Kai province (today's Yamanashi) was renowned for his cavalry and his administration. This saying is traditionally attributed to him:

> 人は城、人は石垣、人は堀、情けは味方、仇は敵なり。\
> *Hito wa shiro, hito wa ishigaki, hito wa hori, nasake wa mikata, ada wa teki nari.*\
> People are the castle, people are the walls, people are the moat. Compassion makes allies; enmity makes enemies.

### Uesugi Kenshin (上杉謙信, 1530–1578)

Shingen's great rival was nicknamed the "god of war" for his battlefield record. A famous legend says that when a blockade cut off salt to landlocked Kai, Kenshin let salt through to his enemy, because he fought with arms, not with hunger. The story gave Japanese the idiom 敵に塩を送る (*teki ni shio o okuru*, to send salt to your enemy), meaning to help a rival in difficulty.

### Hijikata Toshizō (土方歳三, 1835–1869)

Vice-commander of the 新選組 (*Shinsengumi*), a police force that fought for the shogunate in Kyoto in its final years. He kept fighting after the shogun surrendered and was killed at Hakodate in 1869, in the last campaign of the Boshin War. Novels, dramas and anime have made him a romantic symbol of loyalty to a lost cause.

## The Sword and the Armor

### Katana (刀) — The Soul of the Samurai

A Japanese sword is both a weapon and a work of art, and was often called the soul of the samurai. A smith forges it from 玉鋼 (*tamahagane*), steel smelted from iron sand. The steel is folded around 10 to 15 times, which creates thousands of thin layers. Before quenching, the smith coats the blade with clay, thinly at the edge and thickly on the body, so that the edge cools fast and becomes hard while the spine stays tough. That hardening process, 焼き入れ (*yakiire*), also creates the wavy line along the edge, the 刃文 (*hamon*).

The parts of a sword make a compact vocabulary set:

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 刀 | *katana* | Sword (the long sword) |
| 脇差 | *wakizashi* | Short companion sword |
| 鍔 | *tsuba* | Hand guard |
| 刃 | *ha* | Blade edge |
| 鞘 | *saya* | Scabbard |
| 刀匠 | *tōshō* | Swordsmith (also 刀鍛冶, *katana-kaji*) |
| 銘 | *mei* | Smith's signature on the tang |

### Katchū (甲冑) — Armor

Japanese armor, 甲冑 (*katchū*), was built from small lacquered scales (小札, *kozane*) laced together with brightly colored cords (威, *odoshi*). The helmet (兜, *kabuto*) often carried a dramatic crest (前立, *maedate*), such as the crescent moon of Date Masamune.

## The Samurai in Modern Culture

The samurai never quite disappeared; they changed costume.

*Work.* Postwar Japanese companies, with their 終身雇用 (*shūshin koyō*, lifetime employment) and intense loyalty, have often been compared to samurai houses. The comparison is a metaphor: lifetime employment grew up in the 20th century, not the 17th.

*Martial arts.* Kendo (剣道), judo (柔道) and aikido (合気道) are modern 道 (*dō*, way) arts that present themselves as heirs to samurai values of discipline, courtesy and self-improvement.

*Media.* Samurai fill the films of Kurosawa Akira (黒澤明), anime such as *Rurouni Kenshin* and *Samurai Champloo*, and games such as *Sekiro* and *Ghost of Tsushima*. Their speech, full of archaic forms like 〜でござる, is a textbook case of role language ([section 4.3](../04-spoken-japanese/03-role-language.md)).

*Everyday words.* Warrior vocabulary is all through ordinary Japanese:

| Word | Reading | Modern use |
|---|---|---|
| 道 | *michi*, *-dō* | "The way": 剣道, 茶道, 書道 (calligraphy), and any path |
| 覚悟 | *kakugo* | Resolve; 覚悟しろ！ means "Prepare yourself!" in anime |
| 武器 | *buki* | Weapon, also a strength: 笑顔が武器 ("a smile is my weapon") |
| 修行 | *shugyō* | Ascetic training; now any hard apprenticeship |
| 流派 | *ryūha* | A school of swordsmanship; now any school or style |
| 一騎打ち | *ikkiuchi* | Single combat; now a head-to-head race in an election |

## The End of the Samurai

The Meiji government dismantled the samurai class step by step:

- 1869: Samurai were reclassified as 士族 (*shizoku*), a status title with dwindling privileges
- 1871: The domains were abolished and replaced by prefectures (廃藩置県, *haihan chiken*)
- 1873: A conscription law ended the samurai monopoly on military service
- 1876: The **廃刀令** (*Haitōrei*, Sword Abolition Edict) banned wearing swords in public, and samurai stipends were converted into government bonds

The last great revolt was the 西南戦争 (*Seinan Sensō*, Satsuma Rebellion) of 1877, led by 西郷隆盛 (Saigō Takamori), who had helped bring the Meiji government to power. His rebels were defeated by the new conscript army, and Saigō died at Shiroyama in September 1877. He was pardoned posthumously in 1889, and his statue still stands in Tokyo's Ueno Park.

The samurai lost their legal privileges in the 1870s. As a cultural force, they never left.

## Summary

- The samurai (侍, from さぶらう, "to serve") were the hereditary warrior class that governed Japan from the late 12th century until the Meiji reforms of the 1870s.
- Bushidō (武士道) was never a single written code; its famous seven virtues come from Nitobe Inazō's 1900 book, and Hagakure (c. 1716) supplies its most quoted line about death.
- Seppuku (切腹) was ritual suicide used both to preserve honor and as a sentence, and the story of the 47 rōnin (忠臣蔵) is its most famous retelling.
- Giri (義理, obligation), ninjō (人情, human feeling), menmoku (面目, face) and zanshin (残心, lingering awareness) are samurai-era ideas still active in modern Japanese.
- Everyday expressions such as 覚悟, 修行 and 敵に塩を送る (to help a rival) come from warrior culture.
- The Meiji government reclassified the samurai in 1869, ended their military role in 1873 and banned sword-wearing in 1876; the 1877 Satsuma Rebellion under Saigō Takamori was their last stand.
