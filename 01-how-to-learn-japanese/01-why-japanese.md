# Why Japanese

About 123 million people live in Japan (122.8 million in April 2026, by the Statistics Bureau of Japan's estimate), and almost all of them speak Japanese as their first language; Ethnologue's 2026 edition counts about 124 million first-language speakers worldwide. Japanese has no official status outside Japan, yet it travels far beyond its islands. The Japan Foundation's most recent survey of Japanese teaching abroad, carried out in 2024 and published in 2025, counted just over 4 million learners in classes in 143 countries and regions, a record, led by China, Indonesia, South Korea and Australia. That figure counts only learners in schools and courses; the self-taught, with an app and an anime habit, are uncounted and probably more numerous still.

The reasons people give are as varied as the learners. Anime, manga and games pull in millions; so do Japanese food, design, literature and music, from 演歌 (*enka*) to city pop. Some want to work for a Japanese company, study at a Japanese university or pass the JLPT for a visa. Many simply fell in love with a trip and want the next one to happen in Japanese. Whatever brought you here, it is a better fuel than any statistic, and this book is built to keep it burning. The rest of this section deals with the obstacle that stops most people before they start: the belief that Japanese is impossible.

## The Myth of the Impossible Language

Japanese is hard in some specific ways and easy in several ways that surprise people. Start with the easy parts.

**Five vowels, and they never change.** English spells its vowels a dozen ways and pronounces them a dozen more; *a* alone sounds different in "cat", "car", "cake" and "about". Japanese has five vowels, あいうえお (*a i u e o*), and each keeps its sound in every word. Its consonants are mostly familiar to an English speaker too.

**Spelling matches sound.** Hiragana and katakana are syllable scripts: each character stands for one sound, and with a few small exceptions it is always read the same way. Once you know the kana, you can read aloud any word written in them, and write down any word you hear. English speakers, raised on "though", "through" and "tough", rarely appreciate what a gift that is until they have it.

**Verbs barely misbehave.** Japanese verbs do change form, for politeness, tense and much else, but they follow rules, and only two verbs are truly irregular: する (*suru*, to do) and 来る (くる, *kuru*, to come). [Section 4.1](../04-building-sentences/01-grammar-basics.md) shows the three verb groups. And a verb doesn't change for the person doing it: "I eat", "she eats" and "they eat" all use the same 食べます (たべます, *tabemasu*).

> 私は寿司を**食べます**。\
> *Watashi wa sushi o tabemasu.*\
> I eat sushi.
>
> 姉は寿司を**食べます**。\
> *Ane wa sushi o tabemasu.*\
> My older sister eats sushi.

**No articles, no plurals, no gender, no tones.** There is no "a" or "the", nouns have no plural endings and no masculine or feminine, and adjectives never agree with anything. 本 (ほん, *hon*) is "book", "books", "a book" and "the book"; context or a number does the rest. And Japanese has no tones that change a syllable's meaning (its pitch accent is real but gentler, and section 1.2 comes back to it).

> 本を一冊買いました。\
> *Hon o issatsu kaimashita.*\
> I bought one book.
>
> 本を三冊買いました。\
> *Hon o sansatsu kaimashita.*\
> I bought three books.

The grammar is not trivial. The particles は (*wa*) and が (*ga*) take most learners months to feel, and politeness has a whole chapter of its own. But a learner of Japanese spends no time on gender agreement, irregular plurals or long lists of irregular verbs, and in many European languages those eat the first year.

## The Real Hurdles

Being honest about difficulty is the best way to beat it. Japanese has five real hurdles for an English speaker, and this book gives each of them a plan.

**Kanji.** Reading a newspaper means knowing about 2,000 characters, most with more than one reading. 生 alone is read *sei*, *shō*, *nama*, *i(kiru)* and *u(mareru)*, among others, depending on the word. This is the longest single road in Japanese, and there is no shortcut around the hours. The good news is that kanji are built from a few hundred recurring components, and the work compounds: every component you learn makes the next kanji easier. [Chapter 3](../03-writing-system/README.md) is about spending those hours well.

**Grammar that runs the other way.** Japanese puts the verb last and stacks information in front of the noun it describes, so a sentence often reads like English in reverse.

> 昨日友達と食べた寿司はおいしかった。\
> *Kinō tomodachi to tabeta sushi wa oishikatta.*\
> The sushi I ate with a friend yesterday was delicious. [yesterday friend with ate sushi [topic] was delicious]

Nothing in that sentence is hard on its own. What takes time is building the habit of holding the pieces until the verb arrives.

**Politeness levels.** The same request comes out differently to a friend, a stranger and a customer, with different verb forms and sometimes different verbs. [Chapter 5](../05-politeness/README.md) maps the levels, and the good news is that one level, polite です/ます Japanese, carries a beginner almost everywhere.

**What goes unsaid.** Japanese leaves out anything the listener can work out, including the subject. 行く？ (*Iku?*) can mean "Are you going?", "Is he going?" or "Shall we go?", and only the situation tells you which. Learners used to English, which insists on a subject in every sentence, have to learn to read the context as carefully as the words.

**Listening at speed.** Textbook Japanese is slow and complete; real Japanese is fast, contracted and full of dropped sounds. Vowel length and double consonants change meanings (おばさん *obasan* "aunt", おばあさん *obāsan* "grandmother"), and pitch separates words that are spelled alike. Listening is the skill that improves slowest, so this book has you training your ear from the first week, and [chapter 2](../02-sounds-of-japanese/README.md) starts the work.

> **Note:** Notice what is missing from the hurdle list: pronunciation as a whole. With five pure vowels and few unfamiliar consonants, an English speaker can make most Japanese sounds on day one, and Japanese listeners are generous with learners. Many people are surprised to find themselves understood long before they can understand.

## How Long Does It Take?

The United States Foreign Service Institute, which trains American diplomats, puts Japanese in its hardest category, alongside Arabic, Korean and Mandarin, with an estimate of about 88 weeks, or 2,200 class hours, to reach professional working proficiency. Spanish gets 30 weeks. Those numbers sound grim until you read the small print: they describe full-time classes plus homework, aiming at the level where a diplomat can negotiate and brief in the language. Ordering dinner, making friends, following a slice-of-life anime and passing the JLPT's N4 all come long before that.

A more useful way to think about time is in daily habits. At one focused hour a day, plus some listening on the side, many self-taught learners handle everyday conversation within a year or two and read real material, with a dictionary nearby, within three or four. That is learners' experience, not a measured average, and kanji make reading the slower of the two. [Section 1.4](04-the-jlpt-map.md) translates this into the JLPT's levels, so you can see which milestone is next.

> **Tip:** Go say this today. The next time you finish a meal at a Japanese restaurant, say ごちそうさまでした (*gochisōsama deshita*, thank you for the meal) to the staff on your way out. It is the most natural thanks in the language, few staff expect it from a visitor, and it is the start of the habit that matters most: using Japanese with real people.

### Going Deeper: Where Japanese Comes From

Japanese has no proven relatives except its own family. Linguists call that family **Japonic**: Japanese itself and the Ryukyuan languages of Okinawa and the islands around it, which branched off from a shared ancestor many centuries ago and are not mutually intelligible with Japanese. UNESCO's atlas of endangered languages (2009) lists six Ryukyuan languages, all endangered, and [section 6.2](../06-spoken-japanese/02-dialects.md) touches on them.

Beyond Japonic, the picture is contested. The most debated proposal links Japanese with Korean, Tungusic, Mongolic and Turkic in a "Transeurasian" (formerly "Altaic") family; Martine Robbeets and colleagues argued in *Nature* (2021) that it spread with early farmers across Northeast Asia, and Tian and colleagues (2022) replied that its linguistic evidence does not meet the standards of historical linguistics, with shared features that borrowing could explain. The linguist Alexander Vovin, once a supporter of a link with Korean, concluded in *Koreo-Japonica* (2010) that the similarities came from contact, not common descent. The mainstream view, as of 2026, is that Japonic is an independent family whose deeper ancestry is unknown. Whatever its origin, Japanese borrowed its writing and thousands of words from Chinese, a story that [chapter 3](../03-writing-system/README.md) and [section 7.3](../07-living-language/03-language-evolution.md) pick up.

## Summary

- Japanese has about 124 million first-language speakers, nearly all in Japan, and just over 4 million people studied it in classes abroad in 2024 (Japan Foundation).
- Japanese has five unchanging vowels, kana that spell sounds regularly, only two truly irregular verbs, and no articles, plurals, grammatical gender or tones.
- The real hurdles are kanji, word order far from English, politeness levels, omitted subjects and listening at speed, and each responds to the right kind of practice.
- The FSI's 2,200-hour estimate describes full-time training for professional proficiency; everyday conversation comes much sooner with a daily habit.
- Japanese belongs to the Japonic family with the Ryukyuan languages; links to Korean or a wider Transeurasian family remain unproven.
