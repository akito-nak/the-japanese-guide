# Apps, Courses and Tools

A generation ago, a learner of Japanese needed a paper dictionary, a kanji dictionary sorted by radical and stroke count, a box of index cards and an afternoon to look up a single unfamiliar character. Today a free phone app recognizes a kanji you draw with your finger, gives you its readings and plays the word aloud, and a browser extension shows the meaning of any word you hover over on a Japanese website. The tools are no longer the bottleneck. The risk now is the opposite: so many apps compete for your daily minutes that you spend them sampling instead of learning. The rule this book follows is simple. Choose one tool for each part of the learning loop from [section 1.2](02-study-and-immersion.md), use it daily, and ignore the rest until you have a reason to switch.

Everything below is as of 2026. Apps change features and prices often, so check before paying for anything.

## The Essential Two: A Dictionary and Flashcards

Every learner needs a dictionary that is always within reach, and it should do three things: look up a word typed in kana or romaji, find a kanji you can't read by drawing it or picking its components, and play the word aloud. Several free tools do all three.

- **Jisho** (jisho.org) is the free web dictionary most learners start with. It draws on JMdict, an open dictionary of about 170,000 entries, and lets you search by English, kana, romaji, handwriting or the components of a kanji. Each kanji page shows stroke order, readings and common words that use it.
- **Takoboto** has apps for Android and iPhone built on the same open data, and works offline, which matters on a train in the mountains.
- **Shirabe Jisho** is a polished free dictionary for iPhone, iPad and Mac, with handwriting input and a US $9.99 add-on for extra features.
- **Yomitan** is a free browser extension for Chrome, Firefox and Edge, the successor to the long-popular Yomichan. Hover over any Japanese word on a web page and its reading and meaning appear; one key sends the word, its sentence and native audio to Anki. It is the engine behind the **sentence mining** described in [section 3.4](../03-writing-system/04-studying-kanji.md).

The second essential is a spaced repetition system, for the reasons set out in section 1.2. **Anki** is the standard: free on computers, Android (as AnkiDroid) and the web, and a one-time purchase of US $24.99 on iPhone and iPad as AnkiMobile. It is plain to look at and endlessly configurable, and since version 23.10 it has offered FSRS, a newer scheduling algorithm that usually means fewer reviews for the same retention. Shared decks exist for every JLPT level, but cards you make from sentences you met in real Japanese stick better. What matters most is that one app owns your daily review and you open it every day.

## Kanji

Kanji need a system of their own, and [section 3.4](../03-writing-system/04-studying-kanji.md) compares the main ones in detail, so here is only the short version. **WaniKani** teaches components, then kanji, then vocabulary, in a fixed order with spaced repetition built in. Its first three levels, about 100 kanji and 200 words, are free; after that it costs US $9 a month, US $89 a year or US $299 for life. Anki with a kanji deck is the free alternative and fits learners who prefer to build their own order. Either works; switching between them every month does not.

For grammar, **Bunpro** applies spaced repetition to grammar points, with example sentences for each and a reference page tied to common textbooks. Its basic pages are free, and reviews need a subscription (US $5 a month as of 2026).

## Courses and Textbooks

For the first year, a structured course keeps you moving and makes sure nothing basic gets skipped. Four are widely used:

| Course | Level | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| *Genki*, 3rd edition (The Japan Times) | Beginner, about N5–N4 | The most common university textbook in English-speaking countries: two volumes, 23 chapters, with workbooks and free audio in the publisher's OTO Navi app |
| *Minna no Nihongo* (3A Corporation) | Beginner, about N5–N4 | Written entirely in Japanese, with separate translation books; the standard in language schools in Japan, with a third edition of volume I published in October 2025 |
| *Quartet* (The Japan Times) | Intermediate, about N3–N2 | Two volumes that carry *Genki* graduates into intermediate reading, writing and discussion |
| *Tobira* (Kurosio) | Intermediate, about N3–N2 | The classic bridge to advanced Japanese, revised in 2025–2026 as two volumes, とびら I and II |

Two free options deserve to be better known. *Irodori*, from the Japan Foundation, is a complete course for everyday life in Japan at CEFR A1 to A2, with free textbooks and audio to download. *Tae Kim's Guide to Learning Japanese* (guidetojapanese.org) explains grammar from the ground up, in an order that suits self-learners, and has been a starting point for a generation of anime fans. This book is written to sit alongside any of them.

## Reading

Reading is where learners used to hit a wall, and where free resources now help most.

- **Graded readers** come first, for the reasons in section 1.2. The NPO Tadoku Supporters, the group behind tadoku.org, offers a large library of free graded stories online, from a starter level to level 5, many with audio. The longest-running printed series, *Japanese Graded Readers* (レベル別日本語多読ライブラリー, from the publisher ASK), runs from a starter level to level 4 and has over 120 books.
- **NHK News Web Easy** (news.web.nhk/news/easy/) rewrites real news in simpler Japanese for foreign residents and children, with furigana and audio for every article. [Section 12.3](../12-japanese-for-media/03-news.md) shows how to use it as a ramp to the real news.
- **Satori Reader** (satorireader.com) offers original serialized stories and articles written for learners, with tap-to-define, audio and grammar notes. A few episodes are free; the full library costs US $9 a month or US $89 a year.

## Listening and Video

Listening needs the most hours, so it needs material you enjoy. Several long-running podcasts are made for learners: *Nihongo con Teppei* has short, cheerful episodes for beginners and intermediates, *Japanese with Shun* keeps to the grammar of beginner textbooks, *The Miku Real Japanese Podcast* has natural conversation about life and culture, and *Learn Japanese with Noriko* suits intermediate and advanced listeners, with transcripts. On YouTube, the channel Natural Japanese (formerly Comprehensible Japanese) has well over a thousand videos told entirely in Japanese with pictures and gestures, sorted from complete beginner to advanced. All of these were active in September 2026. Once you can follow them, move on to the native media in [chapter 12](../12-japanese-for-media/README.md).

## Tutors and Conversation Partners

Output needs a person. **italki** and **Preply** connect you with Japanese tutors by video, from professional teachers to informal conversation partners; on Preply, the typical rate in September 2026 was about US $20 for a 50-minute session, with a wide range on either side. A good tutor corrects your vowel length and pitch, which no app does reliably, and turns your weekly study into conversation. One 30-minute session a week is enough to transform a self-study routine.

**HelloTalk** and **Tandem** match you with Japanese speakers who are learning English, for free text, voice and video exchange. A language exchange works best with structure: agree on half the time in each language, and ask your partner to correct one thing per message. Closer to home, look for Japanese conversation tables at universities and libraries, Japan societies and Japanese community events.

> **Tip:** Go do this today. Book a 30-minute trial session with a tutor and ask them, in English if you must, to listen for long vowels and double consonants in your speech. Half an hour with a teacher's ear will tell you more about your pronunciation than a month of apps.

## Typing Japanese

You will type Japanese long before you write much of it by hand. An **input method editor** (IME), 日本語入力 (*nihongo nyūryoku*, Japanese input) in Japanese, turns what you type into kana and then offers kanji to replace it. Every phone and computer has one built in.

| Device | Where to switch it on |
|---|---|
| iPhone and iPad | Settings > General > Keyboard > Keyboards > Add New Keyboard > Japanese, then choose Romaji, Kana or both |
| Android (Gboard) | Gboard settings > Languages > Add keyboard > Japanese, then choose a layout: 12-key or QWERTY |
| Windows 11 | Settings > Time & language > Language & region > Add a language > Japanese; the Microsoft IME comes with it |
| Mac | System Settings > Keyboard > Text Input > Edit > + > Japanese > Japanese – Romaji |

On a computer you type in romaji and the IME shows hiragana as you go: *arigatou* becomes ありがとう. Press the space bar to convert to kanji, again to see more choices, and Enter to accept. Long vowels are typed as they are spelled in kana, so 東京 is *toukyou*, not *tōkyō*, and a dash gives the katakana long-vowel mark ー. A few more conventions are worth learning on day one:

| To get | Type | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| ん | *nn* | A single *n* works before most consonants, but *nn* is always safe: *honn* gives ほん |
| っ (small tsu) | a doubled consonant | *kitte* gives きって; *xtu* or *ltu* gives a small っ on its own |
| ぁぃぅぇぉ (small vowels) | *x* or *l* before the vowel | *xa* gives ぁ; useful for loanwords such as ファ |
| Katakana | F7 after typing | *koohii*, then F7, gives コーヒー; the IME also suggests katakana for many loanwords on its own |
| Hiragana, romaji | F6, F10 | F6 turns the input back into hiragana; F10 into plain letters |

Switch input on and off with Alt plus the backquote key (or the half-width/full-width key on Japanese keyboards) on Windows, and Control plus the space bar, or the globe key, on a Mac. On a phone, many Japanese people type with **flick input** (フリック入力, *furikku nyūryoku*) on the 12-key layout: each key holds one row of the kana chart, and you tap for the *a* sound or flick left, up, right or down for *i*, *u*, *e* and *o*. It looks like magic and is faster than romaji once learned, but romaji input is fine for years.

> **Warning:** The IME picks the kanji for you, and it can pick wrong. 帰る (かえる, *kaeru*, to go home), 変える (*kaeru*, to change) and 蛙 (*kaeru*, frog) all sit behind the same keystrokes. Read the suggestion before you press Enter, or your message about going home will be about a frog.

Typing also has a hidden cost: you will recognize far more kanji than you can write by hand, a gap so common among Japanese people themselves that they have a word for forgetting how to write a kanji, 漢字が書けない. Whether that matters depends on your goals, and [section 3.4](../03-writing-system/04-studying-kanji.md) suggests writing by hand now and then for the sake of memory.

## Putting It Together

A complete, inexpensive toolkit for a beginner looks like this:

| Part of the loop | Tool |
|---|---|
| Study | This book, plus *Genki*, *Irodori* or Tae Kim for structured drills |
| Input | A learner podcast and Natural Japanese videos; Tadoku.org graded readers, then NHK News Web Easy |
| Output | A weekly tutor session; shadowing with a podcast transcript |
| Review | Anki or WaniKani, every day |
| Lookup | Jisho, Takoboto or Shirabe Jisho on your phone; Yomitan in your browser |

[Resources for Learning Japanese](../reference/03-resources.md), in the back matter, collects every resource recommended in this book by category.

## Summary

- Choose one tool for each part of the learning loop and use it daily; sampling many apps wastes the minutes you have.
- A dictionary (Jisho, Takoboto or Shirabe Jisho, plus Yomitan in a browser) and a spaced repetition app (Anki or WaniKani) are the essential two.
- A structured course such as *Genki*, *Minna no Nihongo* or the free *Irodori* carries you through the first year; *Quartet* and *Tobira* bridge to intermediate.
- Graded readers, NHK News Web Easy and learner podcasts carry you into real input; a tutor on italki or Preply corrects the vowel length and pitch that apps miss.
- Switch on a Japanese input method on every device: type in romaji, press space for kanji and F7 for katakana, and check each kanji suggestion before you accept it.
