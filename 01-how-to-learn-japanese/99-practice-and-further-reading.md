# Practice and Further Reading

This chapter is about method, so most of its practice asks you to plan, notice and set up rather than memorize, with a first taste of Japanese words and sentences along the way. Do the items in writing, and say every Japanese word aloud. The plan you write in item 26 is worth keeping where you will see it, and worth revisiting in three months. Check your answers at the back of the book once you have tried every item.

## Practice

### 1.1 Why Japanese

1. Translate into English: (a) 私は寿司を食べます。(*Watashi wa sushi o tabemasu.*) (b) 姉は寿司を食べます。(*Ane wa sushi o tabemasu.*) (c) 友達は寿司を食べます。(*Tomodachi wa sushi o tabemasu.*) What changes in the Japanese verb from one sentence to the next?
2. 本 (*hon*) means "book". How would you translate 本を読みます (*hon o yomimasu*) if you were talking about (a) a single novel, (b) a pile of comics? What in the Japanese tells you which?
3. Read these words aloud, then match each to its meaning: おばさん (*obasan*), おばあさん (*obāsan*), きて (*kite*), きって (*kitte*); grandmother, stamp, aunt, come. Which two features of Japanese sound do the pairs show?
4. 行く？ (*Iku?*) is a complete question in casual Japanese. Give three possible English translations, and say what would tell you which one is meant.
5. Put the English gloss in Japanese order: "The sushi I ate with a friend yesterday was delicious." Start with "yesterday" and end with "was delicious".
6. Name four things that make Japanese easier than its reputation, and the five real hurdles.
7. The FSI's estimate for Japanese is about 2,200 class hours. Why is that number misleading as a forecast for someone who wants to travel and chat?
8. What would you say to the staff as you leave a restaurant after a meal? Write it in kana and in romaji.

### 1.2 Study and Immersion: The Middle Path

9. Explain in one or two sentences what comprehensible input is, and why a Japanese late-night talk show in your second week is not a good example of it.
10. Put the four steps of the learning loop in order: review, output, study, input.
11. Rewrite this weak flashcard as a strong one: front 会う (*au*), back "to meet".
12. A learner adds 60 new flashcards on Monday. What problem will they have the following week, and what should they do instead?
13. In the experiment by Karpicke and Roediger (2008), what did the two groups do differently, and how much did each recall a week later? What does that mean for how you use your notes?
14. What makes high-variability phonetic training "high-variability", and why does the variety help? Name two Japanese contrasts worth training this way.
15. A graded story is 400 words long. How many unknown words can it contain if you want 98% coverage? And at 95%?
16. Describe the three steps of a shadowing session with a one-minute recording.
17. You know the kanji 手 (hand) and 紙 (paper). Guess the meaning of 手紙 (てがみ, *tegami*), then check it in a dictionary. What does the result teach you about learning kanji only one at a time?
18. A friend says they are a "visual learner" and only study with pictures. What does the research say, and what would you suggest instead?

### 1.3 Apps, Courses and Tools

19. Which part of the learning loop (study, input, output or review) does each resource mainly serve: Anki, italki, NHK News Web Easy, a learner podcast, Genki? Which tool from section 1.3 supports every part of the loop?
20. On a computer with a Japanese input method in romaji mode, what do you type to get (a) ありがとう, (b) きって, (c) ほん, (d) ちょっと?
21. You have typed *koohii* and the screen shows こーひー. Which key turns it into katakana コーヒー on Windows or a Mac? Which key converts kana to kanji?
22. How do you type a small ぁ or a small っ on its own, without a following consonant?

### 1.4 The JLPT Map

23. Put the JLPT levels in order from easiest to hardest, and give the approximate kanji and vocabulary counts commonly cited for N5 and N3. Why does this book call them estimates?
24. Name two skills the JLPT does not test, and one way to train each of them alongside exam preparation.
25. You want to work in Japan under the Specified Skilled Worker program and need a test you can take often, by computer. Which test might suit you better than the JLPT?
26. Write your own plan: your current level, the JLPT level you want to reach in twelve months, how many minutes a day you can give, and one resource for each part of the learning loop.

## Further Reading

- [JLPT official site](https://www.jlpt.jp/e/) — level summaries, sample questions, the CEFR reference table and test dates, straight from the organizers.
- [Tadoku.org](https://tadoku.org/japanese/en/) — the home of Japanese extensive reading, with the rules of 多読, a level chart and free graded readers with audio.
- *Make It Stick: The Science of Successful Learning*, by Peter Brown, Henry Roediger and Mark McDaniel (2014) — a readable account of retrieval practice, spacing and the other memory research behind section 1.2.
- *Principles and Practice in Second Language Acquisition*, by Stephen Krashen (1982) — the original statement of the input hypothesis, free on the author's website; read it alongside the critiques in section 1.2.
- Hu and Nation, "Unknown vocabulary density and reading comprehension", *Reading in a Foreign Language* (2000) — the study behind the 98% coverage figure, free online.
- Hirata and Kelly, "Effects of lips and hands on auditory learning of second-language speech sounds", *Journal of Speech, Language, and Hearing Research* (2010) — a clear, readable experiment on training English speakers to hear Japanese vowel length.
- Flaherty and Noguchi, "Effectiveness of different approaches to kanji education with second language learners", *JALT Journal* (1998) — the component-versus-rote kanji experiment from section 1.2, free from the publisher.
- Pashler, McDaniel, Rohrer and Bjork, "Learning styles: Concepts and evidence", *Psychological Science in the Public Interest* (2008) — the review that took apart the learning-styles myth.
