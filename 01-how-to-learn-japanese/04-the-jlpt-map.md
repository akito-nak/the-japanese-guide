# The JLPT Map

"How good is your Japanese?" is a hard question to answer, and Japanese people make it harder by telling you 日本語が上手ですね (*nihongo ga jōzu desu ne*, your Japanese is so good) the moment you say こんにちは. A map with numbered stops helps: it tells you where you are, what the next stop asks of you, and roughly how far it is. For Japanese the standard map is the **JLPT**, the Japanese-Language Proficiency Test, run by the Japan Foundation outside Japan and by Japan Educational Exchanges and Services inside it. In 2025 it drew about 1.9 million applicants, the most in its history.

You never have to sit it to use it. Its five levels are the closest thing Japanese has to a shared yardstick, and textbooks, graded readers, apps and job ads all label themselves by JLPT level. This section gives the layout of the map and the key numbers, dated as of 2026. [Chapter 14](../14-the-jlpt/README.md) gives the full treatment: the test format, scoring, dates and fees, and what each level asks of you.

## Five Levels, From N5 to N1

The levels run backward from what you might expect: N5 is the easiest and N1 the hardest. The N stands for "Nihongo" and also, the organizers say, for "New", because the levels were redrawn in 2010. Each level comes with a short official description, and the table adds what that tends to mean in daily life.

| Level | Official summary | What it lets you do, roughly |
|---|---|---|
| N5 | Can understand some basic Japanese | Read kana and about 100 kanji; greet people, order, ask simple questions; follow slow, simple speech |
| N4 | Can understand basic Japanese | Get around Japan on your own; hold simple conversations on familiar topics; read short messages |
| N3 | Can understand Japanese used in everyday situations to a certain degree | Follow everyday conversation at near-natural speed; read simple articles and manga with a dictionary |
| N2 | Can understand Japanese used in everyday situations, and in a variety of circumstances to a certain degree | Read newspapers and follow TV news; work in many Japanese workplaces |
| N1 | Can understand Japanese used in a variety of circumstances | Read editorials and literature; follow lectures and debates; work and study fully in Japanese |

The right-hand column is this book's summary of learners' experience, not part of the test. The jump between levels is not even: the step from N4 to N3 and the step from N3 to N2 are where most learners spend the longest, because that is where textbook Japanese gives way to the real thing.

## Words, Kanji and Why Nobody Agrees

Learners understandably want harder numbers, and the JLPT doesn't provide them. Since the 2010 revision, the organizers have not published official lists of vocabulary, kanji or grammar, on the grounds that the goal is to communicate, not to memorize lists. The numbers you see everywhere are **community estimates**, mostly traced back to the official lists of the pre-2010 test, which had four levels instead of five.

| Level | Kanji (est.) | Words (est.) |
|---|---|---|
| N5 | about 100 | about 800 |
| N4 | about 300 | about 1,500 |
| N3 | about 650 | about 3,750 |
| N2 | about 1,000 | about 6,000 |
| N1 | about 2,000 | about 10,000 |

The pattern matters more than the exact figures. The two beginner levels are gentle: 1,500 words and 300 kanji get you through N4. Then each level roughly doubles your vocabulary, and by N1 you know close to all of the 2,136 常用漢字 (じょうようかんじ, *jōyō kanji*, kanji for everyday use). Different apps and textbooks use different lists and disagree at the edges; pick one and use it consistently.

## Levels and the CEFR

People often ask how JLPT levels compare with the Common European Framework of Reference for Languages, the **CEFR**, whose six levels from A1 to C2 are used for most European languages. Since the December 2025 test, the JLPT has answered officially: score reports now carry a CEFR reference level, based on a standard-setting study by experts in Japan and abroad in October 2024. The reference depends on your score within a level, not only on the level you passed.

| JLPT level | Total score (out of 180) | CEFR reference |
|---|---|---|
| N5 | 80 and above (a pass) | A1 |
| N4 | 90 and above (a pass) | A2 |
| N3 | 95–103 | A2 |
| N3 | 104 and above | B1 |
| N2 | 90–111 | B1 |
| N2 | 112 and above | B2 |
| N1 | 100–141 | B2 |
| N1 | 142 and above | C1 |

Two things stand out. A bare pass at N3 is still A2, and a bare pass at N2 is B1, so the CEFR reading is more modest than the level names suggest. And no JLPT score reaches C2. The reference covers only what the test measures, language knowledge, reading and listening, and says nothing about speaking or writing.

## How Many Hours?

Every learner wants to know how long each level takes, and the honest answer is that nobody has measured it well for self-learners. Two sets of figures are widely quoted, and it helps to know where each comes from.

The first is official but old. The pre-2010 JLPT published the classroom hours its four levels assumed: about 150 hours for the old level 4 (roughly today's N5), 300 for level 3 (N4), 600 for level 2 (N2) and 900 for level 1 (N1). Those figures count class time only, and learners have long found them far too low.

The second is a record kept by one language school in Tokyo, the Japan Language Education Center, of how many hours its full-time students took to pass each level between 2010 and 2015, counting class, homework and self-study. For students with no background in kanji, which covers most English speakers, the averages ranged as follows:

| Level | Total hours (JLEC students, 2010–2015) |
|---|---|
| N5 | 325–600 |
| N4 | 575–1,000 |
| N3 | 950–1,700 |
| N2 | 1,600–2,800 |
| N1 | 3,000–4,800 |

Treat both columns as orders of magnitude, not promises. These were full-time students living in Japan, surrounded by Japanese; a self-learner gets less incidental input and may need more hours, or may need fewer by focusing on exactly what the test needs. The figures also line up with the Foreign Service Institute's estimate from [section 1.1](01-why-japanese.md): its 2,200 class hours to professional proficiency, plus the homework the FSI assumes alongside them, lands around N2. At an hour a day, by these figures, N5 is about a year away for most beginners, N4 about two and N2 something like five or six.

> **Tip:** Set your goal one level up, not three. "Pass N4 next December" gives you a word list, a date and a weekly target; "become fluent" gives you none of those. When you reach it, the next level is already on the map.

## What the Map Leaves Out

The JLPT tests vocabulary, grammar, reading and listening, all by multiple choice. It has no speaking and no writing at any level, and as of 2026 the organizers have announced no plans to add either. That is the map's biggest blank. Plenty of N2 holders freeze in casual conversation because they have never had to produce Japanese under pressure, and plenty of fluent speakers who learned in Japanese workplaces fail N2 on kanji. Use the levels to measure the input side of the learning loop, and measure the output side some other way: by recording yourself every few months, or by how a tutor's conversations change.

Several other tests fill different gaps. The **JFT-Basic**, from the Japan Foundation, is a computer-based test of everyday Japanese held roughly monthly at test centers and used for Japan's Specified Skilled Worker visa; since August 2026 it reports levels from A1 to A2. The **BJT** Business Japanese Proficiency Test, run by the Japan Kanji Aptitude Testing Foundation, scores business communication on a scale of 0 to 800 and suits learners heading into Japanese companies, a world that [chapter 13](../13-business-japanese/README.md) covers. The J.TEST (実用日本語検定, *Jitsuyō Nihongo Kentei*, Test of Practical Japanese) is held six times a year and includes written answers. For speaking, the oral proficiency interview from ACTFL, the American Council on the Teaching of Foreign Languages, is the best-known test. None of these has the JLPT's recognition, but each measures something the JLPT doesn't.

## Summary

- The JLPT's five levels run from N5 (easiest) to N1 (hardest), and its labels are the common yardstick for Japanese learning materials, whether or not you sit the test.
- Official word and kanji lists have not been published since 2010; commonly cited estimates run from about 800 words and 100 kanji at N5 to about 10,000 words and 2,000 kanji at N1.
- Since December 2025, score reports carry an official CEFR reference: from A1 at N5 to C1 for a high score at N1, with a bare N3 pass at A2 and a bare N2 pass at B1.
- Study-hour figures are rough: one school's full-time students without a kanji background took about 1,600 to 2,800 hours to pass N2 and 3,000 to 4,800 to pass N1.
- The JLPT tests no speaking or writing; the JFT-Basic, BJT, J.TEST and oral interviews such as ACTFL's measure other things.
