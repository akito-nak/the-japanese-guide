# Phrases and Idioms — Sound Like You Belong

A colleague packs up at six and says お先に失礼します (*osaki ni shitsurei shimasu*, "excuse me for leaving before you"). Everyone left at their desks answers お疲れ様です (*otsukaresama desu*, roughly "thanks for your hard work"). Nobody is apologizing and nobody has done anything especially tiring; the exchange is how a Japanese office says goodbye. Translate it word for word and it makes no sense. Know it, and you sound like you belong.

Japanese runs on expressions like this: **set phrases** that are said in a fixed form at a fixed moment, plus idioms, proverbs and a dazzling range of sound words. Many are used so constantly that missing them means missing half of what is happening around you. This section gives you the phrases for day one, the social glue that keeps conversation smooth and a first taste of the idioms that make Japanese so vivid.

## Survival Phrases

These are the sentences you will reach for in your first week in Japan. Each one is a pattern from [section 2.1](01-grammar-basics.md) with vocabulary from [section 2.2](02-core-vocabulary.md) slotted in.

### At a Restaurant

| Japanese | Romaji | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| いらっしゃいませ！ | *Irasshaimase!* | Welcome! (staff to customers; you don't reply in kind) |
| 〜をひとつください。 | *… o hitotsu kudasai.* | One …, please. |
| これをください。 | *Kore o kudasai.* | I'll have this one. |
| おすすめは何ですか。 | *Osusume wa nan desu ka.* | What do you recommend? |
| 辛いですか。 | *Karai desu ka.* | Is it spicy? |
| アレルギーがあります。 | *Arerugī ga arimasu.* | I have an allergy. |
| お会計をお願いします。 | *Okaikei o onegaishimasu.* | The check, please. |
| 別々でお願いします。 | *Betsubetsu de onegaishimasu.* | Separate checks, please. |
| いただきます。 | *Itadakimasu.* | Said before eating: "I humbly receive" |
| ごちそうさまでした。 | *Gochisōsama deshita.* | Said after eating: "thank you for the meal" (to the host, the cook or the staff on your way out) |

> *Sumimasen, okaikei o onegaishimasu.*\
> Excuse me, could we have the check, please?

## Social Glue: Phrases People Say All the Time

These phrases fill everyday conversation. None of them translates neatly, and all of them make you sound more natural the moment you start using them.

| Phrase | Reading | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| お疲れ様です | *otsukaresama desu* | "Thanks for your hard work": said to coworkers constantly, in passing, when leaving for the day, when someone finishes a task |
| ご苦労様です | *gokurōsama desu* | "Thank you for your trouble": traditionally said only downward, to staff or juniors |
| よろしくお願いします | *yoroshiku onegaishimasu* | "Please treat me well" or "I'm counting on you": at introductions, with requests and at the end of emails |
| お世話になっております | *osewa ni natte orimasu* | "Thank you for your continued support": the standard opening of a business email or call |
| なるほど | *naruhodo* | "I see, that makes sense": shows you are following |
| そうですね | *sō desu ne* | "Indeed" or "let me see": thoughtful agreement, or buying time to think |
| ちょっと… | *chotto…* | "That's a little…": a polite, vague refusal |
| 大丈夫です | *daijōbu desu* | "It's fine" or "I'm OK": can mean yes or no depending on the question |
| 頑張ってください | *ganbatte kudasai* | "Do your best, good luck": before exams, games and challenges |
| 頑張ります | *ganbarimasu* | "I'll do my best": the reply |
| お邪魔します | *ojama shimasu* | "Sorry to intrude": said when entering someone's home |
| ただいま / おかえり | *tadaima* / *okaeri* | "I'm home" / "welcome back": the pair said every time someone comes home |
| いってきます / いってらっしゃい | *ittekimasu* / *itterasshai* | "I'm off" / "see you later": the pair said every time someone leaves |

> **Warning:** Don't say ご苦労様です to your boss or a teacher. It is traditionally reserved for people below you, so saying it upward can sound presumptuous. お疲れ様です is safe in every direction.

> いってきます！いってらっしゃい！\
> *Ittekimasu! Itterasshai!*\
> I'm off! See you later!

The business phrases here (お世話になっております and its relatives) get a full treatment in [chapter 11](../11-business-japanese/README.md).

## Context-Dependent Phrases

A handful of everyday phrases change meaning completely depending on the situation and the tone of voice. These four cause more confusion than any others.

### 大丈夫: The Wildcard

大丈夫 (*daijōbu*) means "all right", and that is exactly the problem.

- 大丈夫ですか。 Are you OK?
- 大丈夫です。 It can mean "I'm fine", "no, thank you" or "don't worry about it".

When a convenience store clerk asks 袋は大丈夫ですか (*fukuro wa daijōbu desu ka*, "are you OK for a bag?"), the reply 大丈夫です means "no bag needed". Always ask yourself what question came before it.

### ちょっと: The Soft Refusal

On its face, ちょっと (*chotto*) means "a little" or "just a moment". In a reply to an invitation, though, a trailing ちょっと… means "no", and pressing for a reason is awkward. Japanese conversation generally prefers soft, indirect refusals to a blunt いいえ.

> 土曜日はちょっと…\
> *Doyōbi wa chotto…*\
> Saturday is a bit… (Saturday doesn't work for me.)

### なんか and なんて: Softeners

なんか (*nanka*) and なんて (*nante*) blur and soften a statement, much like "sort of" or "kind of", and can add a note of self-deprecation.

- なんか面白い (*nanka omoshiroi*) — it's sort of interesting.
- 私なんて… (*watashi nante…*) — someone like me…

### よろしく: The Phrase That Does Everything

よろしく (*yoroshiku*), in full よろしくお願いします, is used in emails, meetings, introductions, requests and farewells:

- At an introduction — "I look forward to getting to know you."
- At the end of an email — "Best regards" or "thanks in advance".
- After handing off a task — "I'm counting on you."
- Between friends, as a casual よろしく！ — "Thanks!" when asking a favor.

## Fillers and Aizuchi

Fillers keep a conversation flowing and show that you are thinking or listening.

| Filler | Meaning or use |
|---|---|
| えーと / あの | Um… / Uh… (a pause to think; あの also gets attention politely) |
| そうですね… | "Let me see…" (politely buying time) |
| なるほど | "I see; that makes sense" |
| うん | Yeah (casual agreement) |
| へー！ | "Oh, really?" (surprise or interest) |
| まあね | "Well, sort of; I guess" |
| 確かに | "True; that's right" |
| それはそうと | "By the way" (changing the topic) |

Japanese conversation is full of **aizuchi** (相槌), the short responses a listener gives to show they are following: うん, はい, なるほど, そうですね, へー. Listeners give them often, along with small nods, while the other person is still talking. To many Japanese speakers, a listener who stays completely silent can seem uninterested, so sprinkle aizuchi into your conversations from the start. It feels odd at first and natural within a week.

## Idioms: Japanese Has Some Great Ones

Japanese idioms are full of vivid imagery, and many have a close English cousin. These are all in everyday use.

| Idiom | Reading | Literal meaning | Actual meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 七転び八起き | *nana korobi ya oki* | Fall down seven times, get up eight | Never give up |
| 猫に小判 | *neko ni koban* | Gold coins to a cat | Pearls before swine: something valuable wasted on someone who can't appreciate it |
| 石の上にも三年 | *ishi no ue ni mo san nen* | Three years even on a stone | Patience pays off: stick with something long enough and it works out |
| 井の中の蛙 | *i no naka no kawazu* | A frog in a well | Someone with a narrow view of the world |
| 花より団子 | *hana yori dango* | Dumplings over flowers | Substance over style: the snacks matter more than the cherry blossoms |
| 取らぬ狸の皮算用 | *toranu tanuki no kawazan'yō* | Counting the skins of raccoon dogs you haven't caught | Don't count your chickens before they hatch |
| 二兎を追う者は一兎をも得ず | *nito o ou mono wa itto o mo ezu* | Chase two rabbits and you catch neither | Focus on one thing at a time |
| 馬の耳に念仏 | *uma no mimi ni nenbutsu* | A Buddhist prayer in a horse's ear | Advice wasted on someone who won't listen |
| 目から鱗 | *me kara uroko* | Scales from the eyes | A sudden revelation |
| 一石二鳥 | *isseki nichō* | One stone, two birds | Kill two birds with one stone |
| 棚から牡丹餅 | *tana kara botamochi* | A rice cake falling off a shelf | An unexpected windfall |
| 口は災いの元 | *kuchi wa wazawai no moto* | The mouth is the source of disaster | Careless words cause trouble |

> 一石二鳥ですね。\
> *Isseki nichō desu ne.*\
> That kills two birds with one stone, doesn't it?

## Four-Kanji Idioms

**Yojijukugo** (四字熟語, *yojijukugo*, "four-character compounds") are idioms written in exactly four kanji, many of them borrowed from classical Chinese or Buddhist texts. They appear in speeches, essays, school mottos and even casual conversation, and using one well makes a strong impression.

| Expression | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 一期一会 | *ichigo ichie* | "One time, one meeting": treasure each encounter, because it will never come again |
| 自業自得 | *jigō jitoku* | "Your own deeds, your own reward": you reap what you sow |
| 八方美人 | *happō bijin* | "Beautiful in all eight directions": a people-pleaser who tries to agree with everyone |
| 臨機応変 | *rinki ōhen* | Adapting flexibly to circumstances |
| 以心伝心 | *ishin denshin* | "Mind to mind": understanding each other without words |
| 七転八倒 | *shichiten battō* | Writhing in agony |
| 一日千秋 | *ichijitsu senshū* (also *ichinichi senshū*) | "One day, a thousand autumns": longing intensely for something |
| 十人十色 | *jūnin toiro* | "Ten people, ten colors": to each their own |
| 温故知新 | *onko chishin* | "Revisit the old to understand the new": learn from the past |
| 大器晩成 | *taiki bansei* | "Great vessels take long to complete": great talents bloom late |

## Proverbs

**Kotowaza** (諺 or ことわざ, *kotowaza*) are traditional proverbs, usually a short sentence with a moral in it. These are among the most familiar.

| Proverb | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 案ずるより産むが易し | *anzuru yori umu ga yasushi* | "Giving birth is easier than worrying about it": doing is easier than dreading |
| 急がば回れ | *isogaba maware* | "If you're in a hurry, go the long way round": shortcuts backfire |
| 笑う門には福来る | *warau kado ni wa fuku kitaru* | "Good fortune comes to the house that laughs" |
| 知らぬが仏 | *shiranu ga hotoke* | "Not knowing is Buddha": ignorance is bliss |
| 出る杭は打たれる | *deru kui wa utareru* | "The stake that sticks out gets hammered down": standing out invites criticism |
| 論より証拠 | *ron yori shōko* | "Proof over argument": show, don't tell |
| 継続は力なり | *keizoku wa chikara nari* | "Continuing is strength": consistency wins |

> **Note:** 出る杭は打たれる is quoted often in discussions of Japanese social life, where it is used to explain why people may hesitate to stand out at school or work. Treat it as one lens among many: plenty of Japanese people stand out happily, and the proverb is as often said with a wry smile as with approval.

継続は力なり deserves a place on every language learner's wall. Fifteen minutes every day tends to beat a five-hour weekend binge.

## Sound Words: Far Beyond "Woof" and "Meow"

Japanese has a famously rich stock of sound-symbolic words, often called onomatopoeia in English. Linguists split them into two groups: **giongo** (擬音語, *giongo*), which imitate actual sounds, and **gitaigo** (擬態語, *gitaigo*), which depict states, movements and feelings that make no sound at all. The line between the two is blurry (ドキドキ is both a heartbeat and a feeling), but the idea is clear. They appear constantly in conversation, advertising and especially manga, where they are often written in katakana for visual punch; in ordinary prose they are just as often in hiragana.

### Sounds (擬音語)

| Word | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ザーザー | *zāzā* | pouring rain |
| ドカン | *dokan* | boom, a big bang |
| ゴロゴロ | *gorogoro* | rumbling thunder; something rolling; lounging around |
| ガタガタ | *gatagata* | rattling, shaking |
| ドキドキ | *dokidoki* | a pounding heart (from excitement, nerves or fear) |

### States and Feelings (擬態語)

| Word | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ワクワク | *wakuwaku* | excited anticipation |
| ピカピカ | *pikapika* | sparkling clean, shining |
| キラキラ | *kirakira* | glittering, twinkling |
| サラサラ | *sarasara* | smooth, silky, flowing |
| フワフワ | *fuwafuwa* | fluffy, soft and light |
| バラバラ | *barabara* | scattered, in pieces |
| ニコニコ | *nikoniko* | smiling happily |
| イライラ | *iraira* | irritated, on edge |
| ウキウキ | *ukiuki* | cheerful, buoyant |
| ムカムカ | *mukamuka* | queasy; seething |
| ボーっとする | *bōtto suru* | to space out, to daydream |
| グズグズ | *guzuguzu* | dawdling, dithering |
| ダラダラ | *daradara* | sluggishly, lazily |
| ペラペラ | *perapera* | fluent (in a language) |
| ビクビク | *bikubiku* | jumpy, nervous |

> 彼女は日本語がペラペラです。\
> *Kanojo wa nihongo ga perapera desu.*\
> She's fluent in Japanese.

[Section 10.2](../10-japanese-for-media/02-manga.md) shows how manga uses these words as sound effects, and the goal of this whole book is to earn ペラペラ for yourself.

## Phrases by Level

One sentence for each stage, to show how far the same politeness can stretch.

> すみません。ここはどこですか。\
> *Sumimasen. Koko wa doko desu ka.*\
> Excuse me, where am I? (beginner)
>
> お時間があればお話ししたいのですが。\
> *Ojikan ga areba ohanashi shitai no desu ga.*\
> If you have time, I'd like to talk with you. (intermediate)
>
> ご指摘の点、真摯に受け止めております。\
> *Goshiteki no ten, shinshi ni uketomete orimasu.*\
> I take the point you raised very seriously. (advanced; a formal business response to criticism)

The last sentence uses the honorific and humble forms taught in [section 3.4](../03-politeness/04-honorific-japanese.md).

## Summary

- Set phrases such as いただきます, ごちそうさまでした, ただいま and いってきます are said in a fixed form at a fixed moment, and using them is a large part of sounding natural.
- お疲れ様です is the all-purpose workplace greeting and is safe in any direction, while ご苦労様です is traditionally said only to people below you.
- 大丈夫です and ちょっと… often work as polite refusals, so interpret them by the question that came before.
- Aizuchi such as うん, はい and なるほど show you are listening, and a completely silent listener can seem uninterested.
- Japanese idioms include everyday sayings (猫に小判), four-kanji compounds called yojijukugo (一期一会) and proverbs called kotowaza (継続は力なり).
- Sound words divide into giongo, which imitate real sounds (ザーザー), and gitaigo, which depict states and feelings (ワクワク, ペラペラ).
