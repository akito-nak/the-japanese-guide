# Grammar Basics — How Japanese Sentences Actually Work

Take the English sentence "I eat sushi" and say it in Japanese. You get 私は寿司を食べます: literally "I [topic] sushi [object] eat." The verb has wandered to the very end, and two small syllables, は and を, have appeared to tell you who is doing what. That one sentence holds most of what makes Japanese grammar feel strange at first, and most of what makes it logical once it clicks.

Japanese grammar is at once simpler than English and more different from it. There is no grammatical gender, no plural form for most nouns and no articles: nothing corresponds to "a", "an" or "the". In exchange, the verb goes last, a small particle marks the role of every noun, and politeness is built into the verb itself. Learn those three ideas and you can read the skeleton of almost any sentence.

## The Core Structure: Subject, Object, Verb

English puts the verb in the middle: subject, verb, object. Japanese uses **SOV** order: subject, object, then verb.

> 私は寿司を食べます。\
> *Watashi wa sushi o tabemasu.*\
> I eat sushi. (I [topic] sushi [object] eat.)

The verb comes at the end of the sentence in standard Japanese. Time expressions, places, adjectives and everything else line up before it. Because each noun carries its own role marker, the order of the pieces before the verb is fairly flexible: the time often comes first, but it does not have to.

> 明日、私は東京に行きます。\
> *Ashita, watashi wa tōkyō ni ikimasu.*\
> Tomorrow I am going to Tokyo. (Tomorrow, I [topic] Tokyo [to] go.)

> 私は明日東京に行きます。\
> *Watashi wa ashita tōkyō ni ikimasu.*\
> I am going to Tokyo tomorrow.

Both sentences mean the same thing. What does not move is the verb, so the habit to build is patience: in Japanese, you find out what happened at the very end.

## Particles: The Traffic Signs of Japanese

A **particle** (助詞, *joshi*) is a short word, usually one or two hiragana, placed directly after a word to show its role in the sentence. English uses word order and prepositions for this job; Japanese uses particles, which is why the word order before the verb can move around. Particles are the single most important grammar point in this chapter.

The essential particles, each with one example:

| Particle | Role | Example |
|---|---|---|
| は (*wa*) | Topic marker, "as for X" | 私**は**学生です。*Watashi wa gakusei desu.* I am a student. |
| が (*ga*) | Subject marker; new information, emphasis | 猫**が**好きです。*Neko ga suki desu.* I like cats. |
| を (*o*) | Object marker, what the verb acts on | 水**を**飲む。*Mizu o nomu.* Drink water. |
| に (*ni*) | Destination, location of existence, time | 東京**に**行く。*Tōkyō ni iku.* Go to Tokyo. |
| で (*de*) | Location of an action; means or method | 電車**で**来た。*Densha de kita.* Came by train. |
| と (*to*) | And (between nouns); together with | 友達**と**行く。*Tomodachi to iku.* Go with a friend. |
| の (*no*) | Possession; links two nouns | 私**の**本。*Watashi no hon.* My book. |
| も (*mo*) | Also, too | 私**も**行く。*Watashi mo iku.* I'm going too. |
| か (*ka*) | Question marker | これは何です**か**。*Kore wa nan desu ka.* What is this? |
| ね (*ne*) | Seeking agreement, "right?" | 寒いです**ね**。*Samui desu ne.* It's cold, isn't it? |
| よ (*yo*) | Assertion, "I'm telling you" | 知ってます**よ**。*Shittemasu yo.* I know, you know. |
| から (*kara*) | From; because | 東京**から**来た。*Tōkyō kara kita.* Came from Tokyo. |
| まで (*made*) | Until, up to | 5時**まで**働く。*Goji made hataraku.* Work until five. |
| より (*yori*) | Than (comparison) | 猫**より**犬が好き。*Neko yori inu ga suki.* I like dogs more than cats. |

Two spelling quirks deserve a flag. The topic particle is written は but pronounced *wa*, and the object particle is written を and pronounced *o*. Both are leftovers of older pronunciation, and both show up on every page you will ever read.

> **Tip:** に and で both translate as "at" or "in", which makes them easy to mix up. Use に for where something *exists* or where you are *going* (公園に犬がいる, "there is a dog in the park") and で for where an *action happens* (公園で走る, "run in the park").

### は Versus が: The Famous Puzzle

No grammar point in Japanese has filled more pages than the difference between は and が. The practical version fits in two lines:

- は marks the **topic**: what the sentence is about, usually something already known or being contrasted ("as for X…").
- が marks the grammatical subject of a clause, especially when it is new information, the answer to a question or the focus of emphasis.

The classic example uses both at once:

> 象は鼻が長い。\
> *Zō wa hana ga nagai.*\
> Elephants have long trunks. (As for elephants [topic], the trunk [subject] is long.)

Question words such as 誰 (*dare*, who) and 何 (*nani*, what) take が, and so does the answer, because both are the new information:

> 誰が来ましたか。田中さんが来ました。\
> *Dare ga kimashita ka. Tanaka-san ga kimashita.*\
> Who came? Tanaka came.

Don't let this one stall you early on. A wrong は or が rarely makes you hard to understand, and the feel for it grows with every page of real Japanese you read.

## Verbs: Three Groups and a Lot of Conjugation

Every Japanese verb in its dictionary form ends in a sound from the う (*u*) row: く, す, む, る, う and so on. Verbs fall into three groups, and the group tells you how to conjugate it.

### Group 1: U-Verbs (五段動詞, Godan)

A **godan verb** (五段動詞, *godan dōshi*, "five-step verb") changes its final syllable across the vowel rows of the kana chart. For the polite ます form, the final sound moves from the *u* row to the *i* row (書**く** → 書**き**ます); for the negative ない form, it moves to the *a* row (書**か**ない), with う becoming わ (買**う** → 買**わ**ない).

| Dictionary | Meaning | Masu form | Nai form | Te-form |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 書く (*kaku*) | write | 書きます | 書かない | 書いて |
| 話す (*hanasu*) | speak | 話します | 話さない | 話して |
| 飲む (*nomu*) | drink | 飲みます | 飲まない | 飲んで |
| 読む (*yomu*) | read | 読みます | 読まない | 読んで |
| 行く (*iku*) | go | 行きます | 行かない | 行って (irregular te-form) |
| 買う (*kau*) | buy | 買います | 買わない | 買って |
| 待つ (*matsu*) | wait | 待ちます | 待たない | 待って |

### Group 2: Ru-Verbs (一段動詞, Ichidan)

An **ichidan verb** (一段動詞, *ichidan dōshi*, "one-step verb") ends in -iru or -eru and never changes its stem: drop the final る and add the ending.

| Dictionary | Meaning | Masu form | Nai form | Te-form |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 食べる (*taberu*) | eat | 食べます | 食べない | 食べて |
| 見る (*miru*) | see | 見ます | 見ない | 見て |
| 起きる (*okiru*) | wake up | 起きます | 起きない | 起きて |
| 寝る (*neru*) | sleep | 寝ます | 寝ない | 寝て |
| 教える (*oshieru*) | teach | 教えます | 教えない | 教えて |

> **Warning:** Some verbs that look like ru-verbs are in fact u-verbs, including 帰る (*kaeru*, return home), 走る (*hashiru*, run), 入る (*hairu*, enter) and 知る (*shiru*, know). Their ます forms are 帰ります, 走ります, 入ります and 知ります, not 帰ます. These fool everyone, so learn them as a list.

### Group 3: Irregular Verbs

The **irregular verbs** are only two, and you will use them constantly. する also combines with hundreds of nouns to make new verbs: 勉強する (*benkyō suru*, study), 電話する (*denwa suru*, phone).

| Dictionary | Meaning | Masu form | Nai form | Te-form |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| する (*suru*) | do | します | しない | して |
| 来る (くる, *kuru*) | come | 来ます (きます) | 来ない (こない) | 来て (きて) |

### The Forms to Learn First

The polite **masu form** (ます形, *masu-kei*) is the everyday polite verb ending, and it is the safest form for a beginner to speak with. The dictionary form is its casual counterpart; [section 3.1](../03-politeness/01-levels-overview.md) explains when to use each. These eight forms cover most of what you will meet in your first year:

| Form | Ending | Use | Example (食べる) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dictionary | -ru / -u | Casual present or future | 食べる (*taberu*) |
| Masu | -masu | Polite present or future | 食べます (*tabemasu*) |
| Nai | -nai | Casual negative | 食べない (*tabenai*) |
| Ta | -ta / -da | Casual past | 食べた (*tabeta*) |
| Mashita | -mashita | Polite past | 食べました (*tabemashita*) |
| Te | -te / -de | Connecting form | 食べて (*tabete*) |
| Tara | -tara | Conditional, "if" or "when" | 食べたら (*tabetara*) |
| Ba | -eba / -reba | Hypothetical conditional | 食べれば (*tabereba*) |

The polite negative and past negative follow the same pattern: 食べません (*tabemasen*, don't eat) and 食べませんでした (*tabemasen deshita*, didn't eat).

## The Te-Form: The Swiss Army Knife

The **te-form** (て形, *te-kei*) is the verb form ending in て or で. It links actions, makes requests, describes ongoing states and sits inside dozens of grammar patterns, which makes it the most versatile form in the language.

For ichidan verbs it is simple: drop る, add て (食べる → 食べて). For godan verbs, the ending depends on the final kana, and learners often memorize the pattern as a song:

| Dictionary ending | Te-form ending | Example |
|---|---|---|
| う, つ, る | って | 買う → 買って, 待つ → 待って, 帰る → 帰って |
| む, ぶ, ぬ | んで | 読む → 読んで, 遊ぶ → 遊んで, 死ぬ → 死んで |
| く | いて | 書く → 書いて |
| ぐ | いで | 泳ぐ → 泳いで |
| す | して | 話す → 話して |
| 行く (exception) | って | 行く → 行って |

The casual past (た形) follows exactly the same pattern with た and だ in place of て and で: 読んだ, 書いた, 行った.

Once you have the te-form, a whole toolbox opens:

| Te-form use | Example | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Connecting actions | 食べて寝た (*tabete neta*) | Ate and slept |
| Polite request | 見てください (*mite kudasai*) | Please look |
| Ongoing action | 食べています (*tabete imasu*) | I am eating |
| Permission | 行ってもいい (*itte mo ii*) | You may go |
| Prohibition | 触ってはいけない (*sawatte wa ikenai*) | You must not touch |
| Trying something | 食べてみた (*tabete mita*) | I tried eating it |
| Doing in advance | 買っておく (*katte oku*) | Buy it in advance |
| Doing for someone | 教えてあげる (*oshiete ageru*) | I'll teach you (as a favor) |

> 駅に行って、電車に乗ってください。\
> *Eki ni itte, densha ni notte kudasai.*\
> Please go to the station and get on the train.

## Adjectives: Two Flavors

Japanese adjectives come in two kinds, named after what they end in or need.

### I-Adjectives

An **i-adjective** (い形容詞, *i-keiyōshi*) ends in い and conjugates by itself, a little like a verb: you change the final い.

| Form | Example (大きい, big) |
|---|---|
| Present positive | 大きい (*ōkii*) |
| Present negative | 大きくない (*ōkiku nai*) |
| Past positive | 大きかった (*ōkikatta*) |
| Past negative | 大きくなかった (*ōkiku nakatta*) |
| Before a noun | 大きい犬 (*ōkii inu*, a big dog) |
| As a predicate | 犬は大きい (*inu wa ōkii*, the dog is big) |

To make any of these polite, add です: 大きいです, 大きかったです.

Common i-adjectives: 大きい (*ōkii*, big), 小さい (*chiisai*, small), 高い (*takai*, tall or expensive), 安い (*yasui*, cheap), 新しい (*atarashii*, new), 古い (*furui*, old), 難しい (*muzukashii*, difficult), 易しい (*yasashii*, easy), 楽しい (*tanoshii*, fun), 悲しい (*kanashii*, sad), 暑い (*atsui*, hot) and 寒い (*samui*, cold).

> **Warning:** いい (*ii*, good) is an i-adjective with an irregular streak: every form except the plain present is built on the older form よい. The negative is よくない and the past is よかった, never いくない or いかった.

### Na-Adjectives

A **na-adjective** (な形容詞, *na-keiyōshi*) behaves like a noun. It takes な before a noun it modifies and uses the copula (です or だ) as a predicate.

| Form | Example (きれい, pretty or clean) |
|---|---|
| Present positive | きれいです (*kirei desu*) |
| Present negative | きれいじゃない (*kirei ja nai*) / きれいではありません (*kirei de wa arimasen*) |
| Past positive | きれいでした (*kirei deshita*) |
| Past negative | きれいじゃなかった (*kirei ja nakatta*) |
| Before a noun | きれいな花 (*kirei na hana*, a pretty flower) |

Common na-adjectives: きれい (*kirei*, pretty or clean), 元気 (*genki*, healthy or energetic), 好き (*suki*, liked), 嫌い (*kirai*, disliked), 有名 (*yūmei*, famous), 便利 (*benri*, convenient), 静か (*shizuka*, quiet), 賑やか (*nigiyaka*, lively), 丁寧 (*teinei*, polite) and 親切 (*shinsetsu*, kind).

Don't be fooled by きれい and 嫌い: they end in the sound *i*, but they are na-adjectives. The negative is きれいじゃない, not きれくない.

## The Copula: です and だ

The **copula** is the word that links a noun to what it is, the closest Japanese gets to "to be". Its polite form is です and its casual form is だ.

| Style | Present | Negative | Past | Past negative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Polite | です | じゃないです / ではありません | でした | じゃなかったです / ではありませんでした |
| Casual | だ | じゃない | だった | じゃなかった |

> 学生です。\
> *Gakusei desu.*\
> I am a student. (polite)
>
> 学生だ。\
> *Gakusei da.*\
> I'm a student. (casual)
>
> 学生じゃない。\
> *Gakusei ja nai.*\
> I'm not a student. (casual)

Notice that the subject has vanished. Japanese drops anything the listener can work out from context, including "I" and "you", so 学生です on its own is a complete, natural sentence.

## Asking Questions: Add か

English rearranges words to ask a question ("You are going" becomes "Are you going?"). Japanese keeps the word order and adds か at the end.

> これは何ですか。\
> *Kore wa nan desu ka.*\
> What is this?
>
> どこに行きますか。\
> *Doko ni ikimasu ka.*\
> Where are you going?
>
> 田中さんは来ましたか。\
> *Tanaka-san wa kimashita ka.*\
> Did Tanaka come?

In casual speech, か usually drops out and a rising intonation does the work:

> 行く？\
> *Iku?*\
> Are you going?

### Question Words

| Japanese | Romaji | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 何 (なに / なん) | *nani* / *nan* | what |
| どこ | *doko* | where |
| いつ | *itsu* | when |
| 誰 (だれ) / どなた | *dare* / *donata* | who (plain / polite) |
| なぜ / どうして / なんで | *naze* / *dōshite* / *nande* | why (formal / standard / casual) |
| どう / いかが | *dō* / *ikaga* | how (plain / polite) |
| どれ | *dore* | which (of three or more) |
| どちら / どっち | *dochira* / *docchi* | which (of two; polite / casual) |
| いくら | *ikura* | how much (price) |
| いくつ | *ikutsu* | how many; how old |

## Counters: Counting Things the Japanese Way

English counts most things directly ("three cats") and only sometimes needs a unit ("three sheets of paper"). Japanese always uses a **counter** (助数詞, *josūshi*), a suffix attached to the number that depends on the shape or kind of thing being counted. The usual order is noun, then number plus counter.

| Counter | For | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 個 (*ko*) | small objects | りんご3個 (*ringo sanko*, three apples) |
| 枚 (*mai*) | flat things | 紙3枚 (*kami sanmai*, three sheets of paper) |
| 本 (*hon*) | long, thin things | ペン1本 (*pen ippon*, one pen); ビール2本 (*bīru nihon*, two bottles of beer) |
| 冊 (*satsu*) | bound books | 本5冊 (*hon gosatsu*, five books) |
| 台 (*dai*) | machines, vehicles | 車2台 (*kuruma nidai*, two cars) |
| 匹 (*hiki*) | small animals | 猫3匹 (*neko sanbiki*, three cats) |
| 頭 (*tō*) | large animals | 牛2頭 (*ushi nitō*, two cows) |
| 羽 (*wa*) | birds (traditionally also rabbits) | 鳥5羽 (*tori gowa*, five birds) |
| 人 (*nin*) | people | 3人 (*sannin*, three people); one person is 一人 (*hitori*) and two is 二人 (*futari*) |
| 杯 (*hai*) | cups, glasses, bowls | コーヒー1杯 (*kōhī ippai*, one cup of coffee) |

Notice the sound changes: 本 becomes *ippon* and *sanbon*, and 匹 becomes *ippiki* and *sanbiki*. Learn each counter with its numbers one to 10 as a set, and you will absorb the changes as you go.

## Sentence Patterns by Level

The patterns below are worth recognizing now and using as you progress. The beginner ones are enough to get through a day in Japan.

### Beginner

- A は B です — A is B.
- A が好きです — I like A.
- A に行きます — I'm going to A.
- A をください — Please give me A.

### Intermediate

- A てから B — after doing A, do B.
- A ので B — because of A, B (a reason).
- A たり B たりする — do things like A and B.
- A ようになった — it has come about that A (a change over time).

### Advanced

- A に違いない — it must certainly be A.
- A わけにはいかない — I can't possibly do A (given the circumstances).
- A かねない — there's a risk that A could happen.
- A にもかかわらず — despite A.

> 日本に来てから、納豆が好きになりました。\
> *Nihon ni kite kara, nattō ga suki ni narimashita.*\
> Since coming to Japan, I've come to like natto.

## Common Mistakes and How to Fix Them

Every learner makes these. Seeing them side by side is the fastest way to stop.

| Mistake | Wrong | Right | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| の between an i-adjective and a noun | 大きいの犬 | 大きい犬 | I-adjectives attach to nouns directly |
| Verb not at the end | 私は飲む水を | 私は水を飲む | The verb comes last |
| は with a question word | 誰は来た？ | 誰が来た？ | Question words take が |
| Past tense on です after an i-adjective | 美しいでした | 美しかったです | The i-adjective carries the tense; です stays as it is |
| ある for people and animals | 私は弟があります | 私は弟がいます | ある is for things; いる is for living beings |

Grammar is a tool, not a cage. Treat these tables as a map, then go and read and listen to as much Japanese as you can: the patterns stick far better when you meet them in the wild than when you memorize them in isolation.

## Summary

- Japanese uses subject–object–verb order: the verb comes at the end of the sentence, and the pieces before it can move because particles mark their roles.
- Particles follow the word they mark: は marks the topic, が the subject, を the object, に a destination or point in time and で the place of an action or the means.
- Verbs fall into three groups: godan (u-verbs), ichidan (ru-verbs, which drop る) and the two irregulars する and 来る; the group decides how every form is built.
- The te-form links actions and builds requests (〜てください), ongoing actions (〜ています) and many other patterns; its godan endings follow the って / んで / いて / いで / して pattern, with 行く as the exception.
- I-adjectives conjugate themselves (大きくない, 大きかった), while na-adjectives behave like nouns and use な before a noun and the copula です or だ as a predicate.
- Questions keep the same word order and add か; counting uses a counter chosen by the shape or kind of the thing counted.
