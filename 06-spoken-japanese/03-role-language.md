# Role Language — Every Character Wears a Costume

No real Tokyo grandfather says 「わしが知っておるんじゃ」. No real heiress ends every sentence with 「ですわ」. Yet the moment you read those lines, you can picture exactly who is speaking: a white-bearded professor, a young lady with a fan and a ringing laugh. Japanese fiction does this all the time, and it does it with grammar.

**Role language** (役割語, *yakuwarigo*) is a stylized way of speaking that signals a character's type (age, gender, class, era, region or personality) from a single line. The linguist 金水敏 (Kinsui Satoshi) coined the term in his 2003 book 『ヴァーチャル日本語 役割語の謎』 (*Virtual Japanese: The Mystery of Role Language*). His key observation was that these speech styles feel instantly real to Japanese readers even though almost nobody actually talks that way.

Role language matters to you for two reasons. First, it unlocks fiction: once you can read it, characters in [anime](../12-japanese-for-media/01-anime.md) and [manga](../12-japanese-for-media/02-manga.md) tell you who they are before the plot does. Second, it tells you what not to copy. Role language is a convention for the audience, not a transcript of real speech, and speaking it in real life ranges from charmingly odd to genuinely strange.

> **Warning:** Recognize role language for comprehension; don't adopt it as your own Japanese. A learner who has picked up 俺様 and 〜ぜ from a favorite anime sounds like a costume, not a person.

## First-Person Pronouns: Who Says "I"

English has one word for "I". Japanese has dozens, and choosing one is like choosing a costume. This table alone explains a great deal of character speech.

| Pronoun | Reading | Who uses it and what it signals |
|---|---|---|
| 私 | watashi | Neutral and polite for everyone; for men it sounds formal or businesslike |
| 私 | watakushi | More formal and refined: speeches, butlers, heiresses |
| 僕 | boku | Boys and gentle or intellectual men; polite, the "nice guy" |
| 俺 | ore | Casual and masculine; tough guys, and ordinary men among friends |
| 俺様 | ore-sama | "The great me": arrogant princes and villains |
| あたし | atashi | Casual feminine; real women use it too |
| あたい | atai | A rough, working-class or tomboyish girl with old downtown flavor; dated |
| うち | uchi | A girl's casual "I", Kansai-flavored and now widespread among young women in the west |
| わし | washi | Elderly men and bosses; also everyday "I" for men in parts of western Japan |
| 拙者 | sessha | Samurai and ninja ("this humble one"); pairs with 〜でござる |
| 吾輩 / 我輩 | wagahai | A pompous, self-important man; made famous by Natsume Sōseki's 1905 novel 『吾輩は猫である』 (*I Am a Cat*) |
| おいら / おら | oira / ora | A rustic, easygoing country boy |
| 朕 | chin | The emperor, in formal edicts; Emperor Hirohito's 1945 surrender rescript uses it |
| 麻呂 | maro | A Heian court noble; the aristocrat caricature |

## Second-Person Pronouns: Who Says "You" (and How Rudely)

Japanese "you" words have a habit of decaying from polite to rude over the centuries. Both 貴様 and お前 began as respectful forms. That is one reason most Japanese speakers avoid "you" words altogether and use names and titles instead.

| Pronoun | Reading | Politeness | Who uses it |
|---|---|---|---|
| あなた | anata | Polite but distant | Strangers in writing; also a wife to her husband; not safe with superiors |
| 君 | kimi | Mild, slightly downward | A superior to a junior, among close friends, in song lyrics |
| お前 | omae | Rough, downward | Close male friends, or contempt; originally the honorific 御前 |
| てめえ | temē | Very rude, aggressive | Picking a fight; from 手前 |
| 貴様 | kisama | Very rude, hostile | Spat at enemies; once a respectful written form |
| 汝 | nanji | Archaic, literary | "Thou" in scripture and fantasy |
| おぬし | onushi | Archaic | Samurai-era speech to equals or inferiors |
| お宅 | otaku | Polite but stiff | Someone whose name you don't know; fans' use of it gave the subculture its name |

## Sentence Endings: The Character Tags

The way a sentence ends is often the loudest signal of all. Manga writers call these character endings キャラ語尾 (*kyara gobi*), and they are how a translator's "ye olde" or "cutesy" flavor is built in the original.

| Ending | Reading | Archetype | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 〜じゃ / 〜のじゃ | -ja / -no ja | Professor, elderly sage | わしが博士**じゃ** |
| 〜ぜ / 〜ぞ | -ze / -zo | Rough, cool male | 行く**ぜ** / やる**ぞ** |
| 〜わ / 〜のよ / 〜かしら | -wa / -no yo / -kashira | Traditional "women's language" | そうな**のよ** / どう**かしら** |
| 〜ですわ / 〜ですの | -desu wa / -desu no | Rich young lady (お嬢様) | 楽しみ**ですわ** |
| 〜でござる | -de gozaru | Samurai, ninja | 拙者が参る**でござる** |
| 〜ニャ / 〜にゃん | -nya / -nyan | Cat characters | お腹すいた**ニャ** |
| 〜だっちゃ | -daccha | Lum in *Urusei Yatsura*, borrowed from a Tohoku dialect | 好き**だっちゃ** |
| 〜なのだ / 〜のだ | -na no da / -no da | Quirky or childlike; Bakabon's Papa in *Tensai Bakabon* | これでいい**のだ** |
| 〜っす | -ssu | Casual-polite junior; real speech among young men in sports clubs | お疲れ**っす** |
| 〜なり | -nari | Classical copula; robots and mascots | 我は機械**なり** |
| 〜アル | -aru | A stereotyped "Chinese" character (see the warning below) | 私やる**ある**よ |

〜っす is the odd one out: it is a real, living form, a cheerful contraction of です used by younger speakers to seniors they are close to.

## The Archetypes at a Glance

Put a pronoun, an ending and a few signature phrases together and you get a recognizable kit.

| Archetype | "I" | Endings | Other tells |
|---|---|---|---|
| 博士 (professor, sage) | わし | 〜じゃ / 〜おる | 知っておる, a slow grave tone |
| お嬢様 (rich young lady) | わたくし | 〜ですわ / 〜ですの | ごきげんよう, a ringing "ohohoho" laugh |
| 武士 (samurai) | 拙者 | 〜でござる | 〜申す, かたじけない (much obliged), archaic verbs |
| 田舎者 (country bumpkin) | おら / おいら | 〜だべ / 〜ずら / 〜だっぺ | A composite rural speech; no single real region talks exactly like this |
| 関西人 (Kansai character) | うち / わて | 〜や / 〜やで / 〜やん / 〜へん | ほんま, あかん, なんでやねん; strongly linked to comedy |
| Boy and girl | 僕・俺 / あたし | 〜だ・〜ぜ / 〜わ・〜かしら | Boys assertive; girls softer, with more sentence-final particles |
| オネエ言葉 (*onē kotoba*) | あたし | 〜のよ / 〜じゃない | Exaggerated feminine speech in a male voice; campy and performed |

The country-bumpkin kit is a good example of how role language works. Its endings come from real dialects (〜べ from Tohoku and Kanto, 〜ずら from Shizuoka and Yamanashi), but no real community mixes them. The mixture means only "from the countryside", as seen from the city.

## Stereotypes Worth Flagging

Role language is built from stereotypes, and some have aged badly. Knowing them is part of reading Japanese media critically: treat these as history, not as models.

- *The 〜アル voice.* Kinsui traces this pidgin-like speech to the contact Japanese of Yokohama and other treaty ports in the late 1800s; it later spread in the colonial era, including Manchukuo. It is now widely recognized as an ethnic caricature. Understand it when you meet it in older media; never imitate it.
- *Women's language* (女言葉, *onna kotoba*). Endings such as 〜わ, 〜かしら and 〜のよ are far more common in fiction and translated subtitles than in the speech of most Japanese women today. Learners who copy them from anime often think they are learning standard "female Japanese". They aren't.
- *オネエ言葉.* This is a performed, exaggerated register associated with certain TV personalities. It is not an accurate picture of how gay or transgender people in Japan actually speak.

> **Note:** Kinsui also observed that heavy role language tends to belong to supporting characters (脇役, *wakiyaku*). Protagonists usually speak something close to the standard, so that readers can identify with them; the sidekick, the mentor and the comic relief get the costumes.

## Furigana Wordplay

Manga has a second trick for character and emotion: making the small reading printed beside the kanji, the furigana, say something different from the kanji themselves. The kanji carry the written meaning; the reading carries what is spoken or felt. You read both at once.

| Written | Furigana | Literal meaning | The effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| 本気 | マジ | seriousness | Formal kanji with a slangy spoken voice, at the same time |
| 機械鎧 | オートメイル | machine armor | An English ruby names the prosthetic limb (*Fullmetal Alchemist*) |
| 負けて死ね | バイツァ・ダスト | lose and die | The kanji give the lethal command; the ruby gives the attack's name, "Bites the Dust" (*JoJo's Bizarre Adventure*) |
| 親友 | ライバル | close friend | The contrast compresses a whole relationship into one word |
| 永遠 | とわ | eternity | A softer, more poetic reading than えいえん |

This is the creative extreme of **gikun** (義訓), readings assigned by meaning rather than by the kanji's usual sounds. The dictionary has fixed examples you already use: 大人 is read おとな and 梅雨 is read つゆ. [Section 12.2](../12-japanese-for-media/02-manga.md) covers more manga conventions, including sound effects and the famous sound of silence, シーン.

## Summary

- Role language (役割語) is a set of stylized speech patterns that tell the reader a character's type instantly; it is a fictional convention, not real speech.
- First-person pronouns are the biggest signal: 僕, 俺, わし, 拙者 and わたくし each conjure a different kind of character.
- Japanese "you" words drift from polite to rude over time (お前 and 貴様 began as honorifics), so real speakers usually use names instead.
- Sentence endings such as 〜じゃ, 〜でござる, 〜ですわ and 〜ぜ mark archetypes, and only a few (such as 〜っす) are common in real conversation.
- Some role language, such as the 〜アル voice, is an ethnic caricature, and "women's language" is far rarer in real life than on screen.
- Furigana can give a second meaning alongside the kanji, as when 本気 is read マジ.
