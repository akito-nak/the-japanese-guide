# Spoken Versus Written Japanese — Two Different Animals

You have studied hard. You can conjugate 行きます in your sleep. Then a friend leans over and says 「明日学校行く？」, and half of what you expected is missing: no に, no ます, no か. A minute later someone says 「やっぱ分かんないわ」 and you wonder whether you learned the wrong language.

You didn't. Japanese has an unusually wide gap between the way it is spoken and the way it is written. Textbooks teach polished, grammatically complete sentences. Real conversation drops particles, contracts verbs, swallows sounds and runs words together, while newspapers and official documents go the other way, into a stiff, compact style you will almost never hear aloud. This section is your decoder ring for both ends.

## The Register Spectrum

**Register** is the variety of language a speaker chooses for a situation: who they are talking to, where and through which medium. Japanese registers sit on a sliding scale, and the politeness levels you met in [chapter 5](../05-politeness/README.md) are only part of it. The table runs from most casual to most formal.

| Register | Reading | Feel | Typical setting |
|---|---|---|---|
| くだけた言葉 | kudaketa kotoba | Very casual, contracted | Close friends, family |
| 普通体 | futsūtai | Plain, neutral | Peers, thinking aloud, diaries |
| 丁寧語 | teineigo | Polite (です・ます) | Strangers, shops, colleagues |
| 敬語 | keigo | Honorific and humble | Business, customers, ceremonies |
| 書き言葉 | kakikotoba | Written style | News, documents, academic writing |

Most learners spend nearly all their time in the middle row, 丁寧語 (*teineigo*, polite language), and arrive unprepared for both ends: the casual end, which is most real conversation, and the written end, which is most real text. **書き言葉** (*kakikotoba*, written language) and **話し言葉** (*hanashikotoba*, spoken language) are the Japanese names for the two poles of this section.

## What Disappears in Casual Speech

### Particles and Question Markers

Compare the textbook question with what you will actually hear:

> 明日、学校に行きますか？\
> *Ashita, gakkō ni ikimasu ka?*\
> Are you going to school tomorrow?

> 明日、学校行く？\
> *Ashita, gakkō iku?*\
> Going to school tomorrow?

Three things vanished. The direction particle に dropped, because the meaning is obvious. The polite ending ます became the plain form 行く. And the question particle か disappeared, replaced by a rising intonation. In casual speech, は, が, を and に are dropped whenever context makes them clear; か at the end of a plain question can even sound rough or accusing, so rising intonation does the job instead.

### Contracted Verb Endings

Verb endings shrink in fast speech, and these contractions are so regular that you can learn them as a set. The bold part is what changes.

| Full form | Casual contraction | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 〜ている | 〜**てる** | ongoing action, "doing" |
| 〜ておく | 〜**とく** | do in advance |
| 〜てしまう | 〜**ちゃう** / 〜**じゃう** | do completely, or by accident ("oops") |
| 〜ていない | 〜**てない** | not doing, haven't done |
| 〜てはいけない | 〜**ちゃいけない** | must not |
| 〜なければならない | 〜**なきゃ** / 〜**なくちゃ** | must, have to |
| 〜てあげる | 〜**たげる** | do as a favor |
| 〜ては | 〜**ちゃ** | if (you) do… |

Here are three of them in action:

> 今、ご飯食べ**てる**。\
> *Ima, gohan tabeteru.*\
> I'm eating right now.

> 財布忘れ**ちゃった**！\
> *Saifu wasurechatta!*\
> I left my wallet behind! (and I'm not happy about it)

> もう行か**なきゃ**。\
> *Mō ikanakya.*\
> I've got to go.

> **Tip:** Practice the contraction rules in both directions. When you hear 〜ちゃった, rebuild 〜てしまった in your head; when you speak to friends, go the other way. Within a few weeks the short forms stop sounding like a different language.

### Dropped Copulas and Shortened Set Phrases

In casual speech the copula だ often disappears entirely, especially in questions, and polite set phrases lose their tails.

| Polite | Casual | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| そうです | そう / そうだね | That's right |
| 分かりません | 分からない / 分かんない | I don't know |
| ありがとうございます | ありがとう | Thanks |
| 大丈夫ですか？ | 大丈夫？ | Are you okay? |
| 本当ですか？ | 本当？ / ほんと？ | Really? |

Note that 大丈夫だ？ with だ would sound odd; in a plain-form question, the copula drops and intonation carries the question.

### Swallowed Sounds

Sounds also change. The most common shifts are predictable once you have seen them a few times.

| Full pronunciation | Casual | What changed |
|---|---|---|
| わからない | わか**ん**ない | ら becomes ん before な |
| つまらない | つま**ん**ない | same shift |
| 〜ている | 〜てる | the い drops |
| という | って / つー | contraction |
| そういう | そーゆー | vowel blending |
| やはり | やっぱり / やっぱ | an emphatic doubled consonant, then a clipped ending |
| ところ | とこ | final syllable dropped |

> **Note:** Not every casual swap is a contraction. なんで for どうして ("why") is a different word that happens to be more casual, not a worn-down version of the same word.

## Patterns You Hear Everywhere but Rarely Study

These sentence patterns appear constantly in speech and only briefly in textbooks.

| Pattern | What it does |
|---|---|
| 〜じゃん | "…right?", "…you know?" (assertive, seeks agreement) |
| 〜じゃないですか | "As you know…" (presents something as shared knowledge) |
| 〜かな | "I wonder…" (thinking aloud) |
| 〜けど… / 〜けどさ | "but…", a softener that lets a sentence trail off politely |
| 〜んだ / 〜んです | "the thing is…" (explaining or asking for an explanation) |
| 〜ってば！ | "I said…!" (exasperated repetition) |
| 〜って | quotation marker for "they say" or "you said" |
| ね〜 / よね〜 | stretched for warmth or emphasis |
| 全然大丈夫！ | "totally fine!" with 全然 before a positive word |

The last row trips learners up because many textbooks teach that 全然 (*zenzen*, at all) must take a negative. In casual speech, 全然 with a positive word is everywhere, and it has a longer history than the textbook rule suggests; [section 7.2](../07-living-language/02-contemporary-japanese.md) tells that story.

## Gendered Speech Patterns

Japanese has traditionally had speech styles associated with women and with men, mostly at the end of the sentence. They are still alive in fiction and among some speakers, but in everyday conversation they have faded, especially among younger and urban speakers.

Endings traditionally associated with women include:

- わ with rising intonation: 行くわ (I'm going)
- の with falling intonation as a soft statement: 行くの (I'm going, you see)
- かしら, "I wonder": 何かしら (I wonder what it is)
- よ directly after a noun or adjective: きれいよ (it's lovely)

Endings and words traditionally associated with men include:

- ぞ and ぜ: 行くぞ！ (Let's go!), やるぜ (I'll do it)
- だ and だよ where others might use a softer form
- 俺 (*ore*) or 僕 (*boku*) for "I"
- More clipped forms such as うまい rather than おいしい for "tasty"

As a learner, use neutral forms: plain or polite verbs, ね and よ, and 私 or 僕 for "I". Heavy gendered speech copied from anime can make you sound like a character rather than a person. [Section 6.3](03-role-language.md) explains exactly why.

## Written Japanese: A Different World

Newspapers, official documents, academic writing and much literature use features that you almost never hear in conversation.

### The Written Copula and Formal Connectors

Written Japanese replaces だ and です with である, and uses compact connectors that would sound stiff aloud.

| Written form | Example | Spoken equivalent | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 〜である | 問題である | 問題だ / 問題です | is (written copula) |
| 〜であった | 原因であった | 原因だった | was |
| 〜において | 会議において | 会議で | at, in (the context of) |
| 〜に関して | この問題に関して | この問題について | regarding |
| 〜とともに | 時代とともに | 時代と一緒に | together with, as … does |
| 〜ず(に) | 何も言わず | 何も言わないで | without doing |

Written style also prefers certain verbs: 述べる (*noberu*, to state) where speech uses 言う, and 記す (*shirusu*, to record) where speech uses 書く.

The style built on である is called **である体** (*de aru tai*, the plain written style). It is the voice of newspaper editorials, academic papers and many novels.

### The Passive in the News

News Japanese loves the passive voice, because it puts the event first and keeps the reporter out of the sentence.

> 首相は記者会見を開いた。\
> *Shushō wa kisha kaiken o hiraita.*\
> The prime minister held a press conference.

> 記者会見が開か**れた**。\
> *Kisha kaiken ga hirakareta.*\
> A press conference was held.

### Nominalization

Written Japanese frequently turns a whole clause into a noun with こと or の. Speech does this too, but writing piles them up.

> 彼が来る**こと**は知っている。\
> *Kare ga kuru koto wa shitte iru.*\
> I know that he is coming.

> 泳ぐ**の**が好きだ。\
> *Oyogu no ga suki da.*\
> I like swimming.

### Classical Remnants

Classical grammar survives in signs, formal speeches, proverbs and literature. [Section 7.3](../07-living-language/03-language-evolution.md) explains where these forms come from.

| Classical form | Meaning | Modern equivalent |
|---|---|---|
| 〜ず | not, without | 〜ない / 〜ないで |
| 〜べし / 〜べき | should, must | 〜するべきだ |
| 〜べからず | must not (on signs) | 〜してはいけない |
| 〜なり | is | 〜だ / 〜である |
| 〜ぬ | not (in set phrases) | 〜ない |

You will see 立入るべからず (*tachiiru bekarazu*, no entry) on old signs and hear 知らぬが仏 (*shiranu ga hotoke*, ignorance is bliss) in conversation.

## Pitch Accent: How Spoken Japanese Sounds

Speech carries one more layer that writing hides. Standard Japanese has **pitch accent** (高低アクセント, *kōtei akusento*): instead of stressing a syllable as English does, it gives each word a pattern of high (H) and low (L) pitch. Kana spell 箸 (chopsticks, HL) and 橋 (bridge, LH) identically as はし, and 雨 (rain, HL) and 飴 (candy, LH) both as あめ; only the melody tells them apart. [Section 2.4](../02-sounds-of-japanese/04-pitch-accent-and-intonation.md) gives pitch accent its full treatment, including how to train your ear for it.

Two points matter for spoken registers. First, the patterns belong to 標準語 and differ from region to region, which is one reason a Kansai speaker sounds different even when using standard words. Second, casual speech leans on intonation to do grammatical work: once か is dropped, a rising tone is all that turns 行く (*iku*, I'm going) into 行く？ (*iku?*, are you going?).

## Regional Speech

Everything in this section describes 標準語 (*hyōjungo*, standard Japanese), the Tokyo-based variety of textbooks and national news. Japan's regional dialects add another dimension to spoken Japanese, from the Kansai speech of Osaka comedians to the dialects of Tohoku that even other Japanese speakers find hard to follow. They get a section of their own: [section 6.2](02-dialects.md).

## The Same Request in Five Registers

Here is one request, "please wait", across the whole spectrum.

| Register | Japanese | Where you'd hear it |
|---|---|---|
| Casual | ちょっと待って！ | Friends |
| Polite | 少し待ってください。 | A colleague, a stranger |
| Service polite | 少々お待ちください。 | Shops, restaurants, phone lines |
| Formal request | しばらくお待ちいただけますでしょうか。 | Business email, formal requests |
| Written, official | お待ちくださいますようお願い申し上げます。 | Official notices and announcements |

The core meaning never changes. The register shifts through vocabulary (ちょっと, 少し, 少々), verb form and sheer length: the more formal the setting, the longer the sentence.

## Closing the Gap

Each end of the spectrum needs its own kind of input.

- To understand spoken Japanese, listen to unscripted Japanese: variety shows, podcasts, streams and dramas. Your ear will start to rebuild the contractions automatically.
- To speak naturally, copy people of your own age and situation, and get comfortable being slightly wrong with a language partner.
- To read written Japanese, read widely, moving from simplified news such as NHK News Web Easy to regular news and then to essays and fiction.

The gap narrows with every hour of real Japanese you take in, from both sides at once.

## Summary

- Japanese registers run from very casual speech through plain, polite and honorific forms to a distinct written style, and real conversation and real text sit at opposite ends.
- Casual speech drops particles, the copula だ and the question particle か, and uses intonation to carry questions.
- Contractions are regular: 〜ている becomes 〜てる, 〜てしまう becomes 〜ちゃう, 〜なければ becomes 〜なきゃ, and ら or る before な often becomes ん (分かんない).
- Written Japanese uses である instead of だ, formal connectors such as において and に関して, frequent passives and classical remnants such as 〜ず and 〜べからず.
- Standard Japanese has pitch accent: words such as 箸 and 橋 differ in their pattern of high and low pitch rather than in stress, and casual speech uses rising intonation to mark questions; section 2.4 covers both in full.
- Gendered sentence endings survive mainly in fiction and among some speakers; a learner sounds most natural with neutral forms.
