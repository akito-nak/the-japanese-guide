# Dialects — One Language, Many Voices

You order takoyaki at a stall in Osaka, and the vendor grins: 「おおきに！ほんまにうまいで！」 You recognize every sound, and yet nothing quite matches your textbook. Welcome to 方言 (*hōgen*, dialect).

Every Japanese child learns standard Japanese at school, and every Japanese speaker understands it. But at home, with friends and in daily life across the country, regional speech is alive and loved. Some dialects differ from the standard only in a few endings and an accent; others are hard going even for native speakers from elsewhere. Learning to recognize the big ones turns a confusing encounter into a delightful one.

## Standard Japanese and Dialect

**Hyōjungo** (標準語, standard Japanese) is the variety based on educated Tokyo speech. It is the language of national news, textbooks, government and this book. Linguists often prefer the term **kyōtsūgo** (共通語, common language), meaning the shared variety that speakers from different regions use with each other; in everyday talk the two terms overlap.

**Hōgen** (方言, dialect) means a regional variety, with its own pronunciation, vocabulary, grammar and pitch accent. Japanese people often call a dialect by its region plus 弁 (*ben*, speech): 関西弁 (*kansai-ben*), 博多弁 (*hakata-ben*) and so on.

The distance can be large. A speaker of deep rural Tohoku dialect and a speaker of broad Kagoshima dialect, each speaking naturally, would struggle to understand each other. In practice, almost everyone switches: dialect with family and friends, standard Japanese with strangers, at work or on television.

> **Warning:** Never mock someone's dialect. Regional speech is a source of pride and identity, and many people from the countryside remember being made to feel self-conscious about it in Tokyo. Genuine curiosity, on the other hand, is almost always welcome.

## Kansai-ben: The Famous One

Kansai dialect, spoken in and around Osaka, Kyoto, Kobe and Nara, is the dialect learners meet first. Comedians, anime sidekicks and TV personalities have carried it into every living room in Japan, and it sounds warmer, more rhythmic and frankly more fun than the Tokyo standard. Kansai also has its own pitch accent, so even standard sentences sound different in a Kansai mouth.

### Grammar

| Standard | Kansai-ben | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| だ | **や** | is (copula): そう**や** = そうだ |
| 〜じゃない？ / 〜じゃん | 〜**やん** | …right? |
| 違う | **ちゃう** | that's wrong, different |
| 〜ない (しない) | 〜**へん** (せえへん) | not (doesn't do) |
| 〜なかった (しなかった) | 〜**へんかった** (せえへんかった) | didn't |
| 〜ている | 〜**てる** / 〜**とる** | ongoing action |
| 〜からね | 〜**さかい** | because (older speakers) |
| 行かれる (honorific) | 行か**はる** | a light honorific: 先生が来**はる** (the teacher is coming) |
| 〜しよう | 〜**しよ** / 〜**しよか** | let's |

The 〜はる honorific deserves attention: it lets Kansai speakers show light respect without the stiffness of standard 敬語 (*keigo*, honorific language).

### Vocabulary

| Standard | Kansai-ben | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| とても | **めっちゃ** | very (now heard nationwide) |
| 本当に | **ほんまに** (*honma ni*) | really |
| ダメ | **あかん** (*akan*) | no good, can't |
| いい | **ええ** (*ē*) | good |
| 面白い | **おもろい** (*omoroi*) | funny, interesting |
| 疲れた | **しんどい** (*shindoi*) | tiring, exhausting |
| 捨てる | **ほかす** (*hokasu*) | to throw away |
| ありがとう | **おおきに** (*ōkini*) | thank you (traditional, heard from shopkeepers and older speakers) |
| どうして | **なんで** | why (also common in the standard) |

The most famous Kansai line of all is the comedian's retort なんでやねん (*nande yanen*, "why on earth…?!", or "give me a break!").

### Osaka and Kyoto

Both cities speak Kansai-ben, with different personalities. Osaka speech has a reputation for being loud, direct and funny, and Osakans themselves enjoy the stereotype that everyone there is a comedian. Kyoto speech, shaped by more than a thousand years as the imperial capital, is softer and more indirect. Kyoto uses 〜はる even more widely, and some of its negatives differ: "won't come" is きいひん in Kyoto but けえへん in Osaka.

## Hakata-ben: Fukuoka

Fukuoka's dialect, part of the wider Kyushu family, is famous for its charm. Its signatures are the ending と in place of の, and adjectives ending in か instead of い.

| Standard | Hakata-ben | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 何してるの？ | 何**しよ**っと？ | What are you doing? |
| 行くの？ | 行く**と**？ | Are you going? |
| 来ない | 来**ん** (こん) | isn't coming |
| すごい | すご**か** | amazing |
| おいしい | うま**か** | delicious |
| いい | **よか** | good, fine |
| 〜だよ | 〜**ばい** / 〜**たい** | emphatic statement |
| 〜だから | 〜**けん** | because |

> 終電、来とる**ばい**。\
> *Shūden, kitoru bai.*\
> The last train's here, you know.

The line 「好いとうよ」 (*suitō yo*, "I love you") is famous across Japan as a sweet piece of Hakata dialect.

## Tohoku-ben: The Northeast

The dialects of the six Tohoku prefectures in northern Honshu are often described as the hardest for other Japanese speakers to follow. A well-known feature is the merging of sounds that the standard keeps apart: し and す, and ち and つ, can sound alike, which gave rise to the nickname ズーズー弁 (*zūzū-ben*). The vowels い and え can also merge.

| Standard | Tohoku (various areas) | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 〜だろう / 〜しよう | 〜**べ** / 〜**だべ** | probably; let's |
| かわいい | **めんこい** | cute |
| 冷たい | **しゃっこい** | cold to the touch |
| とても寒い | **しばれる** | freezing (also Hokkaido) |
| 私 / あなた | **わ** / **な** | I / you (Aomori's Tsugaru dialect) |

The Tsugaru dialect of Aomori produced Japan's favorite example of dialect economy: どさ？ ゆさ。 (*Dosa? Yusa.*), meaning "Where are you off to?" "To the bath." Two syllables each, a whole conversation.

Tohoku dialect reached a national audience in 2013 through the NHK morning drama あまちゃん (*Amachan*), set in coastal Iwate. Its surprised exclamation じぇじぇじぇ (*jejeje*) was one of the joint winners of that year's buzzword-of-the-year award.

## Nagoya-ben: Central Japan

Nagoya dialect is known for turning the vowel combination "ai" into a drawn-out "yā" sound and for its distinctive endings.

| Standard | Nagoya-ben | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| うまい | **うみゃあ** | tasty |
| エビフライ | **えびふりゃあ** | fried shrimp (the city's affectionate in-joke) |
| とても | **どえりゃあ** | very |
| 疲れた | **えらい** | tired, exhausting |
| 久しぶり | **やっとかめ** | long time no see |
| 〜だよ / 〜だよね | 〜**だがや** / 〜**だがね** | emphatic or agreement-seeking ending |
| 自転車 | **ケッタ** | bicycle |

> **Note:** えらい is a trap word. In standard Japanese it means "great" or "admirable", in Nagoya and much of central Japan it means "tiring", and in Kansai it can mean "very" (えらい暑い, terribly hot). The same word, three regions, three meanings.

## Hiroshima-ben

Hiroshima speech sounds forceful to outsiders, partly because the 1973 yakuza film series 仁義なき戦い (*Jingi naki tatakai*, *Battles Without Honor and Humanity*) made it famous. In everyday life it is warm and expressive, and its polite request forms are gentle.

| Standard | Hiroshima-ben | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| だ | **じゃ** | is |
| だろう | **じゃろう** | probably |
| 〜ない (行かない) | 〜**ん** (行かん) | not |
| 〜から | 〜**けえ** / 〜**けん** | because |
| 来なさい | **来んさい** (きんさい) | come (gently) |
| 見なさい | **見んさい** (みんさい) | look |
| 〜してください | 〜**しんさい** | please do |

## Okinawa: A Separate Language

The traditional speech of Okinawa, うちなーぐち (*Uchināguchi*, Okinawan), is not a dialect of Japanese at all. Linguists classify it as one of the **Ryukyuan languages**, a branch of the Japonic family that is related to Japanese but not mutually intelligible with it. UNESCO's 2009 atlas of endangered languages lists the Ryukyuan languages, along with Ainu, as endangered. Most Okinawans today speak Japanese, often a local variety with Okinawan words mixed in.

| Okinawan word | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| はいさい / はいたい | *haisai* / *haitai* | hello (used by men / by women) |
| めんそーれ | *mensōre* | welcome |
| にふぇーでーびる | *nifēdēbiru* | thank you |
| なんくるないさ | *nankurunaisa* | it'll work out, don't worry |
| おじぃ / おばぁ | *ojī* / *obā* | grandpa / grandma (local Japanese) |
| ちゃんぷるー | *chanpurū* | a stir-fry, literally "mixed together" |

チャンプルー has become a metaphor for Okinawan culture itself, which mixes Ryukyuan, Japanese, Chinese and American influences.

## Dialect in Media

Dialect is everywhere on Japanese screens.

- 吉本興業 (*Yoshimoto Kōgyō*), Japan's biggest comedy agency, was founded in Osaka, and many of the country's best-known comedians perform in Kansai-ben.
- じゃりン子チエ (*Jarinko Chie*), a long-running manga and anime, is set in a working-class Osaka neighborhood and is full of broad Kansai speech.
- ゴールデンカムイ (*Golden Kamuy*), set in Hokkaido after the Russo-Japanese War, mixes the Ainu language with characters from all over Japan speaking their home dialects.
- 龍馬伝 (*Ryōmaden*), the 2010 NHK historical drama, put the Tosa dialect of Kochi in the mouth of Sakamoto Ryōma.

## Dialect and Identity

Regional speakers have long felt a tension between pride and self-consciousness. The phrase 方言コンプレックス (*hōgen konpurekkusu*, dialect complex) describes the embarrassment some people feel about their dialect in Tokyo or on national media. In recent decades attitudes have warmed: many younger people are proud of their local speech, towns put dialect phrases on posters and souvenirs, and characters in dramas and games speak dialect as a sign of authenticity. As a visitor, a single local phrase used with a smile (おおきに in Osaka, めんそーれ in Okinawa) earns enormous goodwill.

## Should You Study Dialects?

Learn standard Japanese first. It is understood everywhere, and you will pick up dialect features naturally once your standard is solid. If you move to a particular region, learn a handful of local phrases and the main endings; people will be delighted.

The one exception is Kansai-ben. It is so common in comedy, anime and television that passive recognition pays off from early on. You don't need to speak it, but you should know that ちゃう means 違う, that あかん means ダメ and that や is the Kansai だ.

## Summary

- Standard Japanese (標準語) is based on Tokyo speech and is understood everywhere; regional dialects (方言) differ in pronunciation, vocabulary, grammar and pitch accent.
- Kansai-ben uses や for だ, 〜へん for the negative, 〜やん for "right?", the light honorific 〜はる and words such as めっちゃ, ほんま and あかん.
- Hakata-ben uses と for の (行くと？), か-adjectives (うまか) and the endings ばい and たい; Hiroshima and Hakata both use けん for "because".
- Tohoku dialects merge sounds such as し and す, Nagoya turns "ai" into "yā" (うみゃあ) and the word えらい means different things in different regions.
- Okinawan is a Ryukyuan language related to Japanese but not mutually intelligible with it, and it is listed as endangered.
- Learn the standard first, recognize Kansai-ben early and treat every dialect with curiosity, never mockery.
