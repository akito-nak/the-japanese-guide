# Long Vowels and Double Consonants

In English you can say "no" or "nooooo", and it is the same word with more feeling. In Japanese, holding a sound one beat longer gives you a different word. ここ (*koko*) means "here"; こうこう (*kōkō*) means "high school". きて (*kite*) means "come"; きって (*kitte*) is a postage stamp. Nothing else changes: same consonants, same vowels, just time. Of all the sound contrasts in Japanese, this is the one that matters most for being understood, and the one English-speaking ears are worst at hearing.

## Length Is Meaning

Japanese uses length in three ways, and each one adds a mora to the word:

- **A long vowel** holds the vowel for two beats: *ko-ko* against *ko-o-ko-o*.
- **A double consonant** holds the consonant for an extra, silent beat before releasing it: *ki-te* against *ki-t-te*.
- **A combined sound** squeezes two kana into one beat, which can make a word one mora shorter: *bi-yo-o-i-n* against *byo-o-i-n*.

Here are the pairs every learner meets sooner or later:

| Short | Romaji | Meaning | Long | Romaji | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| おばさん | obasan | aunt; middle-aged woman | おばあさん | obāsan | grandmother; elderly woman |
| おじさん | ojisan | uncle; middle-aged man | おじいさん | ojīsan | grandfather; elderly man |
| ここ | koko | here | こうこう | kōkō | high school |
| いえ | ie | house | いいえ | iie | no |
| しゅじん | shujin | husband | しゅうじん | shūjin | prisoner |
| ビル | biru | building | ビール | bīru | beer |
| きて | kite | come (please) | きって | kitte | postage stamp |
| さか | saka | slope | さっか | sakka | writer |
| おと | oto | sound | おっと | otto | husband |
| びよういん | biyōin | beauty salon | びょういん | byōin | hospital |

The last pair is worth a second look. *Biyōin* has five morae, *bi-yo-o-i-n*; *byōin* has four, *byo-o-i-n*, because *byo* is one beat. Both contain a long vowel; the difference is whether *bi* and *yo* are two beats or one.

In romaji, a macron marks a long vowel (*ō*, *ā*) and a doubled letter marks a double consonant (*tt*, *kk*, *ss*). How kana spell these, with an extra vowel, a small っ or the long-vowel dash ー, is the subject of [section 3.1](../03-writing-system/01-hiragana.md) and [section 3.2](../03-writing-system/02-katakana.md). Here the question is how they sound.

## Long Vowels

A long vowel is the same vowel held for twice the time, with no break and no change of quality in the middle. It is not two vowels said separately, and it is not louder or stressed; it is just longer. When Yukari Hirata (2004c) measured how Tokyo speakers pronounce pairs like these, long vowels came out about 2.2 times as long as short ones, which fits the two-beat count.

| Japanese | Romaji | Meaning | Beats |
|---|---|---|---|
| かあさん | kāsan | mom | ka-a-sa-n |
| ちいさい | chiisai | small | chi-i-sa-i |
| くうき | kūki | air | ku-u-ki |
| せんせい | sensei | teacher | se-n-se-e |
| とうきょう | Tōkyō | Tokyo | to-o-kyo-o |
| コーヒー | kōhī | coffee | ko-o-hi-i |

Notice せんせい. The kana spell *sensei*, but in ordinary speech the えい is simply a long *e*: *sensē*. The same happens in とけい (*tokei*, clock) and えいが (*eiga*, movie). Read slowly or sung, the *i* may come back; in conversation, a long *e* is what you will hear.

Long vowels are often where the English stress habit sneaks back in. An English speaker who hears a long vowel tends to hear stress, and then says the word with a heavy, stretched beat and squeezes the others: "TOE-kyo". Keep the rhythm even and let the length do the work: four equal beats, *to-o-kyo-o*.

## Double Consonants

A double consonant, written with a small っ in kana, is a held consonant. Japanese calls it 促音 (*sokuon*). For the stop consonants *k*, *t* and *p*, you get into position for the consonant, hold still in silence for a full beat, then release:

> きって\
> *kitte*\
> a stamp: *ki*, then the tongue in place for *t* and silence for one beat, then *te*

English does this too, but only where two words or word parts meet. Say "bookcase" naturally and there is a held *k* in the middle, "boo**k-c**ase". Say "night time" and the *t* is held across the join. That held closure is a Japanese double consonant; Japanese simply uses it inside words. Practice by saying "boo**kc**ase", then *ga**kk**ō* (school) with the same hold.

For the hissing sounds *s* and *sh*, the hiss itself lasts longer instead of a silence:

| Japanese | Romaji | Meaning | What you hold |
|---|---|---|---|
| ざっし | zasshi | magazine | the *sh* hiss |
| いっさい | issai | one year old | the *s* hiss |
| がっこう | gakkō | school | a silent *k* closure |
| ちょっと | chotto | a little | a silent *t* closure |
| いっぱい | ippai | full; a lot | a silent *p* closure, lips shut |
| ベッド | beddo | bed | a held *d*, in a loanword |

The hold is longer than most English speakers expect. Hirata and Whiton (2005) measured Tokyo speakers' closures for double stops at a little over three times the length of single ones. If your *kitte* sounds like *kite* with a slight hiccup, hold longer.

> **Tip:** A double consonant often carries an emotional punch in casual speech, which makes it easier to feel. やっぱり (*yappari*, just as I thought), すっごい (*suggoi*, a stretched すごい, amazing) and ちょっと (*chotto*) all hang on the hold. Say them with feeling and the timing comes naturally.

## Why English Ears Miss Length

English does have long and short sounds, but it never uses length alone to tell words apart. The vowel in "bead" is longer than in "beat" because of the consonant after it; stressed syllables are longer than unstressed ones; and everything stretches when we speak slowly or with feeling. So an English-trained ear has learned to treat length as a by-product of stress, emphasis or speed, and to ignore it when it comes to deciding which word it heard.

Speaking rate makes the problem harder. In fast speech, a long vowel can be shorter than a short vowel in slow speech. A Japanese listener doesn't judge length in milliseconds but relative to the speed of everything around it, and that skill is exactly what learners lack. The research on training this skill says a lot about how you should practice.

## What the Research Says About Training Length

The most extensive work on teaching English speakers to hear Japanese length comes from Yukari Hirata at Colgate University in New York and from a team led by Tajima at Hosei University in Tokyo.

- **Training works, and carries across contexts.** Hirata (2004a) trained American English speakers who knew no Japanese to identify the number of morae in Japanese words, with immediate feedback after each answer. One group trained on words said alone, the other on words inside sentences. Both groups improved compared with an untrained control group, and learning in one context carried over to the other, but the sentence-trained group was more consistent across the two. Real speech comes in sentences, so train with sentences.
- **Variety in speaking rate matters.** Hirata, Whitehurst and Cullings (2007) trained three groups to hear vowel length in sentences spoken slowly only, fast only, or at both rates. Training with both rates produced a robust improvement; slow-only training gave a marginal effect; fast-only training gave no significant gain. The study was designed to test high-variability training, and its result fits it: hearing the same contrast at different speeds teaches the ear what stays constant.
- **Narrow training has narrow effects.** Tajima, Kato, Rothwell, Akahane-Yamada and Munhall (2008) trained English listeners on isolated words contrasting vowel length, all at one normal speaking rate. Overall, the trained listeners did not improve more than an untrained control group. Training did improve perception of the trained contrast types and carried over to new speakers, but it did not help listeners cope with changes in speaking rate, and it did not carry over to untrained contrast types. Their conclusion was blunt: training of that kind helps "only to a limited extent".
- **Gestures add little on their own.** Teachers often have learners clap or tap each mora. Hirata, Kelly, Huang and Manansala (2014) trained 88 English speakers on vowel length with four kinds of hand-gesture training, watching or making gestures that marked syllables or morae. All four groups improved about equally, and the authors concluded that the effect of gestures on learning sounds is limited.

How strong is this evidence? It is a small body of laboratory studies, mostly with beginners or with people who had never studied Japanese, and they all point the same way: perceptual training with feedback improves English speakers' hearing of Japanese length, and the improvement is broadest when the training itself is varied, in sentences, at several speeds and across all the kinds of length contrast. It is consistent with the wider research on high-variability phonetic training described in [section 1.2](../01-how-to-learn-japanese/02-study-and-immersion.md).

For you, that adds up to four rules: practice with **sentences** as well as single words; use **many voices** and **several speeds**; train **all three** kinds of length (long vowels, double consonants and combined sounds); and get **immediate feedback**. Clapping is a fine way to count beats while you learn a word, a habit teachers recommend from experience, but it is no substitute for the listening practice itself.

## Drills

1. **Count, then say.** For each new word, count the morae on your fingers before you say it. Say it at an even pace, one beat per finger.
2. **Listen and choose.** Make flashcards in Anki ([section 1.3](../01-how-to-learn-japanese/03-apps-courses-and-tools.md)) for the minimal pairs in this section, with audio from several speakers on Forvo or YouGlish. Play the audio, decide which word you heard, then check. Ten minutes a day for two weeks is a sensible start, in line with the short, frequent sessions in the training studies.
3. **Put the words in a frame.** Say each pair inside a carrier sentence, slowly and then fast: これは（　）です (*Kore wa ___ desu*, "This is ___"). Record yourself and listen back: does *kitte* still have three beats at speed?
4. **Exaggerate, then relax.** Hold long vowels and double consonants too long at first, then bring them back toward normal. Overshooting is easier to fix than undershooting.
5. **Catch length in the wild.** When you watch Japanese video, listen for one length contrast per episode, such as every *ojisan* or *ojīsan*. Real speech, with its varied speeds and voices, is the best variability there is.

> **Warning:** Don't fix length in your mouth only. Learners who drill production without listening practice often learn to make long vowels carefully in isolated words and then lose them in conversation. The ear has to lead.

## Summary

- Length changes meaning in Japanese: *koko* and *kōkō*, *kite* and *kitte*, *biyōin* and *byōin* are different words, and each extra length adds a mora.
- A long vowel is the same vowel held for about twice as long, without stress; えい in words like せんせい is usually a long *e* in speech.
- A double consonant (small っ) is a held consonant: a full beat of silence before *k*, *t* or *p*, or a longer hiss for *s* and *sh*, like the middle of English "bookcase".
- English ears treat length as a side effect of stress and speed, so learners need ear training; in studies by Hirata (2004a), Hirata and colleagues (2007) and Tajima and colleagues (2008), training helped most when it used sentences and several speaking rates, and narrow training had narrow effects.
- Practice by counting beats, listening to minimal pairs with feedback, saying words in sentences at different speeds and recording yourself.
