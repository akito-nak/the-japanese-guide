# How Japanese Sounds Work

The most famous poem in Japanese is 17 beats long. Matsuo Bashō wrote it in 1686:

> 古池や　蛙飛び込む　水の音\
> *Furuike ya / kawazu tobikomu / mizu no oto*\
> The old pond. A frog jumps in. The sound of water.

A haiku has three lines of 5, 7 and 5. Count the syllables the English way and it doesn't work: *fu-ru-i-ke-ya* is fine, but what about the second line? Count the way a Japanese schoolchild does, clapping once for each beat, and everything fits: *ka-wa-zu-to-bi-ko-mu*, seven claps. The beat they are counting is not the syllable. It is the mora, and it is the key to how Japanese sounds.

## The Mora, Not the Syllable

A **mora** (plural **morae**; 拍 *haku* in Japanese) is a unit of timing: one beat of the rhythm of a word. Most morae are a consonant plus a vowel, like *ka* or *mi*, or a vowel alone, like *a*. But three other things also take a full beat of their own, even though an English speaker would never count them as syllables:

- **The moraic *n***, ん. ほん (*hon*, book) is one syllable to an English ear and two morae in Japanese: *ho-n*.
- **The held consonant**, written with a small っ. きって (*kitte*, stamp) is *ki-t-te*: three morae, and the middle one is a silent hold.
- **The second half of a long vowel.** とう in とうきょう is *to-o*, two morae. The vowel lasts twice as long, and that extra length counts as a beat.

Here are some words counted both ways:

| Japanese | Romaji | Meaning | English syllables | Morae |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ほん | hon | book | 1 | 2: ho-n |
| ねこ | neko | cat | 2 | 2: ne-ko |
| きって | kitte | stamp | 2 | 3: ki-t-te |
| にっぽん | Nippon | Japan | 2 | 4: ni-p-po-n |
| とうきょう | Tōkyō | Tokyo | 2 | 4: to-o-kyo-o |
| がっこう | gakkō | school | 2 | 4: ga-k-ko-o |
| おばあさん | obāsan | grandmother | 3 | 5: o-ba-a-sa-n |

Look at とうきょう. An English speaker hears two syllables, "Toe-kyoe", and says them in about the time it takes to say "token". A Japanese speaker hears four beats and gives each one its time. The combined sound *kyo* (きょ, written with a small ょ) is one mora, not two, which is why *Tōkyō* has four beats rather than five.

> **Tip:** Count morae on your fingers, one finger per beat, whenever you meet a new word in this chapter. It feels childish for a day and then becomes automatic, and it is the single habit that most improves a beginner's Japanese rhythm.

## An Even Rhythm

English is **stress-timed**. It squeezes unstressed syllables and stretches stressed ones, so "the CAT sat ON the MAT" bounces from beat to beat with weak syllables crushed in between. Japanese works differently. Each mora gets roughly the same amount of time, with no stressed syllable stretched and no weak ones swallowed, and the result has a steady, even pulse that linguists call **mora-timed**. Many learners describe it as sounding like a machine gun or a typewriter. Japanese speakers describe English as sounding lumpy.

The beats are not literally equal in milliseconds. Natasha Warner and Takayuki Arai (2001) reviewed nearly 40 years of experiments on Japanese timing and found the evidence mixed: some morae are measurably longer than others, and strict, clock-like timing doesn't hold. What is not in doubt is that the mora is the unit Japanese speakers count with. It is how poets count a haiku, how songwriters fit words to notes and how a word's length is judged. For a learner, the practical rule is simple: give every mora its time, and never squeeze one away.

### Why It Matters for Loanwords

The even rhythm is most dramatic in words borrowed from English. Japanese breaks up English consonant clusters with vowels, since almost every mora ends in a vowel, and then gives every mora equal weight.

> マクドナルド\
> *Makudonarudo*\
> McDonald's

In English, *McDonald's* has three syllables with a strong stress on the second: mic-DON-alds. In Japanese, it has six morae, *ma-ku-do-na-ru-do*, said evenly, with no beat louder than the others. If you say it the English way in a Tokyo shop, people may not understand you; if you say all six beats evenly, they will. The same goes for クリスマス (*kurisumasu*, Christmas, five morae) and ストライク (*sutoraiku*, strike, five morae). [Section 3.2](../03-writing-system/02-katakana.md) teaches you to read these words in katakana and decode them.

## A Small Sound System

Japanese builds every word from a short list of sounds:

- **Five vowels**: *a*, *i*, *u*, *e* and *o*. They are short, pure and never change, whether stressed or not. [Section 2.2](02-vowels-and-consonants.md) covers each one and the English habits that spoil them.
- **About fifteen consonant sounds**, most of which English has too. A handful differ in ways that matter: the *r*, the *f* of ふ, the *ts* of つ and the moraic *n* are the ones to work on.
- **Very few combinations.** Japanese has no clusters like English *str-* or *-ngths*. A mora is at most a consonant, a *y* glide and a vowel, as in *kyo* or *ryu*.

Put together, these give a little over 100 distinct morae. Learn to say those, and you can pronounce every word in the language. English, with its clusters and its many vowels, has thousands of possible syllables. This is why Japanese pronunciation is one of the friendliest parts of the language: the inventory is small and regular, and very little of it is new to an English speaker's mouth.

What *is* new is what Japanese does with time and pitch. Length of vowels and consonants changes the word, as [section 2.3](03-long-vowels-and-double-consonants.md) explains, and standard Japanese gives each word a pattern of high and low pitch, the subject of [section 2.4](04-pitch-accent-and-intonation.md). Those are the contrasts English speakers find hardest, and they get the most attention in this chapter.

## Sounds, Kana and Romaji

This chapter is about sound, so it writes Japanese mainly in romaji, with kana alongside as a preview. The two scripts you will learn in [chapter 3](../03-writing-system/README.md), hiragana and katakana, are both built on the mora: each kana character stands for one mora, with two exceptions that the sound system explains neatly. A combined sound such as きょ (*kyo*) uses two characters, a full-size き and a small ょ, for one mora; and the small っ is a character for the silent held beat. Once you can count morae, kana spelling holds very few surprises. Chapter 3 owns the spelling rules, such as how long vowels are written; this chapter owns how they sound.

**Romaji** (ローマ字 *rōmaji*, "Roman letters") is the romanization of Japanese. This book uses **modified Hepburn**, the system you will see on signs, maps and in most textbooks, with a macron over long vowels: *Tōkyō*, *kōhī* (coffee), *obāsan*. Romaji is a bridge, and a slightly treacherous one, because its letters look like English and invite English habits. *Karaoke* is four even morae, *ka-ra-o-ke*, not "carry-OH-kee". *Sake* is *sa-ke*, two clean vowels, not "saki". When you see romaji in this chapter, read the letters as the Japanese sounds in [section 2.2](02-vowels-and-consonants.md), not as English spelling.

> **Note:** Signs and brand names in Japan often drop the macrons, so you will see "Tokyo", "Kyoto" and "Osaka" where the pronunciation is *Tōkyō*, *Kyōto* and *Ōsaka*. In running English text this book uses the familiar spellings; in examples it always marks long vowels, because the length is part of the word.

## Training Your Ear

You cannot learn sounds from a page, and for the hard contrasts in this chapter you cannot rely on your ear's first impressions either. English has trained it for years to ignore exactly the differences that Japanese uses: vowel length, consonant length and pitch on a word. The good news is that adults can retrain their ears, and there is a well-tested way to do it.

[Section 1.2](../01-how-to-learn-japanese/02-study-and-immersion.md) introduces **high-variability phonetic training** (HVPT): many short rounds of hearing a word and choosing what you heard, with immediate feedback, using recordings from many different speakers. The method was developed in the early 1990s to teach Japanese listeners the English *r* and *l* (Logan, Lively and Pisoni, 1991), and a review by Ron Thomson (2018) found it effective across dozens of studies and many languages. The variety is the point. Hearing one teacher teaches you that teacher; hearing many voices, male and female, fast and slow, teaches you what all their long vowels have in common.

This chapter applies HVPT to the Japanese contrasts, and [section 2.3](03-long-vowels-and-double-consonants.md) reports what happened when researchers trained English speakers on Japanese length. For now, set up your audio sources, because every section from here on uses them:

| Resource | What it gives you | Cost (as of 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| [OJAD](https://www.gavo.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/ojad/eng/pages/home) (Online Japanese Accent Dictionary) | The pitch pattern of any word, with male and female audio, from the University of Tokyo | Free |
| [Forvo](https://forvo.com) | Words recorded by many different native speakers | Free to listen |
| [YouGlish](https://youglish.com/japanese) | Any word or phrase in real YouTube clips, many speakers and speeds | Free |
| Your dictionary app | Audio for each word you look up ([section 1.3](../01-how-to-learn-japanese/03-apps-courses-and-tools.md)) | Varies |

A simple routine works from day one: when you learn a word, hear it from at least three different voices before you say it yourself, then record yourself on your phone and compare. You will hear mistakes in the recording that you cannot hear while you are speaking.

### Going Deeper: A Word Game That Counts in Morae

Japanese children play しりとり (*shiritori*, "taking the end"): each player says a word that starts with the last mora of the word before. りんご (*ringo*, apple) leads to ごりら (*gorira*, gorilla), which leads to ラッパ (*rappa*, trumpet), and so on. The game shows the mora in action, because the unit you pass on is always one beat: from とうきょう you must continue with *o*, the second half of the long vowel, not with *kyo*. And a word that ends in ん loses on the spot, because no Japanese word begins with the moraic *n*. Try a round with a Japanese friend once you know a few dozen words; it is a fine test of whether you are hearing the beats.

## Summary

- Japanese counts time in morae, not syllables: ほん (*hon*) has two, にっぽん (*Nippon*) four and とうきょう (*Tōkyō*) four.
- Besides consonant-plus-vowel beats, the moraic ん, the held consonant (small っ) and the second half of a long vowel each take a full mora.
- Japanese rhythm is even, with no stressed beat stretched or weak one swallowed; loanwords such as マクドナルド (*Makudonarudo*, six morae) must be said that way to be understood.
- The sound system is small: five pure vowels, about fifteen consonant sounds and a little over 100 morae; the hard parts are length and pitch.
- Romaji is a bridge to kana, not English spelling; this book uses modified Hepburn with macrons for long vowels.
- Train your ear with many voices and feedback (high-variability phonetic training), using free sources such as OJAD, Forvo and YouGlish.
