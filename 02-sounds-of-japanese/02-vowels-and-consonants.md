# Vowels and Consonants

Ask an English speaker to say *sake* and you will probably hear "SAH-kee" or "SAH-kay". Ask a Japanese speaker and you hear two short, even beats, *sa-ke*, with an *e* like the one in "bed" that stops cleanly instead of sliding into "ay". The difference is small, and it is the whole story of Japanese vowels: the sounds are mostly familiar, and the work is in keeping them pure. The consonants are the same: most are old friends, and a handful are close cousins of English sounds that need a slightly different tongue.

## Five Pure Vowels

Japanese has five vowels, and each has exactly one sound, wherever it appears in a word.

| Kana | Romaji | Sounds like | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| あ | a | "a" in *father*, but shorter | あか *aka*, red |
| い | i | "ee" in *feet*, short and crisp | いぬ *inu*, dog |
| う | u | "oo" in *food*, short, with flat, relaxed lips | うみ *umi*, sea |
| え | e | "e" in *bed* | えき *eki*, station |
| お | o | "o" in *open*, with no glide at the end | おと *oto*, sound |

The *u* is the one that differs most from English. English "oo" pushes the lips forward into a round pout; Japanese *u* keeps the lips relaxed and nearly flat, closer to a short "oo" said with a slight smile. Say *sushi* in front of a mirror: if your lips push forward on the *su*, ease them back.

### The English Diphthong Trap

English rarely lets a vowel stand still. The vowel in "say" is really two vowels, "eh" sliding into "ee", and the vowel in "go" is "oh" sliding into "oo". These gliding vowels are called **diphthongs**, and they are the commonest source of an English accent in Japanese. Japanese *e* and *o* do not glide.

| Word | Romaji | English-accented | Japanese |
|---|---|---|---|
| ね | ne | "nay" | *ne*, like "ne" in "net" |
| そこ | soko | "sow-coe" | *so-ko*, two clean, short *o* |
| けいこ | keiko | "kay-koe" | *ke-e-ko*, a long *e*, then a short *o* |
| とけい | tokei | "toe-kay" | *to-ke-e*, short *o*, long *e* |

To fix it, freeze your mouth at the start of the vowel and hold it there until the mora is over. It helps to practice in front of a mirror: your jaw and lips should not move during the vowel.

The second trap is the opposite: English reduces unstressed vowels to a weak "uh", called **schwa**. Say "banana" and only the middle *a* is a real *a*; the others are "buh" and "nuh". Japanese never does this. バナナ (*banana*) has three identical *a*, each a full beat. Every vowel in a Japanese word gets its full sound, because no syllable is unstressed.

When two vowels meet, as in あい (*ai*, love) or かえる (*kaeru*, to go home), each keeps its own beat. *Ai* is two morae, *a-i*, even though it sounds close to English "eye". It is not wrong to run them together smoothly in fast speech, but they should never shrink to a single beat.

## Consonants That Differ from English

Most Japanese consonants (*k*, *g*, *s*, *t*, *d*, *n*, *h*, *b*, *p*, *m*, *y*) are close enough to English that you can use your English sounds and be understood. One small refinement: Japanese *p*, *t* and *k* have a lighter puff of air than English ones at the start of a word, so they sound a little softer, like the *p* in "spin" rather than "pin". The consonants in the table below are the ones worth real practice.

| Sound | Romaji | How to make it | English trap | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ら row | r | A single quick tap of the tongue tip just behind the upper teeth, like the *tt* in American "butter" | English *r* (tongue curled back) or *l* (tongue held against the ridge) | ありがとう *arigatō*, thank you |
| ふ | fu | Blow gently through slightly parted lips, with no teeth touching the lip | English *f*, with teeth on the lower lip | ふじさん *Fujisan*, Mount Fuji |
| つ | tsu | The *ts* of "cats", at the start of a word | Dropping the *t* ("sunami") | つなみ *tsunami*, tsunami |
| し | shi | Like English "she", with the tongue flatter and the lips not pushed forward | Saying "see" | すし *sushi* |
| ち | chi | Like "chee" in "cheese", with the same flat tongue | Saying "tee" | ちず *chizu*, map |
| じ | ji | Like "jee" in "jeep", softer | Saying "zee" | じかん *jikan*, time |
| ひ | hi | An *h* with the tongue raised toward the roof of the mouth, like the "h" in "huge" | A plain, breathy *h* | ひと *hito*, person |
| わ | wa | A *w* with only light rounding of the lips | Over-rounding into "wuh" | わたし *watashi*, I |
| ん | n | A full mora of nasal sound; it changes with the next sound (see below) | Swallowing it into the vowel before | ほん *hon*, book |

The Japanese *r* deserves the most attention because it appears everywhere. It is neither the English *r* nor the English *l*, and Japanese speakers don't hear a difference between those two, which is why the English *r* and *l* are famously hard for them. Aim for the quick tap in "butter" or "ladder" said the American way, and practice with *ra ri ru re ro* until it is a light flick. Some speakers make it closer to an *l* or a *d* at the start of a word; any tap-like sound is understood. An English *r*, with the tongue curled back, is the one sound that marks you as a foreign speaker at once.

> **Tip:** If the tap won't come, say "a lotta" quickly, the American way. The *tt* in the middle is almost exactly the Japanese *r*. Keep that light flick and say *a-ra*, *a-ri*, *a-ru*, then drop the first vowel: *ra*, *ri*, *ru*.

### The Moraic N

ん is the only Japanese consonant that stands alone as a mora, and its sound changes with the sound after it. Your mouth does this naturally when you say the words fluently, so the table is mostly for recognition; the thing to practice is giving ん its full beat.

| Before | ん sounds like | Example | Romaji | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| *p*, *b*, *m* | *m* | さんぽ, しんぶん | sanpo, shinbun | a walk, newspaper |
| *t*, *d*, *n*, *ts*, *ch*, *r*, *j* | *n* | みんな, べんとう | minna, bentō | everyone, boxed lunch |
| *k*, *g* | *ng*, as in "sing" | りんご, まんが | ringo, manga | apple, comic |
| a vowel, *y*, *w*, *s*, *h*, or the end of a word | a nasal sound made at the back of the mouth, with the tongue not touching the top | ほん, れんあい | hon, ren'ai | book, romance |

The last row is the one English speakers get wrong. At the end of a word, as in ほん (*hon*), the Japanese ん is softer than English *n*: the tongue tip doesn't touch the ridge behind the teeth, and the sound is closer to the end of "song" said lazily. Before a vowel, ん still takes its own beat, and this is where it can change the word.

> きねん\
> *kinen*\
> commemoration (three morae: ki-ne-n)
>
> きんえん\
> *kin'en*\
> no smoking (four morae: ki-n-e-n)

Modified Hepburn marks the boundary with an apostrophe, as in *kin'en* and *ren'ai*. You will see the sign 禁煙 (*kin'en*) in every station in Japan, so it is worth getting right. Note too that this book writes *shinbun* and *sanpo* with an *n*, following the kana, although the sound is *m*; some romanizations write *shimbun*.

## Vowel Devoicing

Listen to a Japanese speaker say です (*desu*, "is") and you will hear *des*. The *u* seems to vanish. It hasn't quite: it is **devoiced**, said with the mouth in the vowel's position but without the voice, so it comes out as a whisper or disappears into the consonant before it. This is **vowel devoicing**, and it is one of the clearest marks of natural standard Japanese.

The rule is regular. The two "high" vowels, *i* and *u*, are usually devoiced:

- **between two voiceless consonants** (*k*, *s*, *sh*, *t*, *ch*, *ts*, *h*, *f*, *p*): the *i* in した (*shita*, did) and the *u* in すき (*suki*, like);
- **at the end of a phrase after a voiceless consonant**: the *u* in です and ます (*masu*).

| Kana | Romaji | Meaning | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|---|
| です | desu | is | *des* |
| ます | -masu | (polite verb ending) | *mas* |
| した | shita | did | *sh'ta* |
| すき | suki | like | *s'ki* |
| ひと | hito | person | *h'to* |
| くつ | kutsu | shoes | *k'tsu* |
| ちかてつ | chikatetsu | subway | *ch'katetsu* |
| すし | sushi | sushi | *s'shi* |

Two things keep devoicing from being a problem. First, it doesn't change the rhythm: the devoiced mora still takes its beat, so *s'ki* still has two morae. Second, it is never wrong to say the vowel fully; you will simply sound careful, like someone reading aloud. Copy it when you hear it, and don't worry about it otherwise. The vowels *a*, *e* and *o* are rarely devoiced.

Devoicing is strongest in Tokyo and eastern Japan. In Kansai and much of western Japan, speakers devoice much less, and です often keeps a clear *u*: one of the many small differences you will hear when you travel, described in [section 6.2](../06-spoken-japanese/02-dialects.md). The contractions of casual speech, such as わからない becoming わかんない, are a different process, covered in [section 6.1](../06-spoken-japanese/01-spoken-vs-written.md).

> **Warning:** Devoicing is not deletion. Learners who discover that *desu* sounds like *des* sometimes start dropping vowels everywhere, saying *sk'* for *suki* with no time for the second beat, or devoicing *a* and *o*. Keep the beat; let only *i* and *u* go quiet, and only between voiceless sounds or at the end.

### Going Deeper: The Vanishing Nasal G

Older Tokyo speakers, and NHK announcers trained in the standard, often pronounce *g* in the middle of a word as *ng*, the sound in "singer": かがみ (*kagami*, mirror) becomes *ka-nga-mi*, and the particle が becomes *nga*. This soft **nasal g** (鼻濁音 *bidakuon*) was long taught as the refined standard. Among younger speakers in Tokyo and most of the country it has largely given way to an ordinary *g*, and as of 2026 it survives mainly among older speakers, in some regions and in professional broadcasting and singing. Learners should use a plain *g*. Recognize the nasal version when you hear it, in an old film or a news reading, and enjoy it as a sound with a history.

## English Approximations at a Glance

English approximations get you started, but each one has a catch. Use this table as a first guide, then let audio correct you.

| Japanese | Romaji | Close English sound | The catch |
|---|---|---|---|
| あ | a | *father* | Shorter; no "aw" |
| い | i | *feet* | Shorter; no glide |
| う | u | *food* | Flat, unrounded lips |
| え | e | *bed* | Never "ay" |
| お | o | *open* | Never "oh-oo" |
| ら row | r | *butter* (American) | A tap, never a curled *r* |
| ふ | fu | *who*, with a breath through the lips | No teeth on the lip |
| つ | tsu | *cats* | Keep the *t* at the start of a word |
| し | shi | *she* | Flatter tongue |
| ん | n | *n*, *m* or *ng* | Always a full beat |

## Summary

- Japanese has five pure vowels that never glide or shrink: keep *e* from becoming "ay" and *o* from becoming "oh-oo", and give every vowel its full sound.
- Japanese *u* is said with flat, relaxed lips, not the rounded English "oo".
- The *r* is a single quick tap of the tongue, like the *tt* in American "butter"; ふ is blown through the lips; つ keeps its *t* even at the start of a word.
- The moraic ん always takes a full beat and changes its sound to *m*, *n* or *ng* to match the next consonant; *kinen* and *kin'en* are different words.
- In standard Japanese, *i* and *u* are often devoiced between voiceless consonants and at the end of a phrase (です sounds like *des*), but the mora keeps its time.
