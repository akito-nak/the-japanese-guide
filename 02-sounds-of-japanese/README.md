# Chapter 2 — The Sounds of Japanese

Here is some good news to start with: for an English speaker, Japanese pronunciation is one of the friendliest parts of the language. It has five vowels where English has a dozen or more, a short list of consonants that are mostly familiar, and almost no consonant clusters. There are no tones to master and no sounds that need a mouth you don't already have. A beginner who reads *sushi*, *karaoke* or *Hokkaidō* aloud is already most of the way to saying them correctly.

The traps are few, but they are the kind that change words. Japanese counts time in small, even beats, and a word with one beat too many or too few is a different word: おばさん (*obasan*) is an aunt, and おばあさん (*obāsan*) is a grandmother. A held breath separates きて (*kite*, come) from きって (*kitte*, a stamp). And standard Japanese lays a pattern of high and low pitch over each word, so that 箸 (*hashi*, chopsticks) and 橋 (*hashi*, bridge) sound different to a Tokyo ear even though they are spelled alike.

None of these is hard to say once you can hear it, and hearing is where most learners struggle, because English trains the ear to ignore exactly these differences. So this chapter trains your ears as well as your mouth, with listening methods tested on English speakers learning Japanese. It uses romaji, with kana alongside as a preview: [chapter 3](../03-writing-system/README.md) teaches you to read the kana, and every sound you learn here will be waiting for you there.

## In This Chapter

- Count words in morae, the even beats of Japanese, and see why マクドナルド has six of them
- Say the five pure vowels without English glides, and make the consonants that differ from English: the tapped *r*, the soft *f*, *ts*, *shi*, *chi* and the moraic *n*
- Hear and use vowel devoicing, the reason です sounds like *des*
- Hear and produce long vowels and double consonants, the contrasts that matter most for being understood
- Recognize the four pitch-accent patterns of standard Japanese, and the intonation of questions and of ね and よ
- Train your ear with research-backed listening practice, and close each skill with real speech
