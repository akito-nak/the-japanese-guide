# Pitch Accent and Intonation

Every Japanese teacher has a favorite example, and it is nearly always this one. はし (*hashi*) can mean chopsticks (箸), a bridge (橋) or an edge (端). Written in kana, the three are identical. Said by someone from Tokyo, they are not: the voice falls after the first beat for chopsticks, rises and then falls on the particle after the word for a bridge, and rises and stays up for an edge. That rise and fall is **pitch accent** (高低アクセント *kōtei akusento*, "high-low accent"): a pattern of high and low pitch that belongs to each word, the way stress belongs to each English word.

This section is the full treatment of pitch accent in this book. It covers what the patterns are, how much they matter, what research says about learning them, and how to train them, then turns to the intonation of whole sentences.

## Pitch, Not Stress

English marks one syllable in each word as stressed: louder, longer and usually higher. Compare "REcord" (a noun) and "reCORD" (a verb). Japanese does something simpler. Every mora keeps the same loudness and length, as [section 2.1](01-how-japanese-sounds-work.md) explains, and the only thing that moves is pitch, between two levels, high and low.

This book writes pitch with one letter per mora: **H** for high, **L** for low. A dash separates the word from a particle that follows it, such as が (*ga*), because the particle often reveals the pattern. So 箸が (*hashi ga*, chopsticks [subject]) is written HL-L: *ha* high, *shi* low, *ga* low.

Two rules govern standard Tokyo Japanese, and together they explain every pattern below:

1. **The first two morae differ in pitch.** If the first mora is high, the second is low; otherwise the first is low and the second high.
2. **Once the pitch falls, it stays down** for the rest of the word. A word has at most one fall.

The mora just before the fall is called the **accent nucleus** (アクセント核 *akusento kaku*). Everything about a word's pattern follows from where the fall is, or whether there is one at all.

## The Four Patterns of Tokyo Japanese

Tokyo Japanese, the basis of the standard language, sorts words into four patterns by where the fall comes.

| Pattern | Japanese | Where the pitch falls | Example | Romaji | Meaning | Pitch |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Heiban** ("flat") | 平板 | Never; the particle stays high | 桜が | sakura ga | cherry blossom | LHH-H |
| **Atamadaka** ("head-high") | 頭高 | After the first mora | 猫が | neko ga | cat | HL-L |
| **Nakadaka** ("middle-high") | 中高 | Inside the word, after a middle mora | 日本が | Nihon ga | Japan | LHL-L |
| **Odaka** ("tail-high") | 尾高 | After the last mora, on the particle | 弟が | otōto ga | younger brother | LHHH-L |

Heiban is the most common pattern, and the one English speakers find strangest, because nothing happens: the voice rises after the first mora and simply stays up, with no fall at all. An English ear, used to a strong peak in every word, hears it as oddly flat.

### The Particle Test

Look at odaka and heiban. On the word alone, both are low on the first mora and high on the rest, so はな (*hana*) said alone sounds the same whether it means "flower" or "nose". Add a particle and the difference appears:

> 花が\
> *hana ga* (LH-L)\
> the flower (pitch falls on the particle)
>
> 鼻が\
> *hana ga* (LH-H)\
> the nose (pitch stays high)

This is the **particle test**: say the word with が after it and listen for whether the particle drops. It is also the reason a word's pattern can't be learned from the word alone. Learn it from audio of the word in a phrase.

### Accent Numbers

Japanese dictionaries, including OJAD and the NHK accent dictionary, mark each word's pattern with a number: the mora after which the pitch falls. **0** means heiban, with no fall; **1** means atamadaka; and a number equal to the word's length in morae means odaka. So 桜 is [0], 猫 [1], 日本 (*nihon*) [2] and 弟 (*otōto*, four morae) [4]. Once you can read the number, you can work out the H and L pattern of any word you look up.

## Minimal Pairs

A few dozen common pairs of words differ only in pitch. These are the classics:

| Word | Romaji | Meaning | Pattern | Pitch with が |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 箸 | hashi | chopsticks | atamadaka [1] | HL-L |
| 橋 | hashi | bridge | odaka [2] | LH-L |
| 端 | hashi | edge | heiban [0] | LH-H |
| 雨 | ame | rain | atamadaka [1] | HL-L |
| 飴 | ame | candy | heiban [0] | LH-H |
| 牡蠣 | kaki | oyster | atamadaka [1] | HL-L |
| 柿 | kaki | persimmon | heiban [0] | LH-H |
| 花 | hana | flower | odaka [2] | LH-L |
| 鼻 | hana | nose | heiban [0] | LH-H |

Kanji tell these words apart in writing, which is one reason Japanese can afford so many words that sound alike: [section 3.3](../03-writing-system/03-kanji.md) explains how kanji carry meaning. In speech, pitch does part of the job and context does the rest.

## How Much It Matters, Honestly

Here is the plain truth: pitch accent rarely decides whether you are understood. Nobody at a dinner table wonders whether you want rain or candy, and true minimal pairs are few. Most textbooks give pitch accent a page or two and never mark it again, and plenty of learners reach fluency without studying it.

What pitch accent decides is how natural you sound. Teachers and learners alike report that wrong pitch patterns are among the most persistent marks of a foreign accent, long after grammar and vocabulary are solid. That is experience, not a measured result, but it fits the few studies of how learners pick up pitch.

- **It is not picked up by exposure alone.** Irina Shport (2008) tested 16 American learners in their third year of university Japanese, none of whom had been taught pitch accent. They produced the correct pattern 56% of the time and discriminated the patterns 46% of the time, both close to chance, and they tended to give every word a fall, as if it had an English stressed syllable. Sixteen native speakers of Tokyo-type Japanese given the same tasks scored 81% and 59%; the listening task was hard even for them. Shport concluded that, at least at the intermediate level, learners do not acquire the patterns implicitly from classroom input. It is one small study, but its conclusion agrees with an earlier review of 30 years of research by Ayusawa (2003), cited in the same paper, which found that learners mostly failed to produce noun accents correctly whatever their background.
- **Training helps, unevenly.** Shport (2016) trained English listeners with no experience of pitch accent to identify three Tokyo patterns in two-syllable words, using many words, contexts and voices, with immediate feedback after each answer. Their accuracy improved more than an untrained control group's and carried over to new words in new contexts, but the heiban pattern stayed the hardest to identify. Shport traces this to the listeners' native language: English ears listen for where the pitch peaks, and the absence of a fall is easy to miss.
- **Seeing your pitch helps too.** In a small study, Yukari Hirata (2002) had four intermediate learners practice saying words and sentences for ten 30-minute sessions while a computer program drew their pitch and timing next to a native speaker's model. Compared with four untrained learners, they improved in both producing and perceiving pitch and length contrasts, in words and in sentences: their combined scores rose from 52% to about 70%, against about 57% for the untrained group. A journal version of the study followed (Hirata, 2004b).

Taken together, these small studies give a consistent picture: pitch accent needs deliberate attention, it responds to training with feedback, and the heiban pattern needs extra care. So spend a little time on it early, when it is cheap to learn each word's pattern along with the word, and more later if a natural accent matters to you.

> **Tip:** Make the particle test part of how you learn vocabulary. When you add a new noun to your flashcards, listen to it with が or は after it and note whether the particle drops. It costs seconds per word and, over a year, builds the pattern into thousands of words.

## Regional Differences

Everything above describes Tokyo Japanese, the standard taught in textbooks and heard on national news. Japan's regional varieties have their own accent systems. Kansai, around Osaka and Kyoto, has a different system altogether, with many everyday words carrying different patterns from Tokyo; and in parts of southern Tohoku, northern Kanto (Tochigi and Ibaraki) and central Kyushu, many speakers make no accent distinctions between words at all. A Japanese speaker uses pitch partly to place where someone is from, and to Tokyo ears a Kansai accent is instantly recognizable. Learn the Tokyo patterns, and enjoy the others when you meet them; [section 6.2](../06-spoken-japanese/02-dialects.md) introduces the main dialects.

## Sentence Intonation

On top of word accent, Japanese has **intonation**: the rise and fall of the whole phrase, which carries questions, attitude and emphasis. The two work together. Each word keeps its own pattern, the overall pitch steps down gradually through a sentence, and the intonation acts mostly at the end, on the last mora or the final particle.

**Questions rise.** A polite question ends in か with a rising pitch. In casual speech, the rise alone makes a question, so the same words can be a statement or a question:

> 行く。\
> *Iku.* (falling)\
> I'm going.
>
> 行く？\
> *Iku?* (rising)\
> Are you going?

**ね asks for agreement.** The sentence-final particle ね (*ne*) invites the listener to share what you said, like "isn't it?" or "right?". Said with a gentle rise, it asks for agreement; said with a level or falling tone, it sounds more like a sigh of shared feeling.

> いい天気ですね。\
> *Ii tenki desu ne.*\
> Nice weather, isn't it?

**よ tells.** The particle よ (*yo*) marks information the listener may not have, like "you know" or "I'm telling you". A light rise keeps it friendly; a strong fall can sound insistent or annoyed.

> もう九時ですよ。\
> *Mō kuji desu yo.*\
> It's already nine o'clock, you know.

These are tendencies, not fixed rules, and the best way to learn them is to hear them in context. [Section 4.1](../04-building-sentences/01-grammar-basics.md) introduces か, ね and よ with the other essential particles, and [section 6.1](../06-spoken-japanese/01-spoken-vs-written.md) shows how casual speech uses them.

> **Warning:** Don't carry English question intonation into Japanese. English speakers often lift the pitch of a stressed word near the end ("Do you like SUshi?"), which can override the word's own accent. In Japanese, let each word keep its pattern and put the rise on the final mora.

## How to Train Pitch Accent

1. **Look up the pattern with the word.** OJAD ([gavo.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/ojad](https://www.gavo.t.u-tokyo.ac.jp/ojad/eng/pages/home)), from the University of Tokyo, is free and shows the pitch of nouns and of every conjugated form of verbs and adjectives, with male and female audio. For a dictionary, the NHK accent dictionary, 『NHK日本語発音アクセント新辞典』 (2016), has about 75,000 entries and is available as an app with recordings by NHK announcers (as of 2026).
2. **See whole sentences.** OJAD's Prosody Tutor, called *Suzuki-kun*, draws the predicted pitch curve for any sentence you type and reads it aloud. Use it to check a sentence before you practice it.
3. **Shadow.** **Shadowing** is repeating a recording aloud while it plays, a fraction of a second behind the speaker, copying rhythm and pitch as closely as you can. [Section 1.2](../01-how-to-learn-japanese/02-study-and-immersion.md) explains the method and its evidence (Hamada, 2016); for pitch accent, it trains your voice to produce the patterns at speed, inside phrases, where they matter. Start with short clips that have a transcript, such as NHK News Web Easy or a textbook's audio.
4. **Record yourself and compare.** Say a phrase, record it, and play it next to the model. For a picture of your pitch, the free phonetics program Praat draws the pitch curve of any recording, the same kind of visual feedback Hirata (2002) used.
5. **Train heiban on purpose.** Since English speakers struggle most to hear the absence of a fall (Shport, 2016), practice pairs where one word is heiban: *hana ga* (flower) against *hana ga* (nose), *ame ga* against *ame ga*. Listen for the particle staying up.

### Going Deeper: Why Learners Rarely Hear About It

If pitch accent is part of every word, why do most courses skip it? Shport (2008) offered several reasons from the teaching side: a wrong pattern seldom blocks understanding, minimal pairs are few, and communicative teaching puts grammar and function first. There is also a technical reason. Pitch patterns change when words combine and when verbs conjugate, so a full description quickly becomes heavy. Tools such as OJAD were built precisely to make that complexity searchable, and courses such as Dogen's video series on Japanese phonetics now teach pitch accent to learners directly. As of 2026 it is easier to learn than ever, and still optional for being understood.

## Go Say This

Find a Japanese speaker from the Tokyo area, a tutor or a language-exchange partner, and test your pitch on them. Say 雨が降っている (*ame ga futte iru*, "it's raining") with *ame* as HL, then again with *ame* as LH, and ask which one sounded like rain and which like candy. Then try 花がきれい (*hana ga kirei*, "the flowers are pretty") with the particle falling, and again with it staying high, which makes it "the nose is pretty". If they laugh at the second one, your pitch is working. Finally, say 行く (*iku*) once falling and once rising, and see whether they answer the question.

## Summary

- Standard Japanese has pitch accent: each word has a pattern of high and low pitch, while every mora keeps the same loudness and length.
- In Tokyo Japanese the first two morae differ in pitch, and a word falls at most once; the four patterns are heiban (no fall), atamadaka, nakadaka and odaka, and the particle test tells odaka from heiban.
- Minimal pairs such as 箸, 橋 and 端 (*hashi*), or 雨 and 飴 (*ame*), differ only in pitch, but context resolves nearly all of them; pitch matters more for sounding natural than for being understood.
- Learners don't pick up pitch accent from exposure alone (Shport, 2008), but training with feedback improves it, with the flat heiban pattern the hardest to learn (Shport, 2016).
- Sentence intonation rides on top of word accent: questions rise at the end, ね asks for agreement and よ tells.
- Train with OJAD and an accent dictionary, shadowing, recording yourself and deliberate practice on heiban words.
