# Food — Eating Your Way Through the Language

The menu is handwritten in brush-stroke kanji, the photos are missing, and the waiter is waiting. Then you spot 焼き鳥 and realize you can read it: *yakitori*, grilled chicken. That small victory is the moment many learners fall in love with Japanese, and food is the most delicious way to keep it going. Menus, ingredients, restaurant phrases and regional specialties are vocabulary you can use three times a day and taste immediately.

## The Philosophy of Japanese Food

**Washoku** (和食), traditional Japanese cuisine, was added to UNESCO's list of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2013, recognized as a set of social practices built around respect for nature and the seasons. A handful of ideas run through it.

- **Umami** (旨味), the savory "fifth taste" alongside sweet, sour, salty and bitter. The Japanese chemist Kikunae Ikeda identified and named it in 1908, after isolating glutamate from kelp stock. Dashi, soy sauce, miso and other fermented foods are full of it.
- **Shun** (旬), the peak season of an ingredient. Bamboo shoots in spring, eel in high summer, matsutake mushrooms and saury in autumn: dishes and menus follow the calendar, and eating something at its shun is half the pleasure.
- 盛り付け (*moritsuke*), presentation. Food is arranged with care for color, space and the dish it sits on, so that it is enjoyed with the eyes first.
- Balance. Traditional meals aim for variety in flavor, color and cooking method, often summed up as 一汁三菜 (*ichijū sansai*, one soup and three dishes, with rice).
- Freshness, above all for raw fish, which is why fish markets such as Tokyo's Toyosu ([section 8.3](../08-getting-around/03-shopping-and-money.md)) are so central to the cuisine.

The foundation of much of it is **dashi** (出汁), a light stock usually made from kelp (昆布, *konbu*) and dried, smoked bonito flakes (鰹節, *katsuobushi*). It flavors miso soup, noodle broths and simmered dishes, and it is the reason "vegetable" dishes in Japan often contain fish.

## Essential Food Vocabulary

### Cooking Methods

Many dish names are built from a cooking verb, so learning the verbs unlocks whole sections of the menu: 焼き鳥 is grilled chicken, 揚げ出し豆腐 is deep-fried tofu in broth.

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 焼く | yaku | Grill, bake, pan-fry |
| 煮る | niru | Simmer |
| 茹でる | yuderu | Boil (in water) |
| 揚げる | ageru | Deep-fry |
| 蒸す | musu | Steam |
| 炒める | itameru | Stir-fry |
| 漬ける | tsukeru | Pickle, marinate |
| 生 | nama | Raw; also draft (beer) |
| 刺身 | sashimi | Sliced raw fish |

### Flavors

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 甘い | amai | Sweet |
| 辛い | karai | Spicy hot; also salty in some regions |
| しょっぱい | shoppai | Salty |
| 酸っぱい | suppai | Sour |
| 苦い | nigai | Bitter |
| おいしい / うまい | oishii / umai | Delicious (うまい is more casual) |
| まずい | mazui | Bad-tasting |
| こってり | kotteri | Rich, heavy |
| あっさり | assari | Light, clean-tasting |
| 濃い | koi | Strong (flavor), thick |
| 薄い | usui | Mild, weak, thin |

## The Big Dishes

### Sushi

Sushi (寿司) means vinegared rice, not raw fish; the fish is the topping. The main styles are these:

| Japanese | Reading | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 握り寿司 | nigirizushi | Hand-pressed rice topped with fish or other toppings |
| 巻き寿司 | makizushi | Rolls wrapped in seaweed |
| 手巻き寿司 | temakizushi | Hand rolls in a cone, often made at home |
| ちらし寿司 | chirashizushi | Toppings scattered over a bowl of rice |
| 押し寿司 | oshizushi | Rice and fish pressed in a mold; an Osaka specialty |
| いなり寿司 | inarizushi | Rice in a sweet fried-tofu pouch |

At the counter, the chef will gladly choose for you. **Omakase** (お任せ, "I leave it to you") means letting the chef decide what to serve, usually at a set price.

> おまかせでお願いします。\
> *Omakase de onegai shimasu.*\
> I'll leave it to the chef, please.

> わさび抜きでお願いします。\
> *Wasabi-nuki de onegai shimasu.*\
> Without wasabi, please.

At a conveyor-belt sushi shop (回転寿司, *kaiten-zushi*), most orders now go through a touch screen, and the plates' colors or the screen show the price.

### Ramen

Ramen (ラーメン) is a regional passion, and every town has opinions. Broths fall into four main families.

| Japanese | Reading | Base | Associated with |
|---|---|---|---|
| 醤油 | shōyu | Soy sauce | Tokyo; clear brown broth |
| 塩 | shio | Salt | Hakodate; light and clear |
| 味噌 | miso | Miso | Sapporo; rich and hearty |
| 豚骨 | tonkotsu | Pork bone | Hakata (Fukuoka); creamy and rich |

Many shops let you customize your bowl. These requests come up all the time:

> 麺は硬めでお願いします。\
> *Men wa katame de onegai shimasu.*\
> Firm noodles, please.

> 味濃いめでお願いします。\
> *Aji koime de onegai shimasu.*\
> Stronger seasoning, please.

> 替え玉ください。\
> *Kaedama kudasai.*\
> Another serving of noodles, please.

The 替え玉 (*kaedama*, noodle refill) is a Hakata ramen custom: order it while you still have broth left.

### Izakaya Food

The **izakaya** (居酒屋) is a casual Japanese pub where food and drink arrive in waves of small plates for the table to share. It is where coworkers unwind and friends talk for hours.

| Japanese | Reading | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 枝豆 | edamame | Salted boiled young soybeans |
| 唐揚げ | karaage | Japanese fried chicken |
| 焼き鳥 | yakitori | Grilled chicken skewers |
| 冷奴 | hiyayakko | Chilled tofu with toppings |
| 刺身盛り合わせ | sashimi moriawase | Assorted sashimi |
| 漬物 | tsukemono | Pickles |
| だし巻き卵 | dashimaki tamago | Rolled omelet made with dashi |
| お茶漬け | ochazuke | Rice in tea or broth, a classic closing dish |

The last carbohydrate dish of the evening is called 締め (*shime*, the finish). And when you sit down, a small dish you didn't order will usually appear: this is **otōshi** (お通し), a small appetizer served automatically and charged as a cover fee. It is normal, not a mistake.

> とりあえず生で。\
> *Toriaezu nama de.*\
> A draft beer to start, please.

> 生中二つください。\
> *Namachū futatsu kudasai.*\
> Two medium draft beers, please.

| Japanese | Reading | Description |
|---|---|---|
| 天ぷら | tenpura | Seafood and vegetables in light, crisp batter |
| うどん | udon | Thick wheat noodles, served cold (ざる, *zaru*) or in hot broth (かけ, *kake*) |
| そば | soba | Buckwheat noodles |

| 焼肉 | yakiniku | Meat grilled at the table, of Korean origin |

| すき焼き | sukiyaki | Beef simmered in sweet soy, dipped in raw egg |

| 牛丼 | gyūdon | Beef bowl, a fast-food staple |
| 納豆 | nattō | Fermented soybeans; sticky, pungent and either loved or loathed |
| 味噌汁 | misoshiru | Miso soup, served with most traditional meals |

## Regional Food Culture

Regional specialties are a point of real local pride, and souvenir shops at every station sell them. The cities of [section 8.2](../08-getting-around/02-cities-and-regions.md) each have their own, and the table gives a national overview.

| Region | Famous foods |
|---|---|
| Hokkaido | Seafood, dairy, soup curry, miso ramen |
| Tōhoku | わんこそば (*wanko soba*, endless small bowls of soba, Iwate), きりたんぽ (*kiritanpo*, grilled pounded rice on skewers, Akita) |
| Tokyo | Monjayaki, shōyu ramen, Edomae nigiri sushi |
| Osaka | Takoyaki, okonomiyaki, kushikatsu (fried skewers) |
| Kyoto | Kaiseki (formal multi-course cuisine), yudōfu (simmered tofu), Kyoto pickles |
| Hiroshima | Layered okonomiyaki with noodles, oysters |
| Fukuoka | Tonkotsu ramen, mentaiko, motsu nabe |
| Okinawa | Chanpurū stir-fries, Okinawa soba, gōyā (bitter melon), awamori |

## Konbini Food

The konbini ([section 8.3](../08-getting-around/03-shopping-and-money.md)) is a serious part of Japanese food culture. Its shelves change with the seasons, and its products are a daily reading exercise:

- Onigiri (rice balls) in a dozen or more fillings, wrapped so the seaweed stays crisp
- Bento, noodles and pasta that the staff will heat for you
- Steamed buns and fried chicken from the counter, and oden simmering by the register in winter
- Freshly brewed coffee from a self-serve machine
- Egg-salad sandwiches that have their own international fan club

The cashier will usually ask you something as you pay:

> お弁当温めますか。\
> *O-bentō atatamemasu ka.*\
> Shall I heat up your bento?

> お箸はおつけしますか。\
> *O-hashi wa o-tsuke shimasu ka.*\
> Would you like chopsticks?

## Restaurant Phrases

### Arriving

> いらっしゃいませ！何名様ですか。\
> *Irasshaimase! Nanmei-sama desu ka.*\
> Welcome! How many people?

> 二人です。\
> *Futari desu.*\
> Two people.

> 予約した田中です。\
> *Yoyaku shita Tanaka desu.*\
> I have a reservation; the name is Tanaka.

Since April 2020, smoking indoors has been banned in most restaurants, so you will rarely need to ask for a non-smoking seat (禁煙席, *kin'enseki*); some small bars are exempt and post a sign at the door.

### Ordering

| Japanese | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| すみません！ | sumimasen! | Excuse me! (to call staff) |
| 注文お願いします | chūmon onegai shimasu | We'd like to order |
| これをください | kore o kudasai | This one, please (pointing) |
| おすすめは何ですか | osusume wa nan desu ka | What do you recommend? |
| 〜抜きでお願いします | ~ nuki de onegai shimasu | Without ~, please |
| 〜のアレルギーがあります | ~ no arerugī ga arimasu | I'm allergic to ~ |
| ベジタリアンです | bejitarian desu | I'm vegetarian |

Calling out すみません to get a server's attention is normal and polite; many tables also have a call button (呼び出しボタン, *yobidashi botan*).

### Finishing

> お会計お願いします。\
> *O-kaikei onegai shimasu.*\
> The bill, please.

> 別々でお願いします。\
> *Betsubetsu de onegai shimasu.*\
> Separate bills, please.

> ごちそうさまでした。\
> *Gochisōsama deshita.*\
> Thank you for the meal.

Pay at the register by the door rather than at the table, and don't tip: good service is included, and leaving money behind often sends a server running after you to return it.

## Dietary Restrictions

Special diets take some planning in Japan, though awareness is growing, especially in large cities.

Vegetarians and vegans should know that fish-based dashi is in a great many dishes that look vegetarian, including miso soup and noodle broths. Ask directly:

> 出汁は何を使っていますか。\
> *Dashi wa nani o tsukatte imasu ka.*\
> What do you use for the stock?

> 魚介類は入っていますか。\
> *Gyokairui wa haitte imasu ka.*\
> Does this contain fish or seafood?

Gluten-free eating is hard, because most soy sauce is brewed with wheat. Some shops stock tamari or gluten-free soy sauce: グルテンフリーのものはありますか (*do you have anything gluten-free?*). Halal options are growing in Tokyo, Osaka and tourist areas; look for ハラール (*harāru*) certification.

> **Warning:** For a serious allergy, carry a card that states it in Japanese, such as そばアレルギーがあります。少量でも危険です (I have a buckwheat allergy; even a small amount is dangerous). Buckwheat, wheat, egg, milk, peanuts, shrimp and crab are among the allergens that packaged food in Japan must list.

## Drinking Culture

お酒 (*osake*) means alcohol in general, and 日本酒 (*nihonshu*) is what English calls sake. A typical night out runs through several of these.

| Japanese | Reading | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| ビール | bīru | Beer; 生ビール (draft) is the usual first order |
| 日本酒 | nihonshu | Sake: hot (熱燗, *atsukan*), room temperature (冷や, *hiya*) or chilled (冷酒, *reishu*) |
| 焼酎 | shōchū | A distilled spirit made from sweet potato, barley, rice and more |
| ハイボール | haibōru | Whisky and soda, hugely popular |
| チューハイ / サワー | chūhai / sawā | Shōchū with fruit flavor and soda |
| ウイスキー | uisukī | Whisky; Japanese makers such as Suntory and Nikka are world-renowned |

Non-alcoholic staples are just as important: お茶 (*ocha*, green tea, often free with a meal), 麦茶 (*mugicha*, roasted barley tea, served cold in summer) and 水 (*mizu*, water). Tap water is safe to drink throughout Japan, and restaurants bring it without being asked.

## Eating Customs at a Glance

Most visitors worry more than they need to; these few habits cover nearly every situation. [Section 9.1](01-culture-and-norms.md) explains the reasons behind them.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Say いただきます and ごちそうさまでした | Stand chopsticks upright in rice |
| Slurp your noodles | Pass food from chopsticks to chopsticks |
| Pour drinks for others | Fill your own glass at a formal dinner |
| Lift your rice or soup bowl toward your mouth | Leave lots of food uneaten if you can help it |
| Eat street food where you bought it | Eat while walking through crowded streets, which many people frown on |

## Summary

- Washoku, UNESCO-listed since 2013, rests on seasonality (旬), presentation and umami, the savory taste named by Kikunae Ikeda in 1908.
- Dashi, a stock of kelp and bonito, flavors most of the cuisine, so "vegetable" dishes often contain fish; vegetarians should ask 出汁は何を使っていますか.
- Cooking verbs and flavor words (焼く, 揚げる, こってり, あっさり) let you decode a large part of any menu.
- At an izakaya, the small unordered dish called お通し is a normal cover charge; at a sushi counter, おまかせ leaves the choice to the chef.
- Every region has its signature foods, from Sapporo's miso ramen to Osaka's takoyaki and Hakata's tonkotsu ramen.
- Call staff with すみません, pay at the register, don't tip and finish with ごちそうさまでした.
