# Honorific Japanese — Keigo

Your company president has just arrived, and you want to say "the president has come." In ordinary polite Japanese, 社長が来ました (*shachō ga kimashita*) is grammatical but sounds strangely flat, almost presumptuous. What a Japanese office worker says is 社長がいらっしゃいました (*shachō ga irasshaimashita*). The verb itself has changed to one that lifts the president up. Now imagine telling a client "I will come to your office tomorrow": the verb changes again, this time to one that lowers *you*: 明日伺います (*ashita ukagaimasu*).

That is 敬語 (*keigo*, honorific language): a grammatical system that encodes the relationship between speaker, listener and the person being talked about in every verb and many nouns. It is expected in business, required at formal occasions and admired when done well. It also has a reputation for being terrifying. It is less so once you see that it runs on one simple principle: *raise them, lower yourself.*

## The Three Pillars of Keigo

Keigo is traditionally taught as three subsystems that work together.

| System | Reading | Direction | In one phrase |
|---|---|---|---|
| 尊敬語 | *sonkeigo* | Raises the other person | "What *you* do is exalted" |
| 謙譲語 | *kenjōgo* | Lowers yourself or your group | "What *I* do is humble" |
| 丁寧語 | *teineigo* | Polite toward the listener | The です and ます system of [section 5.3](03-polite-japanese.md) |

**Sonkeigo** (尊敬語, respectful language) is used for the actions and things of the person you respect. **Kenjōgo** (謙譲語, humble language) is used for your own actions, or your group's, especially actions directed toward that person. The two work as a pair:

- When your boss *does* something, use sonkeigo to elevate the action.
- When *you* do something for your boss, use kenjōgo to humble the action.

The most common beginner mistake is to mix up the direction: using a humble verb for a customer's action, or an honorific verb for your own.

### Going Deeper: Five Kinds of Keigo

In 2007 Japan's Council for Cultural Affairs (文化審議会, *bunka shingikai*) published official guidelines on keigo, 敬語の指針 (*keigo no shishin*). They split the traditional three categories into five. Kenjōgo is divided into kenjōgo I, which humbles you in relation to the person your action affects (伺う *ukagau*, to visit, 差し上げる *sashiageru*, to give), and kenjōgo II, also called **teichōgo** (丁重語, courteous language), which makes your own speech more formal toward the listener without anyone being on the receiving end (参る *mairu*, to go, おる *oru*, to be, いたす *itasu*, to do). 丁寧語 stays as it is, and the fifth category is 美化語 (*bikago*), the お and ご prefixes from [section 5.3](03-polite-japanese.md). You don't need the five-way system to speak well, but it explains why 参ります can be used even when no one is being "visited".

## Sonkeigo: Raising the Other Person

### Special Sonkeigo Verbs

A handful of everyday verbs have their own special honorific replacements. These are the ones you will hear most.

| Plain verb | Meaning | Sonkeigo | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| いる *iru* | be (a person) | いらっしゃる | *irassharu* |
| 行く *iku* | go | いらっしゃる | *irassharu* |
| 来る *kuru* | come | いらっしゃる / お見えになる | *irassharu* / *omie ni naru* |
| 食べる *taberu* | eat | 召し上がる | *meshiagaru* |
| 飲む *nomu* | drink | 召し上がる | *meshiagaru* |
| 言う *iu* | say | おっしゃる | *ossharu* |
| する *suru* | do | なさる | *nasaru* |
| 知っている *shitte iru* | know | ご存じだ | *gozonji da* |
| くれる *kureru* | give (to me) | くださる | *kudasaru* |
| 見る *miru* | see, look | ご覧になる | *goran ni naru* |
| 寝る *neru* | sleep | お休みになる | *oyasumi ni naru* |
| 着る *kiru* | wear | お召しになる | *omeshi ni naru* |

> **Note:** いらっしゃる covers three verbs at once (be, go and come), and context tells you which. Its ます form is irregular: いらっしゃいます, not いらっしゃります. The same goes for おっしゃいます, なさいます and くださいます.

You will see ご存じ written both as ご存じ and ご存知; the official guidelines write the final syllable in kana (御存じ), and this book follows them.

> 部長は明日いらっしゃいますか？\
> *Buchō wa ashita irasshaimasu ka?*\
> Will the department manager be in tomorrow?
>
> 先生がそうおっしゃいました。\
> *Sensei ga sō osshaimashita.*\
> The teacher said so.
>
> どうぞ召し上がってください。\
> *Dōzo meshiagatte kudasai.*\
> Please, help yourself.

### The お〜になる Pattern

For verbs without a special form, sonkeigo uses a pattern: **お + verb stem + になる**. Verbs made from Chinese-origin nouns with する usually take ご instead: ご利用になる (*goriyō ni naru*, to use a service).

| Verb | Meaning | Sonkeigo | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| 待つ *matsu* | wait | お待ちになる | *omachi ni naru* |
| 帰る *kaeru* | go home | お帰りになる | *okaeri ni naru* |
| 使う *tsukau* | use | お使いになる | *otsukai ni naru* |
| 書く *kaku* | write | お書きになる | *okaki ni naru* |
| 持つ *motsu* | hold, have | お持ちになる | *omochi ni naru* |
| 読む *yomu* | read | お読みになる | *oyomi ni naru* |
| 思う *omou* | think | お思いになる | *oomoi ni naru* |

> 社長はもうお帰りになりました。\
> *Shachō wa mō okaeri ni narimashita.*\
> The president has already gone home.

### The Passive-Form Honorific

A lighter way to show respect uses the passive form of the verb (〜れる, 〜られる): 社長が来られました (*shachō ga koraremashita*, the president came). It is common in business and in speech about people you respect, and it is easy to build, but it sounds less deferential than the special verbs and can be confused with a real passive. Use the special verbs where they exist.

## Kenjōgo: Lowering Yourself

### Special Kenjōgo Verbs

These replace everyday verbs when *you* or your group act, especially toward someone you respect.

| Plain verb | Meaning | Kenjōgo | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| いる *iru* | be (a person) | おる | *oru* |
| 行く *iku* | go | 参る / 伺う (to their place) | *mairu* / *ukagau* |
| 来る *kuru* | come | 参る | *mairu* |
| 食べる / 飲む *taberu* / *nomu* | eat, drink | いただく | *itadaku* |
| 言う *iu* | say | 申す / 申し上げる | *mōsu* / *mōshiageru* |
| する *suru* | do | いたす | *itasu* |
| もらう *morau* | receive | いただく | *itadaku* |
| あげる *ageru* | give (to them) | 差し上げる | *sashiageru* |
| 知っている *shitte iru* | know | 存じておる / 存じ上げる (a person) | *zonjite oru* / *zonjiageru* |
| 思う *omou* | think | 存じる | *zonjiru* |
| 見る *miru* | see | 拝見する | *haiken suru* |
| 読む *yomu* | read | 拝読する | *haidoku suru* |
| 聞く *kiku* | ask, hear | 伺う / 承る | *ukagau* / *uketamawaru* |
| 訪ねる *tazuneru* | visit | 伺う | *ukagau* |
| 会う *au* | meet | お目にかかる | *ome ni kakaru* |

### The お〜する Pattern

The humble counterpart of お〜になる is **お + verb stem + する** (or the more humble お〜いたす): お待ちする (*omachi suru*, I'll wait for you), お持ちします (*omochi shimasu*, let me carry it). Chinese-origin verbs take ご: ご案内します (*goannai shimasu*, let me show you the way), ご連絡いたします (*gorenraku itashimasu*, I will contact you).

> 明日、御社に伺います。\
> *Ashita, onsha ni ukagaimasu.*\
> I will visit your company tomorrow.
>
> 先ほど田中様よりご連絡をいただきました。\
> *Sakihodo Tanaka-sama yori gorenraku o itadakimashita.*\
> I received a message from Mr. Tanaka a short while ago.
>
> お荷物、お持ちします。\
> *Onimotsu, omochi shimasu.*\
> Let me carry your bags.

## Teineigo at Its Most Formal

The です and ます system has its own formal upgrades. You will hear them constantly in shops, offices and announcements.

| Standard polite | Formal | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| です | でございます | *de gozaimasu* |
| あります | ございます | *gozaimasu* |
| すみません | 失礼いたします / 申し訳ございません | *shitsurei itashimasu* / *mōshiwake gozaimasen* |
| 分かりました | かしこまりました / 承知いたしました | *kashikomarimashita* / *shōchi itashimashita* |

### かしこまりました and 承知いたしました

Both mean "certainly, understood, I'll take care of it":

- かしこまりました (*kashikomarimashita*) is the standard reply in service, from convenience stores to luxury hotels, and is also used in offices.
- 承知いたしました (*shōchi itashimashita*) is the typical reply in office settings and business email.

Both sound far more professional than 了解です (*ryōkai desu*, got it), which many people consider too casual to say to a superior or a client.

## Keigo Vocabulary Pairs

Keigo reaches into nouns too. Many have an honorific form for the other side and a humble form for your own.

| Neutral | Honorific (theirs) | Humble (yours) |
|---|---|---|
| 会社 *kaisha*, company | 御社 *onsha* (spoken) / 貴社 *kisha* (written) | 弊社 *heisha* |
| 人 *hito*, person | 方 *kata* / お方 *okata* | 者 *mono* |
| 家 *ie*, home | お宅 *otaku* | 自宅 *jitaku* / 拙宅 *settaku* (very humble) |
| 意見 *iken*, opinion | ご意見 *goiken* | 私見 *shiken* |
| 名前 *namae*, name | お名前 *onamae* | 名前 *namae* |
| 連絡 *renraku*, contact | ご連絡 *gorenraku* | ご連絡 *gorenraku* (in humble お〜する phrases) |
| 説明 *setsumei*, explanation | ご説明 *gosetsumei* | ご説明 *gosetsumei* (in humble お〜する phrases) |
| 確認 *kakunin*, confirmation | ご確認 *gokakunin* | 確認 *kakunin* / ご確認 *gokakunin* |
| 報告 *hōkoku*, report | ご報告 *gohōkoku* | ご報告 *gohōkoku* |
| 挨拶 *aisatsu*, greeting | ご挨拶 *goaisatsu* | ご挨拶 *goaisatsu* |

The last two rows show that the same prefixed noun can serve both sides. In ご説明いただけますか (*gosetsumei itadakemasu ka*, could you explain?), ご説明 is their action; in ご説明いたします (*gosetsumei itashimasu*, let me explain), it is yours, humbly offered.

> **Warning:** Use 御社 or 貴社 only for the other company and 弊社 only for your own. Mixing them up is one of the most noticeable errors in business Japanese.

## Keigo in Real Situations

### A Business Phone Call

A receptionist takes a call for a colleague who is away:

> お電話ありがとうございます。株式会社〇〇でございます。\
> *Odenwa arigatō gozaimasu. Kabushiki-gaisha 〇〇 de gozaimasu.*\
> Thank you for calling. This is 〇〇 Corporation.
>
> 少々お待ちいただけますでしょうか。\
> *Shōshō omachi itadakemasu deshō ka.*\
> Could you hold for a moment, please?
>
> 申し訳ございません。田中はただいま席を外しております。\
> *Mōshiwake gozaimasen. Tanaka wa tadaima seki o hazushite orimasu.*\
> I'm very sorry. Tanaka is away from his desk at the moment.
>
> 戻りましたら、折り返しご連絡させていただきます。\
> *Modorimashitara, orikaeshi gorenraku sasete itadakimasu.*\
> When he returns, we will call you back.

No さん for Tanaka: he is uchi, and the caller is soto (see [section 5.1](01-levels-overview.md)).

### A Business Email

A typical business email follows a fixed shape: recipient's company and name, the opener, the sender's self-introduction, the body and a closing.

```text
件名：〇〇のご確認について

〇〇株式会社
〇〇部　〇〇様

お世話になっております。
株式会社〇〇の田中でございます。

先日はお時間をいただきありがとうございました。
早速ではございますが、ご確認いただきたい件がございます。

[本文]

ご不明な点がございましたら、どうぞお気軽にご連絡ください。
引き続きよろしくお願いいたします。

株式会社〇〇
田中 太郎
```

In order, the lines say: Subject: request to confirm 〇〇; the recipient's company, department and name with 様; "thank you for your continued support"; "this is Tanaka of 〇〇 Corporation"; "thank you for your time the other day"; "to come straight to the point, there is a matter I would like you to confirm"; the body; "if anything is unclear, please feel free to contact me"; "I look forward to continuing to work with you"; and the signature. [Chapter 13](../13-business-japanese/README.md) covers business writing in depth.

### In Meetings

> ご説明いただけますでしょうか。\
> *Gosetsumei itadakemasu deshō ka.*\
> Could you please explain that?
>
> おっしゃる通りでございます。\
> *Ossharu tōri de gozaimasu.*\
> It is exactly as you say.
>
> 検討させていただきます。\
> *Kentō sasete itadakimasu.*\
> We will consider it. (Often a polite way of saying no.)
>
> おかげさまで順調に進んでおります。\
> *Okagesama de junchō ni susunde orimasu.*\
> Thanks to you, things are going smoothly.

## The させていただく Construction

〜させていただく (*-sasete itadaku*) combines the causative "let (me) do" with humble いただく "receive": literally "I humbly receive your permission to do". It is everywhere in modern Japanese.

> 確認させていただきます。\
> *Kakunin sasete itadakimasu.*\
> Let me confirm that.
>
> 出席させていただきます。\
> *Shusseki sasete itadakimasu.*\
> I will (gratefully) attend.

It is expected in business and, used where someone has granted permission, it signals consideration for the listener. It is also widely overused; [section 5.5](05-modern-keigo.md) shows when it misfires and what to say instead.

## Mistakes That Reveal a Learner

### Honoring Your Own Group to an Outsider

> 田中さんはいらっしゃいません。\
> *Tanaka-san wa irasshaimasen.*\
> Wrong when an outside caller asks for your colleague: it elevates your own side.
>
> 田中はおりません。\
> *Tanaka wa orimasen.*\
> Right: Tanaka is not here. Your colleague is humbled to the outsider.

### Double Keigo

Stacking two honorifics of the same kind on one verb, **二重敬語** (*nijū keigo*, double keigo), sounds overdone. [Section 5.5](05-modern-keigo.md) has more examples.

> おっしゃられました。\
> *Osshararemashita.*\
> Wrong: おっしゃる is already honorific, so the extra られ is redundant.
>
> おっしゃいました。\
> *Osshaimashita.*\
> Right: (he or she) said.

### A Humble Verb for Someone Else's Action

> 社長がいただきます。\
> *Shachō ga itadakimasu.*\
> Wrong: いただく humbles the eater, so it cannot describe the president eating.
>
> 社長が召し上がります。\
> *Shachō ga meshiagarimasu.*\
> Right: the president eats, in sonkeigo.

### お or ご?

As a rule of thumb, words of Chinese origin take ご (ご確認 *gokakunin*, ご連絡 *gorenraku*, ご説明 *gosetsumei*) and native words take お (お知らせ *oshirase*, お待ち *omachi*, お届け *otodoke*). Common exceptions, such as お電話 (*odenwa*) and お時間 (*ojikan*), are best learned one by one.

## Choosing the Right Form

When you are unsure, ask who is acting and who is listening.

| Who is acting? | Listener or context | Use |
|---|---|---|
| A superior, client or customer | Anyone | Sonkeigo (いらっしゃる, お〜になる) |
| You | A superior, client or customer | Kenjōgo (伺う, お〜する) |
| Your colleague (uchi) | Someone from outside | Kenjōgo, no さん |
| Nobody in particular | Formal setting | Formal teineigo (でございます) |

If they are doing it, raise it. If you are doing it, lower it.

## Summary

- Keigo has three classic parts: sonkeigo raises the other person's actions, kenjōgo lowers your own and teineigo (です and ます) is polite toward the listener.
- Common verbs have special keigo forms, such as いらっしゃる, おっしゃる and 召し上がる (sonkeigo) and 参る, 申す and いただく (kenjōgo).
- Verbs without special forms use patterns: お + stem + になる for sonkeigo and お + stem + する or いたす for kenjōgo.
- Speaking to an outsider, treat your own colleagues as uchi: no さん, and humble verbs for their actions.
- Honorific and humble nouns come in pairs, such as 御社 or 貴社 for the other company and 弊社 for your own.
- Never use a humble verb for someone else's action or an honorific verb for your own, and avoid stacking two honorifics on one verb.
