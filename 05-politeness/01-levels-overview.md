# The Politeness Levels — The System That Runs Everything

Picture three people asking you the same question, "Are you going?" A friend says 行く？ (*iku?*). A shop clerk says 行きますか？ (*ikimasu ka?*). A hotel concierge, speaking to a guest, says いらっしゃいますか？ (*irasshaimasu ka?*). Same meaning, three different verbs, and each one tells you something about who is speaking to whom.

In English you speak differently to your boss and to your friends, but mostly through word choice and tone. In Japanese the difference is built into the grammar. Verb endings change, whole verbs get swapped out, and even the word you pick for "I" signals your register. Get the level wrong and you sound either rude or robotic. Get it right and people stop noticing your Japanese and start listening to you.

## Why the System Exists

Japanese social life has long been organized around relationships: insider and outsider, 先輩 (*senpai*, a senior in a group) and 後輩 (*kōhai*, a junior), and position in a hierarchy. The language evolved to encode those relationships directly, so that every sentence quietly answers the question "how do we stand toward each other?"

The appropriate level depends on the relationship more than on the place. You can speak casually in a formal restaurant (to a close friend across the table) and politely at a noisy festival (to a stranger who asks for directions).

Four factors decide the level:

1. **Familiarity.** How well do you know this person?
2. **Hierarchy.** Are they senior or junior to you, by age, rank or role?
3. **Group membership.** Are they inside your group or outside it?
4. **Context.** Is this a professional, social or personal situation?

## The Three Major Levels

Learners usually meet the system as three levels. Each has its own verb forms, its own vocabulary and its own typical partners.

### Level 1: Casual

**Casual speech**, often called **タメ語** (*tamego*, speech between equals), is what you use with close friends, family, people younger than you in relaxed settings, and pets.

- Verbs in the plain form (行く *iku*, 食べる *taberu*, 来る *kuru*)
- だ (*da*) instead of です (*desu*), or no copula at all
- Particles often dropped
- Contractions everywhere (〜てる *-teru*, 〜ちゃう *-chau*)
- First person: 俺 (*ore*) for many men, あたし (*atashi*) or うち (*uchi*) for many women, or no pronoun at all

[Section 5.2](02-casual-japanese.md) covers casual speech in depth.

### Level 2: Polite

**Polite speech**, **丁寧語** (*teineigo*, polite language), is the level for strangers, acquaintances, people you have just met, service encounters and everyday work.

- Verbs in the ます (*masu*) form (行きます *ikimasu*, 食べます *tabemasu*, 来ます *kimasu*)
- The copula です (*desu*)
- Particles mostly present
- First person: 私 (*watashi*)

This is the level most textbooks teach first, and it is the safe default for any new relationship. [Section 5.3](03-polite-japanese.md) covers it in depth.

### Level 3: Honorific

**敬語** (*keigo*, honorific language) is for superiors, clients, important guests, formal occasions and older people you do not know well.

- Special verbs (行く → いらっしゃる *irassharu*, 言う → おっしゃる *ossharu*, 食べる → 召し上がる *meshiagaru*)
- Set constructions (〜でいらっしゃる *-de irassharu*, 〜ていただく *-te itadaku*)
- Longer, more cushioned sentences
- First person: 私 (*watakushi*, the formal reading of the same kanji), or 弊社 (*heisha*, "our humble company") when speaking for your employer

Keigo is a system of its own, covered in [section 5.4](04-honorific-japanese.md).

> **Note:** Strictly speaking, Japanese grammar books count 丁寧語 as one *type* of 敬語, alongside the honorific and humble forms. The three-level picture is a learner's simplification, and a useful one: in practice "polite" means です and ます, and "keigo" means the special honorific and humble verbs layered on top.

The spelling of "polite language" also trips people up. The standard reading is 丁寧語 (*teineigo*); you will often see it romanized as *teinego*, which reflects how the long *ei* is pronounced.

## One Concept, Three Levels

The table below shows the same ideas at each level. In the keigo column, "hon." marks a form that elevates the other person and "hum." a form that lowers yourself; [section 5.4](04-honorific-japanese.md) explains the difference.

| Concept | Casual | Polite | Keigo |
|---|---|---|---|
| I | 俺 *ore* / あたし *atashi* | 私 *watashi* | 私 *watakushi* |
| You | 君 *kimi* / お前 *omae* | name + さん *san* | name + 様 *sama* |
| Be (a person) | いる *iru* | います *imasu* | いらっしゃいます *irasshaimasu* (hon.) / おります *orimasu* (hum.) |
| Eat | 食べる *taberu* | 食べます *tabemasu* | 召し上がります *meshiagarimasu* (hon.) / いただきます *itadakimasu* (hum.) |
| Go | 行く *iku* | 行きます *ikimasu* | いらっしゃいます *irasshaimasu* (hon.) / 参ります *mairimasu* (hum.) |
| Say | 言う *iu* | 言います *iimasu* | おっしゃいます *osshaimasu* (hon.) / 申します *mōshimasu* (hum.) |
| Come | 来る *kuru* | 来ます *kimasu* | いらっしゃいます *irasshaimasu* (hon.) / 参ります *mairimasu* (hum.) |
| Know | 知ってる *shitteru* | 知っています *shitte imasu* | ご存じです *gozonji desu* (hon.) / 存じております *zonjite orimasu* (hum.) |
| Receive | もらう *morau* | もらいます *moraimasu* | いただきます *itadakimasu* (hum.) |
| Give (to someone else) | あげる *ageru* | あげます *agemasu* | 差し上げます *sashiagemasu* (hum.) |
| Give (to me) | くれる *kureru* | くれます *kuremasu* | くださいます *kudasaimasu* (hon.) |
| Thank you | ありがとう *arigatō* | ありがとうございます *arigatō gozaimasu* | 誠にありがとうございます *makoto ni arigatō gozaimasu* |
| Sorry | ごめん *gomen* | すみません *sumimasen* | 申し訳ございません *mōshiwake gozaimasen* |

## The "I" Problem

Japanese has a whole wardrobe of words for "I", and the one you wear says something about you before you finish the sentence.

| Pronoun | Reading | Who uses it | Impression |
|---|---|---|---|
| 私 | *watashi* | Everyone, in polite speech | Standard, neutral |
| 私 | *watakushi* | Everyone, in formal speech | Formal, careful |
| 僕 | *boku* | Mostly men and boys | Gentle, boyish, modest |
| 俺 | *ore* | Mostly men, casual | Masculine, direct |
| 俺様 | *oresama* | Arrogant characters in fiction | "I, the magnificent"; never sincere |
| あたし | *atashi* | Mostly women, casual | Soft, informal |
| うち | *uchi* | Women and girls, especially in western Japan | Casual, regional |
| あたくし | *atakushi* | Some older women | Very formal, elegant, a little old-fashioned |
| 自分 | *jibun* | Athletes, the military, some men | Earnest, disciplined; in Kansai, 自分 can also mean "you" |
| わし | *washi* | Older men, western dialects | Elderly, regional |

A related word, 我が (*waga*, our or my), is not a pronoun but appears in formal writing and set phrases: 我が国 (*waga kuni*, our nation). [Section 6.3](../06-spoken-japanese/03-role-language.md) shows how fiction uses pronouns like these to build characters.

## The "You" Problem

This surprises many learners: あなた (*anata*, you) is awkward in most real conversations. Said directly to someone's face, it can sound distant, confrontational or like a line from a soap opera.

| Situation | What to use | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Addressing someone | Their name + さん | 田中さんは？ (*Tanaka-san wa?*) |
| Addressing a superior or teacher | Their title | 先生は… (*sensei wa…*) / 部長は… (*buchō wa…*) |
| Talking to a spouse | あなた (affectionate, traditional) | ねえ、あなた (*nē, anata*) |
| Songs, ads and writing | あなた (poetic, fine in text) | あなたが好き (*anata ga suki*) |
| Addressing a group | 皆さん (*minasan*) | 皆さんはどうですか？ (*Minasan wa dō desu ka?*) |

When in doubt, use the person's name. It is never wrong, and Japanese speakers use names far more often than English speakers do.

## Uchi and Soto

Behind the whole system sits one of the most important ideas in Japanese social life: **uchi and soto** (内 and 外, inside and outside).

- 内 (*uchi*): your group, such as your family, close friends or company
- 外 (*soto*): everyone outside it, such as strangers, other companies and the public

The rule is simple to state and hard to apply: *elevate outsiders, humble your insiders.* When you speak to someone from another company about your own colleague, the colleague counts as "us", so you drop the さん and use humble verbs:

> 田中はただいま席を外しております。\
> *Tanaka wa tadaima seki o hazushite orimasu.*\
> Tanaka is away from his desk at the moment. (No さん: he is uchi, and the listener is soto.)

Speaking to the same colleague directly, he is no longer "us talking to them", so the さん comes back:

> 田中さん、お客様がいらっしゃいました。\
> *Tanaka-san, okyakusama ga irasshaimashita.*\
> Mr. Tanaka, a client has arrived.

The same person gets different treatment depending on who is listening. That shift trips up more learners than any verb form.

## Default Levels for Common Situations

The table below gives sensible starting points. Real life adjusts them, but you will rarely go wrong starting here.

| Situation | Default level | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Meeting someone for the first time | Polite | Start polite and let the other person signal a shift to casual |
| At a convenience store | Polite | Basic politeness; staff will speak keigo to you |
| With close friends | Casual | Staying formal with close friends feels cold |
| With your boss | Polite, rising to keigo | Depends on the company culture |
| With a client | Keigo | Expected in business |
| In a job interview | Keigo | Throughout |
| A work email | Keigo | Written business style |
| Personal social media | Casual | Business accounts post in polite style |
| Older people you do not know | Polite | A respectful default |
| A group with mixed familiarity | Polite | Match the least familiar person present |

## When the Level Is Wrong

Getting the level wrong has real social consequences, in both directions.

**Too casual too soon** sounds rude or presumptuous, especially with seniors or at work. It creates instant awkwardness.

**Too formal with close friends** creates distance and can sound cold or even sarcastic. Imagine answering "you coming out tonight?" with "I regret that I am unable to attend this evening."

**Keigo used incorrectly** can be worse than no keigo at all. Applying an honorific verb to yourself, for example, sounds as if you are praising yourself. If you are unsure, plain polite speech with です and ます is always acceptable.

The good news: Japanese speakers are generally forgiving of a learner's register mistakes. A sincere attempt at politeness counts for far more than perfect forms.

## Code-Switching in Real Life

Native speakers move between levels constantly and without effort. In a single morning, an office worker might:

- Chat casually with a coworker at the coffee machine
- Switch to polite speech the moment a manager walks up
- Use full keigo when a client phones
- Message the same coworker casually five minutes later

For a learner, the goal is not to hit the perfect register every time from day one. It is to develop a feel for which register each situation calls for, and that feel grows with every conversation you have and every drama you watch.

## Summary

- Japanese encodes social relationships in its grammar: verb forms, vocabulary and pronouns all change with the level of politeness.
- Learners work with three levels: casual (plain forms, タメ語), polite (です and ます, 丁寧語) and honorific (敬語, special honorific and humble verbs).
- The right level depends on familiarity, hierarchy, group membership and context, more than on the physical setting.
- Uchi and soto mean you elevate outsiders and humble insiders, so you drop さん for your own colleague when speaking to someone from another company.
- Avoid あなた in conversation; use the person's name or title instead.
- When unsure, start polite and follow the other person's lead toward casual speech.
