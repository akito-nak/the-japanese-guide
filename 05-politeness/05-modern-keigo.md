# Modern and Manual Keigo — バイト敬語 and the Living Politeness System

You order a coffee at a café in Tokyo. The server sets it down and says こちら、コーヒーになります (*kochira, kōhī ni narimasu*). Taken literally, that is "this will become coffee." You hand over a 1,000-yen note for a 600-yen bill and hear 1000円からお預かりします (*sen en kara oazukari shimasu*), "I am holding from 1,000 yen." Neither sentence matches what your textbook taught you, and both are perfectly normal Japanese that you will hear every single day.

[Section 5.4](04-honorific-japanese.md) presented keigo as the textbook and the official guidelines describe it. Behind shop counters, you hear something a little different: **バイト敬語** (*baito keigo*, part-timer keigo), also called マニュアル敬語 (*manyuaru keigo*, manual keigo) or コンビニ敬語 (*konbini keigo*, convenience-store keigo). These are polite formulas that spread through the training manuals of chain stores and restaurants from roughly the 1990s onward. Some of them break traditional rules. Some have a logic that linguists still argue about. All of them are everywhere, so your first job is to recognize them, and your second is to decide what you will say yourself.

Textbooks teach you to produce correct keigo. They rarely help you understand the slightly unorthodox keigo you will hear 20 times a day. Each form below comes with its traditional alternative, so you can understand the one and choose the other.

## The Classic Disputed Phrases

For each phrase below: what you will hear, why purists object and what they would prefer.

| You'll hear | Reading | The objection | Traditional version |
|---|---|---|---|
| お皿のほう、お下げします | *osara no hō, osage shimasu* | のほう (*no hō*, "the direction of") normally contrasts options; here it is padding | お皿をお下げします *osara o osage shimasu* |
| こちら、コーヒーになります | *kochira, kōhī ni narimasu* | なる means "become"; the coffee is already coffee | コーヒーでございます *kōhī de gozaimasu* |
| 1000円からお預かりします | *sen en kara oazukari shimasu* | から marks a starting point; the clerk is taking the whole note | 1000円お預かりします *sen en oazukari shimasu* |
| 1000円ちょうどお預かりします | *sen en chōdo oazukari shimasu* | お預かりする implies giving change back; with the exact amount, nothing comes back | 1000円ちょうど頂戴します *sen en chōdo chōdai shimasu* |
| ご注文はよろしかったでしょうか | *gochūmon wa yoroshikatta deshō ka* | The past tense confirms an order that is being placed right now | ご注文は以上でよろしいでしょうか *gochūmon wa ijō de yoroshii deshō ka* |
| お会計のほう、1万円になります | *okaikei no hō, ichiman en ni narimasu* | Combines both complaints: an empty のほう and a "becoming" total | お会計は1万円でございます *okaikei wa ichiman en de gozaimasu* |

> **Note:** Several of these are less clearly wrong than purists claim. 〜になります can mean "comes to", so 合計1000円になります (*gōkei sen en ni narimasu*, "the total comes to 1,000 yen") is fine for a calculated sum. One popular theory traces よろしかったでしょうか to northern dialects, where a past form softens a question.

These forms survive because they do a job: each adds a little distance and softness to a transaction. Whether that softness is elegant or empty is exactly what people argue about.

## Double Keigo

The opposite failure is being *too* polite. 二重敬語 (*nijū keigo*, double keigo), introduced in [section 5.4](04-honorific-japanese.md), stacks two honorifics of the same kind on one word. Japan's official keigo guidelines ([敬語の指針](https://www.bunka.go.jp/seisaku/bunkashingikai/kokugo/hokoku/pdf/keigo_tosin.pdf), *keigo no shishin*, 2007) treat it as generally inappropriate, while accepting a few combinations that have become customary, such as お召し上がりになる (*omeshiagari ni naru*, to eat) and お見えになる (*omie ni naru*, to come).

| You'll hear | Reading | Why it's excessive | Better |
|---|---|---|---|
| おっしゃられる | *ossharareru* | おっしゃる is already the honorific of 言う; られる adds a second honorific | おっしゃる *ossharu* |
| ご覧になられる | *goran ni narareru* | ご覧になる is already honorific; られる piles on another | ご覧になる *goran ni naru* |
| 拝見させていただく | *haiken sasete itadaku* | 拝見 is already the humble form of 見る; させていただく adds more humility | 拝見します *haiken shimasu* / 拝見いたします *haiken itashimasu* |

The last row is strictly a case of excessive humility rather than textbook double keigo, but style guides treat it the same way.

## The させていただく Problem

〜させていただく has grown into an all-purpose humility softener, often where it makes little sense. The 2007 guidelines say it fits when two conditions hold:

1. **Permission.** You are doing the thing with the other party's permission.
2. **Benefit.** You receive a favor or benefit from being allowed to do it.

When neither holds, a plain humble form is better.

| Overused | Reading | Why it's off | Better |
|---|---|---|---|
| 休業させていただきます | *kyūgyō sasete itadakimasu* | Closing the shop is your own decision; no one granted permission | 休業いたします *kyūgyō itashimasu* |
| 担当させていただきます | *tantō sasete itadakimasu* | Usually no one has given permission; reflexive humility | 担当いたします *tantō itashimasu* |
| 確認させていただきます | *kakunin sasete itadakimasu* | A softener where いたします is enough | 確認いたします *kakunin itashimasu* |

Compare a case where it fits perfectly:

> 本日は発表させていただき、ありがとうございました。\
> *Honjitsu wa happyō sasete itadaki, arigatō gozaimashita.*\
> Thank you for giving me the opportunity to present today.

The overuse matters in business Japanese too, not only in retail; see [chapter 13](../13-business-japanese/README.md). Scattering させていただく through every sentence is the corporate equivalent of ending each sentence with "if that makes sense."

## Service Phrases You Should Learn

Not everything at the register is controversial. The phrases below are completely standard, used everywhere and oddly absent from many textbooks. Several of them are **cushion words** (クッション言葉, *kusshon kotoba*): set phrases placed before a request or refusal to soften it, such as 恐れ入りますが and 申し訳ございませんが.

| Japanese | Reading | When it's said |
|---|---|---|
| いらっしゃいませ | *irasshaimase* | "Welcome" as you enter; no reply expected |
| かしこまりました | *kashikomarimashita* | "Certainly"; on taking your order. More deferential than 分かりました |
| 承知いたしました | *shōchi itashimashita* | "Understood"; the office equivalent |
| 少々お待ちください（ませ） | *shōshō omachi kudasai(mase)* | "One moment, please" (少々, not ちょっと) |
| お待たせいたしました | *omatase itashimashita* | "Sorry to keep you waiting"; when they come back to you |
| 恐れ入りますが | *osoreirimasu ga* | "I'm sorry to trouble you, but…" before a request; politer than すみませんが |
| 申し訳ございません | *mōshiwake gozaimasen* | "I am very sorry"; preferred to すみません in service |
| 〜円のお返しです | *… en no okaeshi desu* | "Here is your … yen change" |
| 〜でございます | *… de gozaimasu* | The formal "it is…"; the traditional alternative to 〜になります |

### Forms to Avoid Producing

A few popular phrases sound polite but draw criticism. Recognize them, and prefer the alternatives in your own speech.

| Avoid | Reading | Why | Say instead |
|---|---|---|---|
| なるほどですね | *naruhodo desu ne* | なるほど evaluates what you heard, which sounds presumptuous toward a superior | おっしゃる通りです *ossharu tōri desu* |
| お名前を頂戴できますか | *onamae o chōdai dekimasu ka* | You cannot "receive" someone's name | お名前を伺ってもよろしいですか *onamae o ukagatte mo yoroshii desu ka* |
| とんでもございません | *tondemo gozaimasen* | とんでもない is one word, so purists object to changing its ない to ございません | 恐れ入ります *osoreirimasu* / とんでもないです *tondemo nai desu* |

> **Note:** The 2007 guidelines accept とんでもございません as a way of modestly brushing off praise, so it is not strictly wrong; it is on this list because many careful speakers still dislike it. Don't swap in とんでもないことでございます here: the guidelines point out it can sound as if you are calling the compliment itself outrageous.

## Politeness Is Alive

These forms exist, and people argue about them, because politeness changes like every other part of a language. 貴様 (*kisama*) and お前 (*omae*) were once respectful words for "you" and slid over centuries into rudeness and insult; [section 6.3](../06-spoken-japanese/03-role-language.md) shows how fiction still uses them. バイト敬語 is the same process caught in the act: service workers reaching for extra politeness, the language settling into new conventions, and an older generation insisting it is all wrong.

A practical approach for learners:

- **Working in Japan?** Follow your workplace's manual, even when it uses バイト敬語. That is what your manager expects.
- **For your own polished speech?** Use the traditional forms in this section. They are never wrong.
- **Everywhere else?** Learn to recognize every form here, because you will hear them daily and need to understand what is being asked.

The next time a server tells you your coffee になります, you will know exactly what is happening, both grammatically and socially. It is a small, quietly satisfying superpower.

## Summary

- バイト敬語 (manual keigo) is the set of polite formulas spread by service-industry training, such as 〜になります, 〜のほう and 〜からお預かりします.
- Purists object to these forms on grammatical grounds, but they are universal in shops and restaurants, and some have a defensible logic.
- Double keigo (二重敬語), such as おっしゃられる, stacks two honorifics on one verb; use the single honorific form instead.
- 〜させていただく fits when you act with someone's permission and benefit from it; otherwise 〜いたします is better.
- Service staff rely on standard set phrases such as かしこまりました, 少々お待ちください and the cushion word 恐れ入りますが.
- Recognize every form you hear, follow your workplace's conventions on the job and use the traditional forms in your own careful speech.
