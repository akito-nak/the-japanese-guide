# Casual Japanese — How Friends Actually Talk

Your textbook taught you 明日は暇ですか？ (*Ashita wa hima desu ka?*, "Are you free tomorrow?"). Then a Japanese friend texts you 明日ヒマ？ (*Ashita hima?*) and you wonder where half the sentence went. Nothing is missing that matters. The topic particle, the です and the question particle have all been dropped, because between friends the tone of voice and the question mark do the work.

Casual Japanese (タメ語, *tamego*, or くだけた話し方, *kudaketa hanashikata*, relaxed speech) is how people talk with close friends, family and anyone they are truly comfortable with. It is fast, contracted, light on particles and happy to leave sentences unfinished. If textbook Japanese is a suit, casual Japanese is what people wear at home, and it is what you will hear most in anime, dramas and real friendships. Producing it naturally takes time, but recognizing it will transform how much you understand.

## The Core Shifts from Polite to Casual

Casual speech is built from the polite forms you already know by three moves: drop the polite endings, drop particles and contract verbs.

### Drop ます and です

The verb goes back to its **plain form** (the dictionary form and its relatives; see [section 2.1](../02-building-sentences/01-grammar-basics.md)), and です either becomes だ or disappears.

| Polite | Casual | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 行きます *ikimasu* | 行く *iku* | go |
| 食べます *tabemasu* | 食べる *taberu* | eat |
| 来ます *kimasu* | 来る *kuru* | come |
| します *shimasu* | する *suru* | do |
| そうです *sō desu* | そう *sō* / そうだ *sō da* | that's right |
| 大丈夫です *daijōbu desu* | 大丈夫 *daijōbu* | it's fine |
| 分かりません *wakarimasen* | 分からない *wakaranai* / 分かんない *wakannai* | I don't know |

Plain だ can sound blunt or emphatic on its own, so in conversation it is often dropped or softened with a particle: 大丈夫だよ (*daijōbu da yo*) rather than a flat 大丈夫だ.

### Drop Particles

The particles は, が, を, に and へ often vanish when the meaning is obvious.

| Full sentence | Casual version | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 学校に行く *gakkō ni iku* | 学校行く *gakkō iku* | go to school |
| これを食べる *kore o taberu* | これ食べる *kore taberu* | eat this |
| 明日は暇ですか？ *ashita wa hima desu ka?* | 明日暇？ *ashita hima?* | free tomorrow? |
| どこへ行くの？ *doko e iku no?* | どこ行くの？ *doko iku no?* | where are you going? |

### Contract Verbs

Fast speech squeezes common verb combinations into shorter forms. These contractions are one of the clearest markers of casual speech.

| Full form | Contracted | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 〜ている *-te iru* | 〜てる *-teru* | is doing |
| 〜ていた *-te ita* | 〜てた *-teta* | was doing |
| 〜てしまう *-te shimau* | 〜ちゃう *-chau* | end up doing, do completely |
| 〜てしまった *-te shimatta* | 〜ちゃった *-chatta* | ended up doing |
| 〜ておく *-te oku* | 〜とく *-toku* | do in advance |
| 〜てしまえ *-te shimae* | 〜ちゃえ *-chae* | go ahead and do it |
| 〜なければいけない *-nakereba ikenai* | 〜なきゃ *-nakya* / 〜なくちゃ *-nakucha* | have to do |
| 〜なければならない *-nakereba naranai* | 〜なきゃならない *-nakya naranai* | have to do |

> 宿題、まだやってない。やらなきゃ。\
> *Shukudai, mada yattenai. Yaranakya.*\
> I still haven't done my homework. I've got to do it.

## Sentence Endings That Carry Meaning

**Sentence-final particles** (終助詞, *shūjoshi*) are the small words at the end of a sentence that carry attitude: certainty, softness, a request for agreement. In casual speech they do much of the emotional work that polite endings do in formal speech. Some lean masculine or feminine, though these associations are looser today than textbooks suggest.

| Ending | Reading | Lean | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 〜ね | *ne* | Neutral | Seeking agreement: "right?" |
| 〜よ | *yo* | Neutral | Informing, asserting: "you know" |
| 〜よね | *yo ne* | Neutral | Confirming shared knowledge: "isn't it?" |
| 〜ぞ | *zo* | Masculine | Strong assertion: "I'm telling you" |
| 〜ぜ | *ze* | Masculine | Casual, swaggering assertion |
| 〜わ | *wa* | Feminine (rising, Tokyo); neutral in Kansai | Soft emphasis; in western Japan an everyday assertion for everyone |
| 〜の | *no* | Neutral, statements lean feminine | Asking for or giving an explanation; softener |
| 〜な | *na* | Leans masculine | Thinking aloud, mild assertion |
| 〜かな | *kana* | Neutral | "I wonder…" |
| 〜じゃん | *jan* | Neutral, younger speakers | "See? Isn't it obvious?" |
| 〜もん | *mon* | Neutral, sounds childlike or sulky | "Because…" when justifying yourself |
| 〜っけ | *kke* | Neutral | Trying to remember: "what was it again?" |
| 〜だっけ？ | *dakke?* | Neutral | "Was it…? Remind me." |
| 〜よな | *yo na* | Leans masculine | Firm agreement-seeking |

> 今日は寒いね。\
> *Kyō wa samui ne.*\
> It's cold today, isn't it?
>
> 明日テストだよ。\
> *Ashita tesuto da yo.*\
> There's a test tomorrow, you know.
>
> 会議って何時だっけ？\
> *Kaigi tte nanji dakke?*\
> What time was the meeting again?

## Common Casual Expressions

These short responses make up a surprising share of real conversation. Learn them as whole units.

### Agreeing

| Expression | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| うん | *un* | Yeah |
| そうそう | *sō sō* | Exactly, that's it |
| だよね！ | *da yo ne!* | Right?! |
| まじで | *maji de* | Seriously? For real |
| 確かに | *tashika ni* | True, fair point |
| ほんとに？ | *honto ni?* | Really? |
| そうなんだ | *sō nan da* | Oh, is that so |
| あー、なるほど | *ā, naruhodo* | Ah, I see |

### Refusing

| Expression | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ちょっと… | *chotto…* | That's a bit… (a soft no) |
| むり | *muri* | No way, impossible |
| やだ / いやだ | *yada* / *iya da* | No, I don't want to |
| どうかな… | *dō kana…* | I'm not sure… |
| うーん… | *ūn…* | Hmm… (thinking, probably no) |

### Reacting

| Expression | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| え！？ | *e!?* | What?! |
| うそ！ | *uso!* | No way! You're kidding! |
| マジか！ | *maji ka!* | Seriously?! |
| すごい！ | *sugoi!* | Amazing! |
| やばい！ | *yabai!* | Whoa! (good or bad, depending on tone) |
| さすが！ | *sasuga!* | As expected of you! |
| かわいい〜！ | *kawaii!* | So cute! |
| 最高！ | *saikō!* | The best! |
| ウケる | *ukeru* | That's hilarious |

## Casual Questions

Casual questions usually drop か (*ka*). Rising intonation does the work, and in writing a question mark carries it.

| Casual question | Reading | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 行く？ | *iku?* | You going? |
| 何してる？ | *nani shiteru?* | What are you up to? |
| どうした？ | *dō shita?* | What happened? Are you OK? |
| 大丈夫？ | *daijōbu?* | You alright? |
| どうだった？ | *dō datta?* | How was it? |
| 何食べたい？ | *nani tabetai?* | What do you want to eat? |
| 一緒に行かない？ | *issho ni ikanai?* | Want to come along? |
| これ知ってる？ | *kore shitteru?* | Do you know this? |

A negative question such as 行かない？ (*ikanai?*, "won't you go?") is the standard casual invitation. It leaves the other person an easy way to say no.

## The Art of the Unfinished Sentence

Japanese lets a sentence trail off after a particle or a conjunction and trusts the listener to fill in the rest.

| Said | Left unsaid | Full meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ちょっと… *chotto…* | 難しい *muzukashii* | That's a bit… [difficult] |
| 行けたら… *iketara…* | 行く *iku* | If I can make it… [I'll go] |
| それって… *sore tte…* | 変じゃない？ *hen ja nai?* | Isn't that kind of… [weird?] |
| 実は… *jitsu wa…* | 言いにくいんだけど *iinikui n da kedo* | The thing is… [this is hard to say] |
| でも… *demo…* | (an objection) | But… [I don't agree] |

Trailing off is a social skill, not sloppiness. It lets you say something without committing to it and gives the listener room to offer you a way out. Reading what is left unsaid is part of learning the language.

## Inviting, Accepting and Declining

Invitations are where casual Japanese shines, and where the soft no matters most.

### Inviting

> 今夜飲みに行かない？\
> *Kon'ya nomi ni ikanai?*\
> Want to go for drinks tonight?
>
> 明日ヒマ？一緒にどこか行こうよ。\
> *Ashita hima? Issho ni dokoka ikō yo.*\
> Free tomorrow? Let's go somewhere together.

### Accepting

> いいね！行こう！\
> *Ii ne! Ikō!*\
> Sounds good! Let's go!
>
> まじ？行く行く！\
> *Maji? Iku iku!*\
> Really? I'm in, I'm in!

### Declining

> ちょっとその日は…\
> *Chotto sono hi wa…*\
> That day is a bit… [difficult].
>
> 今日はちょっと体調が悪くて…\
> *Kyō wa chotto taichō ga warukute…*\
> I'm feeling a bit under the weather today…
>
> 次回ぜひ！\
> *Jikai zehi!*\
> Next time for sure!

Even among friends, a flat "no" is rare. Give a vague reason, let the sentence trail off and the listener will understand.

## Casual Japanese in Context

### With Close Friends

Two friends plan their afternoon:

> ねえ、今日何する？\
> *Nē, kyō nani suru?*\
> A: Hey, what should we do today?
>
> うーん、別に何も。映画でも見る？\
> *Ūn, betsu ni nani mo. Eiga demo miru?*\
> B: Hmm, nothing in particular. Watch a movie or something?
>
> いいじゃん！何がいい？\
> *Ii jan! Nani ga ii?*\
> A: Nice! What do you want to see?
>
> ホラーとか見たくない？\
> *Horā toka mitakunai?*\
> B: Don't you want to watch something like horror?
>
> えー、怖いの苦手だよ。\
> *Ē, kowai no nigate da yo.*\
> A: Ugh, I'm no good with scary stuff.
>
> じゃ、アクションにしよ。\
> *Ja, akushon ni shiyo.*\
> B: Then let's go with action.

Notice しよ (*shiyo*), a clipped form of しよう (*shiyō*, let's do), and the dropped particles throughout.

### With Younger Siblings and Children

Casual speech to children is often gently instructive:

> ご飯食べたの？\
> *Gohan tabeta no?*\
> Did you eat?
>
> もうお風呂入りなよ。\
> *Mō ofuro hairi na yo.*\
> Go take your bath now.

### In Chat Apps

Messages between friends on LINE, Japan's dominant messaging app, push abbreviation to the limit:

> おはよ〜！今日学校？\
> *Ohayo! Kyō gakkō?*\
> Morning! School today?
>
> 無理、眠すぎ\
> *Muri, nemusugi*\
> Can't. Way too sleepy.

## Watch Out For

### Casual Can Flip to Rude

The same words can be friendly or hostile depending on tone and relationship:

> 何してんの？\
> *Nani shiten no?*\
> What are you doing? (Affectionate from a friend; accusatory from a stranger.)

### Not Too Casual Too Soon

Relationships in Japan usually have a warming-up period before casual speech feels right. Speaking casually to someone you have just met can come across as arrogant, especially with older people.

> **Tip:** Follow the other person's lead. When they drop です and ます, or say タメ語でいいよ (*tamego de ii yo*, "you can talk casually"), you can follow. Don't initiate the shift yourself unless you are clearly peers.

### Fillers Are Your Friends

あの… (*ano…*) and えっと… (*etto…*) are ordinary filler words, the Japanese "um" and "let me see". Using them makes you sound more natural, not less confident.

## Casual Speech Across Generations

Casual Japanese is not one thing. Teenagers, people in their 20s and 30s, the middle-aged and the elderly each have their own casual vocabulary, and slang dates quickly.

| Age group | Typical casual features (as of 2026) |
|---|---|
| Teens | Heavy, fast-changing slang; やばい *yabai* and エモい *emoi* (moving, nostalgic) are long-lived examples |
| 20s and 30s | Moderate slang, chat-app and internet vocabulary |
| 40s and 50s | Older casual forms, little recent slang |
| 60s and over | More regional dialect, older vocabulary and sentence endings |

Slang ages faster than any other part of the language; [section 5.1](../05-living-language/01-internet-and-slang.md) covers it in depth.

## Summary

- Casual Japanese is built from polite speech by dropping です and ます, dropping obvious particles and contracting verbs (〜ている → 〜てる, 〜てしまう → 〜ちゃう).
- Sentence-final particles such as ね, よ, かな and じゃん carry the speaker's attitude and do much of the emotional work in casual speech.
- Casual questions usually drop か and rely on rising intonation; a negative question like 行かない？ is the standard invitation.
- Unfinished sentences such as ちょっと… are a deliberate social tool that lets people refuse or disagree softly.
- Wait for the other person to signal a shift to casual speech, especially with anyone older or more senior than you.
